# Character State

Where each active character stands, what they want, and what they are carrying. Updated after every batch. This is state, not biography; fixed background lives in `bible/characters.md`.

Positions below are as of the end of **Chapter 20, batch Day 27** — the Sunday after the review.

## Volume 01 — The Unfiled Ward

### Owen Rusk, 32 — Copy Clerk, **assisting clerk to the second desk's supervised tray** (a posting, not a qualification), and still posted to the tray of unfiled correspondence

Wants: a person at the far end of the ward's appeal route, and the ability to stop being the only person carrying the case. Holds: an assisting clerk's card that forbids him to sign a continuation, a certification, a suspension or a hold; a private notebook that leaves the building and that he has now named *a register*; a counterfoil proving he is a line in a request register; a cut on his right middle finger that has not healed. Stage 0, and the batch says so by never saying otherwise. **He has had no feeling about his own instrument working, and has understood that the condition he is now in is the ordinary condition of every other clerk in the building.** He has learned, in order: that a filing is a set of checks and not a signature; that the effect field is a menu and a careless clerk is the most powerful person in the room; that a document can be *received* without being *examined* and that the difference is free; that an answer which grants nothing is appealable and an answer which grants a date nobody can compute is not; that a copy of a county intake register in a private book turns the book into a thing a man may be searched for; that his mother's handling of a registry was an error of receiving, not an error of lying; and that careful and right are not the same and never have been. He has begun to do without Nell without noticing he is doing it. He has **not** been paid for the ward's hours. He is a man in the institution, and has stopped pretending that is a temporary condition.

### Nell Arbour, 35 — Claimant Advocate, Arbour Kitchen

**Not on stage in Batch 0002.** Owen has not gone to her; he went up the hill instead. He has said, in his own notebook, that the advocate gave him four names and that two of them are women she has had words with. Pell has refused to hand him one until he has watched somebody use the instrument, and told him to go to the ward instead — which is advice Pell gave him about Nell's condition without naming it. She has still refused to be his witness, his advocate for the ward, or his relief, and those refusals stand. She does not know the ledger, the continuity statement, the intake number, or Alder Row. The Chapter 4 charge is open and unresampled.

### Ada Rusk, 24 — Bridge Mechanic and crew leader, Lower Lock

Wants: her ward to hold an instrument she did not have to be rescued into, and to be asked what she wants rather than what she is owed. She is **one of the four who voted no** and is **not one of the eight branches**; she is inside the return's scope through the crew's acts and has stated out loud, without apology, that she voted against the transfer and is inside a return that performs the duty. In this batch she: told the whole ward about the order at eight in the morning before Owen could, and said in front of thirty people how he came to be telling her; **dictated the four-clause amendment**, the first document in the case she has written entirely herself; **filled in the box Owen could not fill** — the class of persons who may object, narrowed to the eight branches and any person the office names in writing, which excludes her and the other three for ever, at the price of their having said no in a room with witnesses; said nothing at all while Nessa Kray withdrew, which was the hardest thing in the room; and told Owen that a reviewing officer had handed the ward something it asked for and told the truth about where he got it, which she called the second true thing he had said to her. She calls him *copyist*. She has a crew of nine, a repair at four thousand one hundred and sixty that nobody is funding, and a Thursday and a Friday marker at seven forty in the morning.

### Junia Pell, 22 — Junior Copy Clerk, second desk, supervising the assisted tray, and the return's second observer

Wants: to be competent without becoming a custodian of other people's lives, and she has now done six public things that are not that. She sustained a challenge to **her own independence** against herself, in her own hand, on Day 22, before Owen and before Voss, and stayed on the instrument, and asked the reviewing officer to hold her separability open — converting the ward's one weakness into the thing that makes it renewable for review rather than binding. Her separation of interest is on the file twice, in two hands: four sentences in a margin on Day 7, and a page against herself on Day 22 that is longer than the first. She wrote down, at Owen's request, that a stability order can name a district that entered a return later in the quarter, and told him plainly that writing it made her a participant, which is what he had done to her on Day 7. She read the district works inspection roll in public at Owen's request and gave him four lines and no commentary. She refused to name a person for the far end of the appeal route. She told him to his face that she knows what is in his notebook, that she has never told anybody, and that there is no reason in the world for her to be standing near him except that she knows. **She is the only living witness to the destruction of the continuity statement and has not called it a debt and has not used it.** She owes him nothing and has said so kindly.

### Halden Pike, 47 — Deputy Registrar, Docketford

Wants: Docketford solvent and a decision actionable by Friday. In this batch he built **Stability Order 114** in a corridor, in about a minute, without concealment: a stability order closes returns on *lateness* rather than *failure*, and the argument that carries it is that the district cannot fund sixteen of them. He told Owen the schedule was struck on the morning of the twelfth and that no document will move a line on it, and he meant it. In the corridor on the twenty-third he would say only that he would sign the order **that week**; the Thursday-at-ten-past-four rule and the Friday Council counter are Owen's own discovery the next morning, and **Pike must not be given the day or the hour in advance again.** He signed at ten past four on the twenty-fourth, and the instrument is filed for the Council's counter and is **public and appealable to the Glass Stair**. He told Owen in advance that the ward must be visible when it gets signed. He has not been written as a liar and must not be. He has not yet surfaced the real reason for the first Lower Lock withdrawal, which is a Chapters 31–40 debt.

### Marguerite Bray, 61 — Ward Steward of Lower Lock

Keeps the ward's roll and, in its back half, a **delivery book with a second column for paper that comes back**, which she has **ruled by her own hand for thirty-six years, since she was twenty-five**; the book itself has been in the Bray family for fifty-one years, and she is sixty-one. That book is the only reason Chapter 20 could happen. She read the answer, came to the Glass Stair in the rain without asking, asked for the receipt clause to be read into plain words, and refused to thank anybody. She told the ward about the transfer's real origin out of her own book and her own pencil, and her reading is the one that matters: *the office did not answer him and did not refuse him, it received a document.* She asked Owen what the refusals were worth and wrote nothing down when he answered. She is not his ally, has never thanked him, and now holds the answer to a case he had to give away. She wants the ward to exist without the Office and does not believe he is the person who will get it there.

### Wend Voss, 51 — Counter clerk, Mill Ward annex

The man whose counter received the transfer. Second observer, and now the holder of **three** written reservations about his own interest, the last of which he wrote on Day 22 as the price of standing on a document a Registry officer had just been challenged on. He walked up the hill twice in this batch on his own time, came to the review in the dark, and did not announce any of it. He said: *The Office's interest is not separable. I have never pretended it was. The only reason any of it is tolerable is that it is written down in my own hand where a reviewer can find it.* He is not a convert and not a coward. He wants a district that performs what his district cannot afford to keep, and he wants it named.

### Cobb Rennie, 58 — Lower Lock

Nineteen years at the same two rooms and off the Registry roll for two of them. Outside the thirteen households and outside the thirteen erased entries; nothing in this case reaches him and nothing is going to. Still cannot draw a pension. **He now walks a twelve-year-old home every night in the dark and does not know why, and Ivy Sarr has independently concluded that he is the most independent person available because nobody can say he wants anything.** That is not a cure for his pension and must never be written as one. He appeared as a *name on a page* in Chapter 15 and got no scene, and that is correct.

### Dory Anstruther, 19 — Bridge-crew apprentice, Lower Lock

First of the seven adults on the no-local-record schedule, and now a man with a broken collarbone and a chest that has gone the colour of candle wax, holding a coil on the top path and not going in the water, and being extremely good at it. He is a branch of the return. Two descriptions of him stand side by side in the file and the chapter is the point. He has not been asked to sign anything and is not going to be asked by Owen.

### Ivy Sarr, 12 — Lower Lock, lives with her aunt Wenna Sarr

First line of the no-local-record schedule, one of the six who cannot answer, and now the only person in the volume whose face Owen knows. She cannot read the notices and has been carrying her aunt's to the mill's factor on Tuesdays for a fortnight. She counts seed trays, keeps seven against an official nine, and is right. She named five care acts performed this week in her own words and concluded, unprompted, that the man no record places anywhere is the best one to ask. Owen met her once, on a step in a lane, and got four minutes of the best kind he has had in a month. She needs a protective return; the ward has three of five parts and two are nobody's to pay for. Unresolved at the end of the batch and unresolved at the end of the volume.

### Wenna Sarr — Lower Lock, Ivy's aunt, one of the four dissenters

Does the actual arithmetic of her niece's protection herself and gets it exactly right, including the custodian the office may replace without asking the ward. Cannot read the notices the Office hands her and has been sending the child to a counter instead. Said *I am not a witness. I am the only grown person that child has got* in Chapter 7 and has now spent a fortnight being that and doing it well. She asked Owen one question and made him answer it with the first thing in the case that was not a complaint.

### Nessa Kray, 71 — Lower Lock, seed stall

Forty-one years on the same pitch, in neither leaf of the annex, and the second person this month to tell Owen what a paper was actually about. She went to the Mill Ward annex and was told there was no provision for a person to take herself out of a return; **she withdrew her branch on Day 23, standing up, unasked, in her own words, and the return narrowed by one page and stood.** She is not leaving the ward and the ward knows it. Two people have already told Owen she walked. She is in Marguerite Bray's drawer with nothing else in it and that is where she stays.

### Ilse Mern, 44 — Lower Lock, laundress, a branch

Wants the roof she voted for. Demanded her own page in Chapter 7 and got it. Watched the nine adults carry the ward's burden out of the hall on the fourth morning and did not go with them, and said the reason out loud, and was not thanked, and did not ask to be. Gave up the granary double count on Day 25, stayed in the return, and said in the hall that she was not going to be the second thing in a month that everybody had to be told about. Takes Ivy's washing in with hers. Holds the second lamp at a night channel clearance. Four households on her standpipe rota and Cobb Rennie on it.

### Marta Rusk — deceased

Fifteen years dead of fever after a medicine shortage in a flood-year closure. The Registry's delay in revising her residence status did not cause the fever; it delayed her priority medicine and left her **burial disputed for eight months**. Her record is **not** amended in this batch and is not amended until Chapters 46–50. The correct record says she is dead and says the Office delayed. **Batch 0003 is the beat that traces her delay into the same redacted batch as the ward's**, and nothing before that may move it.

### Marguerite Bray's delivery book, as an object

Not a person, and it has no voice of its own, and it is the only thing in Docketford that can prove what a ward was asked for. It has an inward column and a returns column, it has been in Marguerite Bray's family for **fifty-one years**, and **she has ruled and kept it for thirty-six of them**, since she was twenty-five. It recorded the day the ward's copy of the transfer came back, the day the ward ratified nine to four, the day of Kray's withdrawal, the date of the amendment, and — behind a flyleaf, on the backs of three grey notices — the words *ward public works — schedule wanted*. It is the ward's own book, and the Office has never explained those three refusals and never will.

### Verity Lound — offstage

Her first circular is on the page (Chapter 10) and closed the local-return route. Her second is owed in Chapters 33–35. She is not named in person anywhere in Batch 0002 and must not be.
