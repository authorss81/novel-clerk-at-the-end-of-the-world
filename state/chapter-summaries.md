# Chapter Summaries

## Volume 01 — The Unfiled Ward

### Batch 0001 — Chapters 1–10, *The Omission* (written, C.Y. 487, Days 1–14)

**Chapter 1 — The Fourth River Desk (Day 1).** Owen's weekly continuance batch is one sheet short: Lower Lock's return has been replaced by a transfer entry moving the crossing, the Fetter Lane pump and the granary to a Mill Ward return, carrying two Registry witnesses and none from the ward. He reads the dated annex schedule beside the annex counter's receiving stamp and gets the batch's one margin notice: the stated effective date is earlier than the act performed, two days before the receipt. He refuses the countersignature, writes a memorandum instead, and loses his ten-week clean run. In the request register for that fortnight he finds three refused requests inside the two-day window, initialled by an office he cannot name, and copies all three into a private pocket notebook. He goes home to Lower Lock.

**Chapter 2 — What the Ward Knows (Day 1 dusk to Day 2 dawn).** The ward has been told softly: a letter inviting residents to consolidate claims at the Mill Ward annex, mentioning a roof and a water grant and not a crossing. Owen tries to run the room and is told to sit down in front of his own neighbours. He learns the weir's real condition, the ward's economy, and Cobb Rennie, who has been off the Registry roll for two years while living at the same address and cannot draw a pension because nothing in this quarter's business will ever reach him. Marguerite Bray lets him copy her hand-kept roll all night, with two witnesses per entry where the ward can supply two. At dawn he reads the second leaf: thirteen named people with *no local record at the named place*, and the named place is the crossing.

**Chapter 3 — Two Witnesses (Day 3).** Pike gives Owen exactly what the rules require: two Registry officers, the countersignature review standard, no Docketford number, no issuing office. His real resistance is arithmetic — sixteen wards with failing returns this quarter and a stability order that must be actionable by Friday — and the sentence that carries the volume: every district he stabilises is a district that does not drown somewhere else. He offers Owen a lateral posting at better pay; Owen refuses, the first time he has chosen to be poorer. Pell teaches him the proper name of the inspection roll and asks *who consented*. He files a **continuance inquiry** in his own name as an **error** case against the second leaf, and copies Pike's sixteen names into his notebook with Lower Lock on it.

**Chapter 4 — Arbour Kitchen (Day 4).** Nell has been taking Lower Lock families for eleven days because they came to her. She puts both leaves of the annex on the table and asks him which one he has been arguing about, then tells him, in front of a full room, that existing was never the question: the Office's claim is about a place, and a ward cannot answer a claim about a place by saying *we live here*. She refuses to be his witness. He offers no apology because he knows it is the cheapest thing he owns. When he finally asks the right question — what would the ward need in order to file at all — she gives him the terms of a district return and the sentence the whole volume runs on: nobody else will perform it.

**Chapter 5 — Nobody Else Will Perform It (Day 6, one night).** The ward can supply acts but not witnesses. Marguerite produces her delivery book: the transfer was never delivered as a proposal, it was entered as an acceptance, received eleven months ago on a receipt signed and witnessed by **Tal Sarr** for his own household, while alive, valid when written, undisturbed by his drowning in the March flood. Ada's reading is the midpoint: *somebody didn't lie, and that's worse.* Owen withdraws his own provable two-day error in writing, says why out loud, and gives up the three refused requests with it; then he amends his inquiry to allege **scope**. He proposes the only thing that can work — a district return — and the ward understands at once that a ward which files can lose.

**Chapter 6 — Notice (Days 7–8).** Instructed by the Mill Ward annex, and the instruction reasonable, Owen fills a ward notice's scope box with a pointer to a document ten of thirteen households have never read, initials the preparing clerk's line, and lets the district office tick the accuracy clause without him. Posting is the act; the amendment is refused at both the hall and the counter. Ada reads it twice on the hall table and asks which person it treats as disposable; Owen cannot answer. He tries to amend, is refused, and is removed from the meeting by the hall warden when he tries to state the omission. The transfer is ratified **nine to four**, witnessed and sealed. Ada, Bram Tillet, Holm Ferris and Wenna Sarr are the four.

**Chapter 7 — Performing It (Days 9–10).** Every household member who could witness the ward's duty is a party to the return, and each refusal is right. Pell signs as an outside witness, separates her own interest in the margin in words that indict her own office, and touches no drafting; Voss signs what he personally saw, having walked down from the annex on his own time, and records that the ward's performance is a cost to his district. Three of the nine demand their own pages and get them. Ada fights Owen for two hours over the word *crossing* and wins: the return claims a **duty** and expressly claims nothing as to safety, condition or use. Then the ward's own copy of the transfer — closed at the Day 5 review — returns **nothing**, and Owen learns that a closed file is not a thin file.

**Chapter 8 — Load Restriction (Day 10 night into Day 11).** Pike issues a load-restriction suspension on a correct finding: the abutment crack has moved five inches since the spring, and no officer of the Office will certify the crossing open. Owen goes out at dusk to serve it and keeps the witness count honest while Dory Anstruther goes into the spillway and the river comes over the coping. The boy comes out alive. At two in the morning Owen writes an engineer's continuity statement that would convert *not certified open* into *supported as maintained*, copies the false wording into his own ledger first, and tears the sheet up himself in front of Pell, saying out loud that he wrote it. The ward takes the honest version: a preservation claim, a repair duty that is not theirs, a renewal interval that starts at entry, and a closing invitation to any district that can do the job better. Owen copies and enters the suspension and initials nothing, and is quietly proud of his copy.

**Chapter 9 — Petition (Day 12).** Owen writes a short-form attestation that is accurate in every particular, asks Pell to copy it, and is refused in a corridor: he has certified a witness line with nobody in the room. He loses the window. Marguerite Bray, who has been in the corridor for three hours, files her own petition in her own words and her own name, and the clerk on duty cannot refuse a request for a hearing without writing a reason. Entered, not granted. The clerk then tells her, kindly and in advance, that a petition holds nothing, that the Monday roll goes on regardless, and that the Office's answer is due in seven days. Owen files a **statement of his own part** in the notice: his drafting, his pointer, the accuracy clause he let somebody else tick, and his removal from the meeting. Pell dates the entry and nothing else. A sealed Council circular arrives at the end of the day.

**Chapter 10 — Unfiled Correspondence (Days 13–14).** The Day 14 roll enters the crossing's status as **lapsed — no supported district**, and Owen is the only man in the hall who notices that the ward's own witnessed ratification sits two lines below it and does not stop the roll. Four consequences land in order: the pump grant suspended, every household frozen with no name struck, the load restriction still in force on top, and Ada's crew maintaining a crossing no record says they maintain. The crew hand-primed the failed pump for twelve hours; Ada refuses Owen's money; the granary is four weeks from empty. Pike posts Owen to the tray of unfiled correspondence — a punishment that is also the one posting nobody watches. The Council's circular names the Council as the issuer of the countersignature standard and closes community returns after the close of the quarter; a clerk on the seal stair tells him Form Zero is the only instrument that could supersede a stability order, has no valid signature path, and is due in thirteen years. Pell hands him the six names that need protective returns and none of the three parts to build them. He copies the roll entry into his ledger, leaves the finished return on Pell's tray with a covering slip, hears from Nell that he will have to ask people to be responsible in public and cannot do it for them, and finds a brass tab in the locked drawer he is now trusted with: **ALDER ROW — 461**. He does not open it. At the tray a name he has copied for six years will not come, and comes back a minute later out of his own notebook.

### Batch 0002 — Chapters 11–20, *The Ward's Own Return* (written, C.Y. 487, Days 15–27)

**Chapter 11 — Entry (Day 15, Tuesday).** An entry is a set of checks in an order. The third one bars a clerk named in the case or living in the district, and both bars are Owen, so the seal stair appoints him an **assisting clerk** on the second desk's supervised tray — a posting, not a qualification, on a card that forbids him to sign a continuation, a certification, a suspension or a hold. Pell had gone and got the appointment at seven that morning and did not mention it. **On her advice, and because he has learned to ask before he decides, Owen narrows the composition** himself from *household branches* to *the nine persons whose acts are recorded* — Pell stops him at the wording and makes him find the clause; she does not narrow it for him. She then teaches him the effect field, which is a printed list of five clauses and the most powerful thing in the building. He reads all five aloud and enters two: the ward's restored standing for district acts, and its performance received and reviewable. He refuses clause ii, which is a status claim about the crossing; clause iv, which would hand the repair to any district claiming jurisdiction; and clause v, which is the sentence Ada beat him with. Pell's warning: he has made the instrument unimpeachable, not strong. **487-LL** is sealed at ten past four; the freeze lifts and the crossing returns as a duty and nothing else; the pump grant stays suspended and the load restriction stands; the review is due on the twenty-second. He cuts his hand on the press's catch tray. He feels nothing, and finds that worse.

**Chapter 12 — The Answer Is a Date (Days 16–17, Wednesday–Thursday).** Every route into the district office is a route into the same room, and the room is the Office, and the Deputy Registrar is the man who will be asked about it in public. Pell explains that a district return is reviewed by the district office and not by the Council, and that the two kinds of receipt — received, and received and examined — are the difference between being unable to hurry the file and being unable to refuse it. She also tells him, for his own good and with no use for it yet, that the petition's answer is being drafted from a template and a docket clerk may refuse a template as inadequate. That night the crew does the channel clearance at night because the mill's silt settles by day, and Owen is handed the place he held on the weir path. On the Thursday he asks that the **district works inspection roll** be read in public against the return's four acts, and does not attend, and Pell goes and reads it, and gives him four lines: the district engineer has recorded four entries in three weeks describing the ward's acts, two of them marked *not district work* and two *no district crew present; two private persons*, and the night of the tenth of the month is in there. He watches a household return from Havelock Reach be held for a request to name a person rather than a place. Kray tells him at her stall that a counter clerk has told her there is no provision for a person to take herself out of a return.

**Chapter 13 — Saturday (Days 18–19, Friday–Saturday).** Owen drafts three good attacks on an answer he has not read and does not make them: *an answer that gave nothing would be appealable, and this one cannot be appealed, because nobody can say it is wrong.* He files the one objection that is free — that the answer is on a template — and the docket clerk sustains it, and the answer that comes down on Saturday is four paragraphs instead of one, in the district office's own words, and is worse. It accepts the return **for review**, not binding, and expressly does not determine the observers' separability. It records that the load-restriction suspension is not affected. And it grants a **date in the last week of the rain season**, and no instrument in Docketford fixes when a season ends, so the date can be neither missed nor wrong nor appealed. Pell's gloss on that is not hers: a safe, a load restriction, a waiver, a grant and a repair are five separate things and none of them came back. Owen copies all two hundred and twenty-four words into his notebook and writes nothing beside them, then reads the answer out in a full ward hall, going back to the fourth paragraph seven times, and Ada asks who wrote it and he tells her the truth: he knows the form and not the man, and he is not going looking.

**Chapter 14 — The Index (Day 20, Sunday).** The tray has no schedule, so the tray man is in an empty building with the Front Desk index. The transfer's originating entry is filed under a **county intake number** at the Fetter Bend sub-table, not a district number, and the day-book gives two entries and a hole: a receipt received for intake and forwarded on the eleventh with a schedule and counterfoil, and, read back ten days, a no-local-record schedule made out at the sub-table on the ninth with a form presented and not received. **There is nothing at all between them**, and Owen looks at it, catches himself inventing a reason for it, and writes the word *nothing* in his own book. The presenter column on the forwarded item says *household 14-B, Fetter Lane. Unattested. No district steward. Not a district act.* He puts the day-book beside the index card and gets a second margin notice in the volume: the card says *two leaves attached*, the day-book says *schedule and counterfoil*, and the originating entry's review interval was **renewed twice and not a third time** — a term, not a date. The next number in the intake series is **ALDER ROW — 461**, a district return, no forwarding office, no Registry witness, *never opened* — a return for a ward officially closed in Concord Year 461, filed four weeks after the transfer in the same unbroken column. He asks for the volume to be fetched and then insists it be logged, and is written into a request register for the first time in his life, and thinks about the three refusals he surrendered on purpose. He does not copy the intake volume, and the reason he gives himself is about power and not about honesty: a copy of a county intake register in a private notebook makes the notebook a thing a man may be searched for. Wenna Sarr stops him outside her stall and asks him one question about Ivy, and it is the first question anybody has asked him about her in a month.

**Chapter 15 — What Ivy Needs (Day 21, Monday).** Wenna Sarr has done the arithmetic herself and it is correct: two unrelated witnesses not living together, a care act this week, a route that is not her, a seven-day review, and a custodian who is not her family and whom the office may replace without asking the ward. The only candidate in Lower Lock is a sixty-one-year-old woman with a hand-kept roll, and a sixty-one-year-old woman with a brass weight is not a custodian for a child who will outlive her. Owen says out loud, for the first time in the case, something that is not a complaint: what a protective return is, from the side of the person it exists for — it records the care, renews it weekly, gives her a road to complain along, and expressly reserves her name, hands, vote, wages, marriage and private life; it expires; it is the only instrument in the city built so that it cannot own her. Three of the five parts are the ward's and two are Registry functions nobody is paid to provide. At the seal stair he reads the requirements sheet for the sealed instrument and it is silent, and the silence has no date, no stamp and nobody to be angry at, and it frightens him in a way he cannot put in a notebook usefully. Ivy Sarr gives him four names and says, flatly, that Cobb Rennie is the best one to ask because nobody can say he wants anything. He writes the four names and does not write the word *custodian* beside any of them.

**Chapter 16 — Separability (Day 22, Tuesday).** A challenge comes down the correct way, from the countersignature desk, in four correct paragraphs: that the second observer is an officer of the Office before which the return is filed, that the Office received the transfer, that a separation of interest by an officer of an interested office is a statement by that office and cannot cure it, and that the return should stand or fall on Voss alone. Owen's instinct is to find the filer and Pell stops him for a reason that is not moral: a named filer can be answered, and an answer by guessing becomes a fight about character. Then the harder thing: the challenge is good, she would sustain it as a reviewer, and she will sustain it herself at ten to four unless he produces something better by four. Owen finds that the form says *separable interests* and nothing about self-separation, and goes to Voss as a favour, telling him exactly what it costs including that a second challenge will fall on him. Voss stands and writes a third reservation about himself. Pell sustains the challenge against herself, on the day, in her own hand, and does it first, and asks the reviewing officer to hold her separability open. The review is adjourned to the twenty-fifth. Owen tells her he has been keeping a notebook the way this Office keeps registers, and she says *yes, you have*.

**Chapter 17 — Sixteen (Day 23, Wednesday).** Pike builds the instrument in a corridor, and its whole argument is two distinctions: a stability order closes returns on *lateness* rather than *failure*, and the district cannot fund sixteen of them. He will say only that he will sign that week; the Thursday-at-ten-past-four rule is Owen's discovery the next morning. Lower Lock was sixth on the schedule struck on the morning of the twelfth, three days before Owen entered anything, and no document will move a line. Owen has all sixteen names in a private book and says out loud, to a man looking at the floor, that he is not going to put the book on a table, because the moment he does he becomes a name somebody has written down against a schedule. Pell finds and writes down that an order can name a district that entered a return later in the quarter because the schedule is struck on the twelfth, and tells him that writing it makes her a participant, which is what he did to her on the seventh day. Pell refuses to name a person for the far end of the ward's appeal route and sends him to the ward instead. In the hall that evening **Nessa Kray withdraws her branch**, standing up, unasked, in her own words: *I thought a branch was a name. It is not a name. It is a thing you do, and I have not done that thing.* Ada, a dissenter and not a branch, says nothing at all. The room calls it a walk-off. The return narrows by one page and stands.

**Chapter 18 — The Signature (Day 24, Thursday).** Owen works out that an instrument for Friday's Council counter is signed on Thursday at four, gets the hour from the seal-stair clerk, and goes upriver — two hours late, because he has learned about the order from a clerk and Ada has told the ward herself, at eight in the morning, in front of thirty people, including the part about how he came to be telling her. **Stability Order 114** suspends sixteen named returns together; it does not close Lower Lock, it puts it in a column, and the difference is that a closure is a decision about you and a column is a queue. Ada dictates a four-clause amendment — the order is a fact arising after entry and not caused by the ward, it names Stability Order 114, it alters no act, scope, term, observer or duty and erases nothing, and the Office is asked not to read the order as a finding that the crossing is unsafe — and it reaches the countersignature desk at four minutes to four, carried down by Pell, so that Owen never sees the signature. The ward's version arrives by a boy from a bar first and is wrong: closure, and the crossing gone with it. The countersignature desk's acknowledgement slip says *suspended* sixteen times and *public, appeal lies to the Glass Stair*. Owen reads sixteen names aloud and feels nothing about fifteen of them, and says so. He is relieved the order is public and ashamed of the relief, and tells Ada so, and she says it is the first true thing he has said to her in a month and she already knew it.

**Chapter 19 — The Review (Day 25, Friday).** Ada solves the box Owen has been three days unable to fill: the class of persons who may object is *the eight persons whose branches are in this return, and any person the district office names in writing before the review is concluded* — which excludes her and the other three for ever, and she says that is what it costs to have said no in a room with witnesses. The first seven-day review, three days late because of Owen, is conducted in a room the ward has arranged and not the Office. The reviewing officer asks no question about ability, because an officer who asks it puts it on the file, and where it becomes a reason. He answers Ada's third clause in writing and does not pretend to have arrived at it independently: a stability order suspends a *status*, a duty is not a status, a suspended district can still perform. The return is **renewed for review only**, on terms, with the two observers re-separated in writing at each renewal and a separation made once not carrying to a second. He makes a margin note putting the return and the order in the same drawer on purpose. The ward hall hears four new acts, Ilse Mern gives up the granary count and stays in the return, and Ada tells Owen that a reviewing officer handed the ward something it asked for and told the truth about where he got it. Owen finds he has no feeling about the instrument working except that it is now larger than his opinion of it.

**Chapter 20 — Who Sent It (Days 26–27, Saturday–Sunday).** Standing in an empty copying hall on the Saturday, Owen works out that no one dates a document two days early to hide it; people date it early when they have been refused and the date is the only evidence they have. On Sunday at eight with a lantern he asks Marguerite Bray for the delivery book's second column, and behind a flyleaf there are three grey notices returned to a house in Fetter Lane on the sixteenth, seventeenth and eighteenth, and on the back of each, in her own pencil, is *ward public works — schedule wanted*, and on the third, underlined twice, *can one house consent for a ward*. He reconstructs the order: refused on the ninth, twice more on the tenth, and then a sealed sheet on the eleventh, received for intake at the sub-table, forwarded, stamped at the Mill Ward annex, with a schedule bearing the ninth because the ninth is the day he asked — and then he has to admit that he had the day-book open at the ten-day gap on the Sunday before, had looked straight at the nothing in it, and had gone upriver without asking what a person does on a day when nothing happens to them at a counter they have already been to. Bray's reading: the Office did not answer him and did not refuse him, it *received a document*, and a document is what an office is built to receive, and that is how a refusal becomes a consent with nobody in the room deciding anything. He does not reinstate the withdrawn charge, because a charge withdrawn by the man who withdrew it cannot be re-entered by him, and because the charge that matters is scope, and because the ward's own hand-kept book — which Marguerite Bray has ruled for thirty-six years, in a book that has been in her family for fifty-one — is the right place for what the ward asked and not the place for what he wants. He arrives at the end of the month: the Office is careful, and careful is not the same as right, and he is a man in it.

### Batch 0003 — Chapters 21–30, *The Amended Name* (written, C.Y. 487, Days 28–39)

**Chapter 21 — The Same Batch (Day 28, Monday).** A clerk's summary of the ward's week goes on the tray with the renewal on it, and Owen learns what a redaction batch is: a set of entries struck in one sitting by one officer, kept in the *Redaction Register* under a ground, with a schedule lodged. Marta Rusk's residence status sits in **Redaction Batch 471/Q4/19, struck on the twelfth of the fourth quarter of C.Y. 471, forty-one entries, ground "revision of residence status, flood quarter arrears," struck by H.L.P.** A batch may be produced to a person named in one of its entries, alone, once, and Owen is the only living person named in his mother's — he presented the claim at sixteen, and the entry's presenter column says *claimant informed* and the claimant was a boy at a counter. He is named in **entry nine**, which is the first row of the batch because the rows are in the order the officer went down the queue and not in the order of the numbers. He takes away the batch number, the initials and the date, copies nothing, writes nothing next to the entry, and tells nobody. A **residence status** is the entry that places a person at an address for priority medicine, priority ration and burial: she was not at her address for eleven days, and eleven days is not the fever, and her burial was disputed for eight months afterwards and is not eleven days.

**Chapter 22 — Forty-One (Day 29, Tuesday).** The schedule of a redacted batch is a single sheet of forty-one entry numbers, numbered down the side by line, and it is the only part the Office gives away for nothing. The same interval is on every row, so the eleven days is the *sitting* and not her row. Three of the forty-one numbers are numbers Owen handled inside nine years off Fourth River Desk **route lists** — two of them quarterly roll notices going to a public counter, and one a **form 12-B** carried against the same entry number to a sub-table counter in the week of the eleventh of C.Y. 471, which is *not* one of the forty-one and is *not* about a residence status, and he has to stop himself from letting the second thing get at the first. He understands he was a mechanism: he has built a month of case on somebody else's deliberate act, and under the case is a layer of his own handwriting. He refuses to make a list of forty-one names. Pell gives him the whole procedure: a person who may be affected may ask, forty people must each ask, and the Office built that, honestly. Her answer to *has anybody ever asked forty people at once* is no, and then: **nobody has ever needed to until now.**

**Chapter 23 — What the Registry Did Once (Day 30, Wednesday).** Owen goes to the district office record room with a point of law and is given the adverse precedent **12/A** first — a return is a statement, not a claim, and it is true, and it is the second half of the one he wants. **37/C** comes up on the counter after the lunch hour and he reads it the same afternoon, at ten past two, and re-reads it that evening and once more on the Friday morning looking for a *must* and not finding one. It is two hundred and nine words and it records the Registry recognising a community return, entering a **maintenance grant** of a hundred and forty pounds a year against a named public work, in writing, in the district's own quarter, on the day the district asked — because the district performed the duty in public with a roll, two witnesses, and a **published schedule of the office's refusals**. The reversal: the precedent does not compel the Registry to honour a return. It compels it, before refusing one, to have published a schedule of what it has refused. The fight is not to be forgiven. It is to be **listed**. He does not tell the ward; he leaves the schedule on Bray's table at half past eight that night, she leaves it out for a man from the mill, and the ward has read it by Thursday morning.

**Chapter 24 — What Ada Does With It (Day 31, Thursday).** Ada got there first: a man from the mill read the second column aloud in the granary on the Wednesday night and by Thursday morning twenty-two people are in the hall and it is not a granary meeting. Ada works out that the ward satisfies three of the four grounds and cannot satisfy the fourth, which is the Office's, and then says the frightening thing out loud: **performance in public is not a duty, it is a shift**, so it is a roster, and a roster is a document, and a document can be read by somebody who was not there. She reads out **five terms** — a printed roster, two witnesses' names on it, any outsider may read it on any day without notice, any person may complain in the book, and the roster is performed whatever the return does. Marguerite Bray attaches **the condition**: *and the wage* — *wage not funded*. Owen tries to slow it down and is stopped in front of twenty-two people — *a man who keeps a thing alive by holding on to it has no business telling a ward what it may perform* — and does not defend himself. Ada sends him to find somebody from another district.

**Chapter 25 — Be Told No Kindly (Days 31–32, Thursday–Friday).** At Arbour Kitchen on the Thursday afternoon, Owen asks Nell for one of the four names and it is the worst sentence he has said in a month; she refuses, and repeats her condition unchanged: *the route and nothing else*. On the Friday morning **Elias Trant**, a millwright, is told no kindly and gives the reason: a man with nothing to lose is not a check, he is a tourist. **Ivet Sterne**, who keeps a tide table at Wexford Landing and is *not* the Coal Hill tide-table holder, is not an inspector and has no office; her whole power is that she was there and that she can stop coming. She gives him **six conditions** better than anything he could have written, and a no, and says come back at four the same afternoon with three answers. He gets them: the figure; the fact that the *ward* must ask and not the Registry, which he nearly fumbles by claiming a request the ward had not made and only saves by telling her the truth about the minute Marguerite Bray ruled into the delivery book on the Thursday; and the honest answer that a roster of unpaid hours is a list of what people are owed and the ward must say so itself first. She writes four lines on a ferry ticket: **the thirty-eighth of the month, first light to dark, one day**, the book handed over with both hands by a person of the ward, and the ward may name a further one. Nell says the same four sentences and Owen does not argue, which is new and which she notices.

**Chapter 26 — The Schedule of Refusals (Day 33, Saturday).** As a co-petitioner Owen may inspect the documents produced in his own matter, and the district office, answering, has produced its **refusal column** in extract: **one hundred and nine entries** in the quarter, and **forty-one dispositions are the same four words — *No district office competent*** — a correct sentence about the Office and about none of the forty-one people. The precedent compels publication only on a point of law argued by a party, so Owen does not file. He prints a **comparison** on the public table by the Glass Stair door, with a card saying what it is and where the four sheets may be inspected, and no conclusion. Pell calls it a poster, tells him the only person who will notice is whoever maintains the standard forms, says he will make enemies who are not enemies yet, and refuses to be in the room. He says both sentences out loud: he is not proud of it, and he is not sorry for it. He has spent a month learning to file and has never once learned to print.

**Chapter 27 — The Standard Was Issued (Days 34–35, Sunday–Monday).** A standard is issued by somebody and recorded somewhere, and the district office keeps a **Schedule of Instruments in Force**: **item 14, issued by the Provisional Council in the Concord Year 44, in force in all district offices, answering to no local office, amended never.** Its **recitals** — which a Council instrument must be discoverable with — give the ground: flood-quarter returns were made in haste, commonly by a person with an interest in the work and witnessed by the same district, and the office could not tell a duty performed from a status asserted. The reasons are right. The effect is that no community return here can be acted on without a second officer, and the second officer comes off the **roll of countersignature** — nineteen names, eight outside their districts, a district-exclusion roll, appointments continuing, withdrawn only by the officer's own hand. **Pell is fourth on it**, appointed at nineteen for a Havelock Reach return on a Tuesday because she was the only person free. She cannot get off it without leaving the next instrument with one observer. Owen asks her in the open, in the hall, and gets the answer that the rule ruining his month was written by careful people, is four hundred and forty-three years old, and has been right the whole time.

**Chapter 28 — The Name (Days 35–37, Monday–Wednesday).** The name is **VANE, H.** — the receiving officer in the third column of a covering slip, about four hundred times a quarter for nine years, a retrieval and not a memory. It comes on Monday night, comes back on the Tuesday, fails on the Tuesday afternoon, and on the fourth time his hand goes to that column it fails again and there is nothing at all: no noise, no crisis, no cause, and nobody in a hall of sixty who could see it, because the absence looks exactly like a clerk who did not read that line today. On the fourth occasion he finds it in the unrouteable tray, in a returned quarterly works notice addressed to three letters and a surname at **4 Lantern Walk**, returned twice, *not known at the address*. And Owen works out, standing up in a lane, that the ward is poorer because of the water and not because of the transfer, and that the difference between the two is that one has a person in it and one does not.

**Chapter 43 — A Duty for One Household (Days 57–58, Tuesday–Wednesday).** The protective-return requirements sheet is read aloud in a hall by everybody. **Five parts — two unrelated living witnesses, a current care act, a named local appeal route, a named place at which the thing is kept, a named person who keeps it** — and the ward has three; the review and the reservation of consent are printed on the same sheet as conditions, not parts, and the chapter says out loud that a man arrives with four blanks and finds five. The route now has a name at the far end of it and the **two parts the ward does not have are the place and the person**, which is the custodian, and it is a Registry function this city has never paid for. **Ilse Mern is asked in front of the room and says the number out loud**: *five hours a week, not five hours on a Thursday, for as long as the care situation lasts, and the office may put somebody else in my place without asking the ward, and the hours of the person who was there do not stop.* Nobody can pay it and Ada will not pretend otherwise in front of her. Owen rules into the book that **the word is not written beside any of the four people who could be asked**. Hester Lund's answer arrives the same afternoon, four lines, out of the ward's bag from the hall door: she will be in the room on the fifty-ninth, she is not a witness, her name is not on the return, and *do not tell me in advance what you are going to say.* On the Wednesday night **Ada asks Owen to accept a named duty for Ivy Sarr's household** — not an officer, not a witness, but the person who will be on the page and answerable for it: *you would be the one it comes back to.* **He asks what it costs before he says yes**, is told a duty of this kind does not close, and says yes, and requires the reason written down: **nobody else will, and that is not a noble reason and is the true one.** Ada tells him at the door that the narrowing is coming and that he is to be two miles away when she does it.

**Chapter 44 — Ten of the Clock in the Long Room (Day 59, Thursday).** The marker goes in at seven forty and nine people from Lower Lock walk and boat for four hours to be in the room at ten. The chair says the distinction first and says it in thirty seconds: two matters, one room, one day, **not merged**, and nothing said in one half is evidence in the other. **Marguerite Bray reads the ward's file out of her own book**, including the two hundred and twenty-four words of the Day 19 answer and the standpipe rota, and reads the disclaimer three times. **Ada Rusk is there as one of the four who said no and not a branch**, argues the return and the schedule and the withdrawal right, and says **not one word about the abutment**, which is the single most valuable self-restraint in the case. **Hester Lund takes her standing** — *a standing in the room with a document on the table in front of it*, **fifteen words, and the return says fifteen words** — and spends it on the one disagreement she has with the ward, out loud: the ward's schedule does not claim a seed stall, a woman of seventy-one has lost one, she is not a witness to it, and the district is right. The Office answers in a paragraph and produces the refusal column under Precedent 37/C: **one hundred and nine entries, forty-one dispositions the same four words.** Owen spends the sentence he carried up a hill — **and you hid them inside a favourable reading** — in the room, the twenty-two go into the record in front of forty people, and **Pike does not fight about the word *hid***, because the word is not the point. **Stability Order 114 is carried into public appeal** with a date. And Owen sees the coincidence of two forty-ones, wants it to be a practice, and refuses it on the page, because a coincidence of a number is not a cause and there is no column for it — and what he is refusing is his own arithmetic of the thirty-ninth day, which is in his own book and in no file in this city.

**Chapter 45 — The Narrowing (Day 60, Friday).** The reviewing officer puts the correct form first — a hold cannot be carried by an instrument renewed every seven days and not binding — and then the worse half: **a duty the district cannot afford to keep is not a duty**, and the evidence is the ward's own schedule. Ada takes the choice herself in front of twenty-six people. She gives up **the night**: the pump prime at four and the standpipe rota come off the return, and the hours a man of fifty-eight and a girl of twelve were standing in stay on the roster, at the weir path, where anybody may read them. She signs as **six named people with one duty each that one of them can do alone** — Bess Crale the marker, Enid Salter the channel report, Tam Ockey the count, Ruth Mapp the reading, Sully Dace the bins, Wil Pomfret the copy — and fixes the scope clause at **four words: *Duty, and no status***, in her own capitals, and makes him say *the district has told you* and hand over the sheet. **Bram Tillet writes a refusal on his own page** in a cook's hand with the reason and the date, **a day after the day the ward had named as the limit for the four pages**, and Bray rules it and rules *nobody is to be asked*, and rules with it that **the ward has not closed an offer on the morning after its own limit and that there is no instrument in this city that closes a page a ward has ruled.** Wenna Sarr's page stays blank and she says why in one sentence. The return is renewed **for the sixth time, for review, on narrower terms**, and the observers are separated again in their own hands.

**Chapter 46 — The Certification (Days 61–63, Saturday–Monday). The volume's certification, and the only acquisition in it.** Owen asks the standing question at the bottom of the seal stair on the Saturday of Day 61 — *does an officer certifying a district return have a district, and if so is that a bar* — and the answer is neither yes nor no: a certification is made by a **person**, without a seal, with no office behind it, and the instrument has a box that says *the person making this certification is of the district named in it*, and being of the district is a bar on the **second examination** and nothing else. **The long room with the high windows is on the Monday afternoon of Day 63, at about half past three**, and in it he fills that box in his own hand and the third box — what he has done in the last seven days, in plain words, with the flood of Day 54 put down and owned as outside the seven — and Ada stops him once. **Pell and Voss discharge the second examination in writing, in their own hands**, and the reviewing officer says the ward's own sentence about not being examinable by anybody who lives in it is the reason a certification is possible and not the reason it is comfortable. Inside goes the **temporary preservation hold**: the crossing's last supported legal status and its appeal window, seven days, with a stated end, **one thing only** — it does not make the Ninth Weir safe, funded, repaired or usable, it is not a grant and not an opening, and the crossing stays closed to loads — and a **list of five lines** the holder must carry: Cobb Rennie's pension, the six with three parts of five, the four pages, four thousand one hundred and sixty, and the receipt nobody ever gave. He signs **one line, as a person, without a seal** — the card says *a certification* and he signs a certification, and the answer is the box on the second page: *this one has no office in it* — and it is the first line he has been allowed to sign since the card was drawn. He feels **relief first and the understanding second**, and is not allowed to have only the second.

**Chapter 47 — One Active Hold (Days 64–65, Tuesday–Wednesday).** A certification stops a man holding things in general, and the list of five arrives in the ordinary work as exact particulars that do not aggregate: Cobb Rennie's pension as the smell of an oiled coat; the six as the exact weight of a wet roster board; the four pages as a woman's exact pitch of voice; the repair as a number; the receipt as a form standing on a spike since the nineteenth that nobody has taken off. And on the Wednesday afternoon **a second name goes**: **Hiram Cray**, fifty-one, of the water gate, thirty years in the post and ten of them on that gate, who carries bundles up a stair and puts them on a ledge and says nothing, and whose name Owen copied about four times a week for six years out of the third column of a weekly post sheet. **He is a different man from the first one and is not any named character of the volume, and both names are spoken in this chapter and nowhere else in Volume 01.** It is not resolved, not paid for, not confessed and not written down, and nobody is ever told. What the cost is, in the end: not pain and not dread but the ordinary Tuesday texture of a thing that cannot be put down.

**Chapter 48 — What the Office Owes (Days 66–67, Thursday–Friday).** Stability Order 114 is argued in public and Owen says out loud, in a room of thirty, that the same four-hundred-and-forty-three-year-old rule that ruined Lower Lock is the rule that put sixteen names in the room, and that he will not make either fact louder than the other. **Wenna Sarr argues the six protective returns** in public for the first time, by a person who is not an officer and is not a party, and puts the custodian's cost in the transcript. **Cobb Rennie names his own pension** in his own voice and says it is worth less than money and more than nothing. **Nell Arbour is in the room in a capacity of her own**, makes the argument nobody at that table is positioned to make — that the order is a rule which picks a place by a column, and that goes to sixteen places and not one — and says to Owen's face that **the case is right and that is why she is doing it**, and is not available. Owen asks her for nothing, which is new; she notices it; neither says so. **Pike asks to be entered in a register of the contested closures of this district, to be the first name in it, and to be the one who signs the entries.** Owen wins the argument and feels nothing, and works out that what he did in the room was describe a list, and that describing a list is now what he is for.

**Chapter 49 — Marta Rusk (Days 68–69, Saturday–Sunday).** The only procedure the Office has: *a batch may be produced to a person named in one of its entries, alone, once, with a clerk at the door.* He is the only living person named in entry nine, and the batch is not disputed, and the amendment is not contested, and the reason is arithmetic: **conceding a delay costs nothing once the delay is over.** The amendment records eleven days pending, the delayed priority medicine, eight months of disputed burial, the office, the batch, and states that no part of either figure is returned. He asks for one sentence — *the record of this residence status is corrected now; it was not corrected then* — is told it is not customary, asks whether it is untrue, is told no, and it goes in. His own name stays on row nine because the officer went down the queue in that order, and it is the single most comforting thing he finds in the record room and it is not a comfort. **The other forty people in that batch have not been asked and cannot be made to ask.** That evening in the ward hall the four pages are answered in their own hands: Bram's refusal ruled on Day 60, **Holm Ferris's duty** (the copy at the weir path) which answers the question he asked in the third week, **Ada's four sentences** whose second is that the district's paper does not claim a seed stall, and **Wenna's page blank on purpose**, sent blank a fifth time. He got the thing. It is a sentence about a delay that has already happened.

**Chapter 50 — One Page, and a Drawer (Day 70, Monday).** The season has ended; the water is down. A posting comes down in an envelope with no letter in it: back to the Fourth Desk, the unfiled correspondence tray, and the record of the district returns. A lateral move, and the work is better, and nobody did him a favour. He reads District Return 487-LL — **one page, four words of scope, twenty-one living witnesses, and not one of them Owen Rusk** — and does the arithmetic twice: eight branches, two observers, four named objectors, six persons whose acts are recorded and who are not branches, and the steward. Three names people will look for are not on it, each for a reason on the same file. **Nothing is restored**: the pump grant, the crossing, the repair, the wage, the sixteen, the order, the answer, the six, the forty unasked people, the receipt on the spike, and the name of a man of fifty-one who carries bundles up a stair. He works out that the volume was not about the page. The last action of Volume 01 is his hand flat on black braided cord on a drawer he is not permitted to touch, with a brass tab reading **ALDER ROW — 461** and an index card behind it saying **never opened** — and he does not open it, and chooses, that afternoon, not to. He goes back up to a desk he no longer wanted **at ten minutes to three, with about forty minutes left on a certification sealed on Day 63 at about half past three, and a list of five lines**, and does not write the name of a man of fifty-one down anywhere at all.

## Volume 02 — *Clean Count*

### Batch 0001 — Chapters 51–60, *The Season Opens* (written, C.Y. 487 Days 70–91 into C.Y. 488 Days 1–12)

**Chapter 51 — The Hour of the Seal (Day 70, Monday).** Nothing happens, and the chapter is about that. A certification that arrives at the end of its term produces no form, no notice and no clerk, and at about half past three a line in the record of the district returns changes in a column ruled by a machine: the crossing's status is suspended again, the preservation has run its term and fallen with it, and the Day 10 load-restriction appeal window is **closed** — expired, not refused, and in no register of contested closures anywhere. Owen writes down what a preservation actually is (*the person who holds it carries the part that is not resolved*) and that the part that is not resolved has not gone anywhere, and that there is now no instrument in this city with a person on it who is answerable for it. In the ward hall Ada asks **what did it cost** and will not accept *four weeks and a form*; he gives her five hours a week of somebody's life, and the two things he could hold in his head at once. Bray rules it, rules the hour, and rules that nobody in the room is under it. He says the whole of it was *a status with a date on it and a window in it, for four weeks*. Then, eleven inches below the repair notice on the post at the top of Fetter Lane, a notice that is not from his office: the counting season opens on the first day of the month, and the schedule has been printed and posted at Cutter's Fields. Eleven lines. He realises, standing in the wet, that seven weeks of arguing about whether his ward **existed** never once asked whether it could be **counted**.

**Chapter 52 — The List Comes Off (Days 71–72).** The tray is not a punishment. It is where the correspondence of **places the Office cannot find** goes, and it arrives because it cannot be returned, and the first thing to come up is a quarterly notice addressed to a **culvert house at Pegler's Cut**, returned three times, the third with *demolished* pencilled across it by a careless hand: the building is a heap of stone in a river, the drain still runs into a field, and the field is in the schedule of premises while the house is in nothing, and there is no form on which a person may say *the water still comes*. Owen finds the entry in the district works return — the eighteenth of the month, *removed from schedule, not reinstated* — and does not copy anything. He then goes up the hill with **no sheet** and reads the five lines out in a ward hall in the order they were written, at the pace of a man reading a menu, and four people say the number four thousand one hundred and sixty out loud at the same time, because it has stopped being a number and become a noise. Bray rules the five in with the day and the hour, and rules under them, unasked, that no person in the room is under any of them, including the man who read them out. Coming down the hill his arms are shaking and the reason is that the skill of holding something did not go away when the reason for it did. On the Wednesday he finds a **retention notice** wired to the spike: paper is retained for the period stated on the sheet it belongs to, and on expiry disposed of by the standing rule, and **the rule does not require a person to be told**. He works out that the receipt is the oldest thing on the spike and that nobody is going to tell the ward. He takes it off the spike and carries it up a stair and puts it under a weight on his own tray, in full view of a building where he is a stranger, and writes one line about it in a book that leaves the building. **The line turns out to be worth nothing, and he gets the arithmetic wrong by thirty-eight days while making it.**

**Chapter 53 — Premises of Record (Days 73–76, Thursday–Sunday).** The Return of Premises comes down with eleven other bundles, one per district in the county, and it is a good form: the premises, the persons at it, the water by instrument, and a fourth column for any person at a premises who cannot be asked — the care act now performed, and the name of the person who performs it, and *this line may not be left blank; if there is no such person, enter none*. The ward can fill three columns out of its own seven weeks of public paper. The twelfth line of the first column is **the pitch at the foot of Fetter Lane**, which has no door, is not premises of record, and cannot be made one by a district writing it on a form; nobody fills it in, and Bray rules in the delivery book that no premises is claimed at the pitch, that the pitch is not the district's to give away, and that the district has never made an entry of a place against a claim and is not beginning now because somebody has printed a form. **Nessa Kray, forty-one years on the pitch, declines to be thanked for the strangeness of being told it is hers and then declined.** Column three is four true lines: water drawn by hand at four in the morning, a pump whose grant is suspended, a standpipe rota that is a sheet and not an instrument, and a man of fifty-eight who is owed a pension no column will hold. Column four is the one they argue about for two days. **Ada asks the room, once, whether anybody will take it, and says beforehand that anybody who said yes out of that would be doing her a disservice.** Nobody answers. Bray says no. Ada dictates the eighth line of the return, in capitals: the district has six persons it cannot be asked, is performing a care act for all six, **there is no person in this district who keeps anything for anybody, the word for that is not written beside any name in this hall, nobody is to be asked, and the cost is five hours a week and that figure is a figure and not a person.** **Wenna Sarr writes to Owen for the first time in her life** — four lines, about the fourth column, not a plea, and asking only that it be recorded that she has read it — and his reply says he has not looked up the name on the board by the second counter and is not going to, because that person is frightened and writes better forms than he does. The return goes up on the Sunday morning of the seventy-sixth day with a line missing on purpose, and **in nine days it comes back marked *incomplete* in a hand nobody in that hall had seen — the ward's first non-refusal**, and Owen works out standing up that a thing marked incomplete comes back with a question on it, and the question is a person, and an answer to a person has to have a name in it. The word is still on the file.

**Chapter 54 — The Board at Cutter's Fields (Days 77–79).** Cutter's Fields is nine flat acres behind the tanneries and it is the only ground in this city where a person can stand and see the water gate from where they are standing. **There is no queue.** There are three columns and eleven rows: name, year of last reconciliation, premises of record — and the figures run **nine, four, six, eleven, two, nine, seven, three, eleven, nine, six.** He reads the third column first because he is a man who reads columns, and stops being a man reading a column. **The fourth row is Lower Lock, 486, eleven.** Three rows are 479, one is 481, and the Count of C.Y. 487 was suspended in the month of rain and was not taken, so the third column has not been checked by anybody in nine years. A woman of about thirty with a slate — no name on the board, twice refused to have one elsewhere — asks him whether a county figure is a figure about a settlement or about its premises, tells him his own ward has two household references that are not buildings and one that is a pitch, and says she will not talk about the Glass Stair because a man from there asked her nineteen questions about arithmetic once. At Arbour Kitchen, **Nell Arbour** has a count sheet for a different settlement, eleven rows, **three hands** and two of them the office's, and gives him the thing nobody else can: *the Guard writes about buildings, never about a person, in nine years, not once.* Her own sentence is in the chapter and it becomes the volume's question — **somebody decided, and it was not the field, and I want to know on what** — and she asks him for a skill and not for anything else, and notices the difference, and neither of them says anything about it. The quota of **eleven** goes up on a second sheet, set at Concord Hall on the twenty-eighth, posted whether or not the settlements on the schedule are known to the person who sets it. At the foot of the first sheet, in a smaller hand, one line: *the year of the last reconciliation is given against each settlement, and a settlement whose last reconciliation was not a reconciliation of premises may be reconciled of something else.* He reads it twice and does not go and find out.

**Chapter 55 — The Boat and the Tent (Days 80–84).** He is on a wet bank at Fetter Bend at a quarter past seven on the Thursday with a bucket because the pump did not take on Monday or Tuesday, and the Census Guard comes up the river: a flat-bottomed survey boat, a sawn mast, a new tarpaulin, a wooden case, and **two flags at the bow**, upper one a blue field with a stacked stone on it, which he has seen twice in his life on a form and on a wall and never on a river. The device is the whole of the symbolism and is faintly insulting and never said so. **Warden Oress Kade**, on stage for the first time anywhere in this novel: about the height of Ada Rusk, river mud to the knee on one boot, who will not do it standing up in a field at a quarter past eight because she can see the pump does not take. She states the method the way a doctor states a dosage — **fourteen days** of emergency suspension per person, **thirty** before supported restoration is impossible, no office can extend either, and at the end of the fourteen a **count correction** made by the Provincial Counting Office and attested by her, with a schedule of premises attached, a date, and **a reason, and the reason is a figure, because a figure is the only thing that can be written in a column that has to mean the same thing in Concord Hall as it means in a field behind the tanneries.** The correction takes the draw back into the district's main, and with it the fund and **the right of the people living in it to be witnessed at an address on a district act**, which is the thing nobody outside the service puts on a table. Asked whether fourteen days is ever enough: **no**, and she says what it is for, and it is nearly all it is for, and she has never found a way to make the form say it. Asked who wrote the schedule: **the District Counting Office**, on the returns received for a count that was never taken, printed on the twenty-eighth and posted with a quota, dated before she knew the name of one of the eleven. She has written four letters, had three answered in eleven weeks correctly, **returned the schedule once and had it come back with the same eleven and a date four days later**, and will not write a fifth. She will not say who in the office, and she explains exactly why not: *I could not tell you where my number would come from.* She reads the district's list of officers, tells him she has had his file since the spring, and says she did not put Lower Lock on her list — and asks him why he thinks that is a favour. He works out in a minute what took her four seasons: eleven settlements, eleven days, a quota, and a district that gets reported short. She asks him to write to **her and not the Council** when he knows who wrote the list, and to expect nothing: *I will do nothing with it and I will not pretend to.* Then **Maud Tray** arrives with a fire pump on a cart for Ossery, unasked, having drawn water off it since the spring on her own hook, and Kade turns the district's draw round in about ninety seconds with a rule nobody had thought to use — a work of record maintained within the season carries the premises it serves. Owen cannot tell whether that is the service working or one competent person rescuing a rule, and never does. Three more settlements go: a saw, a hard winter, and a place cut in two by a road in C.Y. 459 that still has two numbers and one door.

**Chapter 56 — Nether Sill (Days 85–89, Tuesday–Saturday).** The count sheet is a public document and it took him four minutes to get and forty to read, in a room at the Glass Stair with a window that does not shut. It is the reconciliation of C.Y. 486 and the county's only description of the place. **Nine premises.** The fourth column says, in the ordinary form's voice, why the form is four columns long: *a room in a building is not a premise unless it is a dwelling, and a room in a mill has a roof and has a door at the bottom of a stair but has not got its own water and has not got a door to the outside, and cannot be made one by a district writing it on a sheet.* Under the columns, in a different hand, dated the ninth of the month in C.Y. 486, a counting clerk's margin note: nine premises, six of them empty on the day and empty since, asked about four times in nine years and not struck; persons found, thirty-one; *if they are struck, the six households that have come in since will be counted against four premises and the settlement will fail on arithmetic and not on fact, and I would rather it failed on nothing.* Owen reads both eleven times and works out that the harm is being done by the district's correct answer to the wrong question. He tells Ada on the Thursday in a hall, as a form and not a story, and Ada stops a man who says *write to the district office*: *do not tell him to write to the district office. He works in the district office. He has sat two floors above a book that says what premises this county has, and he has not opened it.* **Nell Arbour does not come**, and says no in nine words, and the reason is on the page: two women at two landings on the same reach keep two tide tables and have not spoken in two years, and she will not stand in a room between them on a Registry errand. She gives him instead a sheet from a different settlement with **three hands** and the question: which of us has been writing the figures. They go up on the Saturday with **Prue Hallam**, seventeen, who pilots. The bend is ordinary and working: a market on a road that is not a street of record, a post with the price of meal on it, bread, a curtain round a bed at the top of a mill. **Hanna Roe**, fifty-four, keeps the sluice, has opened the cut twice a day for twenty-six years, and refuses the second column in advance and without being asked, in the plainest terms in the book: *nobody has ever come and told me I was the reason.* She also gives him the whole case: a room at the top of a mill is a dwelling if it has a tap, a drain, a door to the outside on the ground, and **a number on the door**, and the number has to be given by a district office, and the form is item nine of a schedule in a book in that office, and **there is a copy of that book on a public stand in the count room at the Glass Stair which he has walked past eleven times in eight years.** He understands, standing up, that he is going to spend the next forty-eight hours failing because of it, and tells her so, and she offers to answer one question. On the water he writes seven lines and does not add an eighth, because the eighth is the one that would have worked and he cannot write it because he does not know whether it is true. She tells him at the slip: **do not bring a figure.**

**Chapter 57 — The Objection (Day 90, Sunday). The batch turn.** He wrote it on the Saturday night and it is four sheets and it is correct, and the chapter says so first, because everything else in it is about a man who did everything right. The third clause is the one that would have worked: *that the district office be required to consider the making of a dwelling of record of two rooms at the head of a working mill, there being nine houses upon a road on that bank which is not a street of record and has never had a number.* Then he works out he has no standing to file it. **Hanna Roe's refusal is at a quarter past eight and takes four words: she has read it, it is a request, and the request has a second column on the other side of it that is going to want a name in it by Wednesday, and the second column wants the person who performs the act, and she is the person in the sluice, and when the count comes back wrong somebody will come and ask Hanna Roe how a thing in her own name was allowed to fail — and she would rather the file said nobody was keeping anything for the man in the chair.** **Neve Searle**, twenty-six, eleven days without a night off, reads all four sheets standing up, says the clause is a good clause first because she will not be the kind of person who cannot read one, and refuses for her father: sixty-eight, a stroke the night the water came to the sill, a chair by the window that can see the cut, four questions a day and two answers, and *enter none* is the only true entry and she would like it on a public file more than she would like the water. Owen says out loud, before the room, that he has no standing and is not asking either of them. **Neve Searle gives him the ground for the whole batch: the reason you are up here is a boy in a chair.** Nine people in the mill office, a stone on the table, a slate. He speaks once, four minutes, facts and not argument, because an argument is the one thing that cannot go in the note. Kade writes six minutes in his words and says the note is not the reason, and then gives the four answers, all of them the law: the fact is four; **she has known about the nine houses on that bank for four years and has never written it down, because a Warden who writes down a thing she has seen but cannot act on is doing a district office's work badly and at a distance**; the fourteen runs to the eleventh and nobody can extend it; and the fourth, which is the only one that is not the law — **you have asked the right question to the wrong room for two days, and a dwelling of record is made by a district office, in a book, on an application, and the form is item nine of the schedule in the same book, and there is a copy of that book on a public stand in the count room at the Glass Stair, and I have never mentioned it because I have never had to sit in a room for two days with a man who could open a book.** He is answered, and the door is **locked from the inside**, by two people who cannot read very well and who were not frightened and who have done nothing wrong. On the bank she makes him say it to the water — *that is the first true thing anybody has said in front of my count and it is not a fact about the count* — and tells him the order will not be changed for him because changing it would change it for eleven places. Then she tells him the shape of the door: **do not write to me yet**; there are **thirty-one settlements of record in this district, twenty answered, eleven did not**, and she has never once asked herself why the twenty that answered are the twenty that answered, and neither has he. The woman with the slate asks him whether the eleven have a shape, whether they are the ones that never answered or the ones that answer worst, whether the twenty that answered are small, whether the eleven are all upstream, and **whether there is one figure on that board that appears eleven times out of eleven and whether that figure is a draw.** He does not read the board. He does the tray. He is fifty minutes late and notices on the Thursday.

**Chapter 58 — What the Warden Knows (Day 91 and 488 Day 1).** He reads the board on the Monday, finally, four days late, twice, the second time slower — and finds the shape: nine of the eleven are outside the town, and the two that are inside it are the fourth name and the eleventh, and the eleventh is six houses that apologised for being late. **The count does not go where the people are. It goes where the figures are stale.** The Count of 487 was never taken, so a row's figure is a count of doors made in the year in the other column, and a door that has come down does not write to anybody. And **there is no fourth column**: no column of water, no column of draw, on a board whose own notice says the Count is a count of draws and premises and works maintained. Her fifth question has a first half he can answer — eleven different numbers, no figure repeated — and a second half he cannot, because the column is a floor below the counting clerk who wrote *demolished* in pencil in the spring, in the **schedule of draws**, in a building he has worked in for eight years. He does not go and open it. That night in the tent, with the lamp and the case and the man of sixty asleep on a cot, Kade answers the question he came to ask. **She cannot be dealt with**, in four reasons: she cannot amend a schedule; she does not make figures and a warden who could would be asked for a different figure next season; **she decides which losses she will accept in about nine minutes on the first morning of the tent, before she has heard whether the people would rather be struck than counted, and has never asked them, and knows exactly what that is**; and she keeps a **private list of the settlements she was right about**, nine seasons, ten of the eleven say *held* and one says *wrong and I knew*, and it is the reason she cannot be dealt with, because a list of the ones she was right about is a list of the ones she was wrong about. He asks which one and is refused. She then gives him the whole method, including the review: a person of the district, a **current local act performed by the settlement**, heard before an officer of the count with no district in the matter, **within seven days of the reconciliation**, and the person **named on the request**, on a public file. The window ran to a quarter past eleven on the seventh and he is a person of Lower Lock. There is a local act at Nether Sill and it is the best answer to that clause in the district, and if the settlement had asked on Sunday it would have gone to Concord Hall on Monday. The door is locked from the inside, and she would like it written down that **the reason there is no review at Nether Sill is that two people who cannot read very well declined to put their names on a piece of paper, and they were not frightened, and nobody has done anything to them.** She asks him to come to Marl Dykes and stand in a room and say nothing; he cannot, because if he stands in a room then what the room says about itself becomes a document of his office — and she calls that the best answer anybody in the Registry has given her in nineteen years. He walks up under the two notices and does not go to see a man who knows what a district office does with a settlement it cannot account for, because the seventh was a Monday and the seventh was the last hour of a door. At about ten past eleven he asks the question he has been carrying — *have you ever been wrong* — and she says **seventeen times** and then says **Hark Holm**, flatly, as a season she worked: the Concord Year 484, four households, a low bank eleven miles up, a notice returned twice, a man of about seventy and his sister and a young couple with a child on a slip a ferry needed; she struck them, the correction is correct and attested and her name is on it and she did not lie in one word; the slip was rebuilt in about eleven days by the people who lived on it, and the low bank came down on the fifteenth and took **eleven households** off a reach that had been on the count all year, because the four that had come off were not on it, and **there was nothing on the count that said the wall was there.** It worked. It is the best thing she has ever done. **She does not know how many died and never counted them, because the form has a schedule of premises and a figure for a reason and no box anywhere for the number of people in eleven households on a reach**, and she has asked twice and been told in eleven weeks that the matter is closed because the premises are recorded as struck and the premises are the count. One of the four is alive. She is at Wexford Landing, she is not one of the four premises there, she is the fifth, she is seventy-nine, she keeps the book, and she asked whether the warden had remembered the dog. The year turns with no ceremony and no bell. The first thing C.Y. 488 does in Docketford is put a **day-book of the count** on the board with a quota of eleven at the head and one line on it: *Ninetieth. Nether Sill. Premises counted: four. Reason: four. Sealed.*

**Chapter 59 — Three Holds (488 Days 2–6).** The application is one sheet and the sixth thing it wants is *the settlement in which the applicant is a person*, and Docketford district is a district and not a settlement, and he writes that and underlines it and writes *I understand that this may be refused.* On the Wednesday morning he finds the receipt gone: the disposal register has one entry that is not a Tuesday or a Friday, made on the Monday he spent in a field — **487-GS-19, papers of a petition concluded, certification of the sixty-third expired, no instrument in the matter in force, disposed, item nine** — and the period was fourteen days from the last entry, and the last entry was the twentieth of the month before, and **he had counted the fourteen from the day the notice went up and the thing was in its fifty-second day, and a notice does not start a clock, it reports one.** Taking it off the spike made no difference: the standing rule disposes of the papers of a matter concluded wherever the papers are put. He does not tell anybody for six days. Then he goes to the count room at the Glass Stair, which he has walked past four times a week for eight years, and opens the **public stand** for the first time, and at half past twelve on the Wednesday reads four minutes of a blue book last revised in the Concord Year 402 and finds, in the ordinary hand of a clerk of that year: ***The Mill Road, Nether Sill. Nine premises. Two dwelling-houses and a shop, a barn, and five dwelling-houses.*** Nine premises on a street of record. **What it meant:** the eight households in the mill are not a mystery; their houses are nine rows in a book with a street against them, and a house full of silt is still a house. **What it did not mean:** a row in a copy on a public stand is not an act, and somebody between the copy and the current book struck those nine rows or never carried them forward, and he does not know in which book they went, and if they went in a district office, somebody in a district office did it. He is examined on the Thursday by a woman of about fifty from Concord Hall with no district in the matter, who asks the standing question — *state the figure, state what the figure is a figure of, and state who put it there* — and **Owen supplies the third part before she does**: the District Counting Office, on a return of premises for C.Y. 486, from a schedule of premises which is kept in that building **for the Water Office of this district**, carried into a count that was never taken and from the suspended count into the schedule printed on the twenty-eighth. She records, in one sentence, that he is the answer to his own third question. Then the limits, given in full on the first morning: an examiner may challenge arithmetic, procedure and the currency of any paper a reconciliation rests on, and may not examine the schedule, may not examine a choice of places, and **may not put a word in the reason column** — *you will spend your whole term finding errors in arithmetic and every one of them will be about a figure and not one about a person's water.* The supervised case is **Gallants Reach**, where a count sheet of the Concord Year 479 was never closed because the Count of 480 was suspended, was carried into 481, and every season since has been taken off the carried sheet; he challenges it in the proper form, it is sustained the same morning, and **Gallants Reach comes back onto the schedule of settlements to be reconciled — six premises, eleven households, a stone, a room, fourteen days and a reason in a box in the second week of the season** — and **no person's water changes by a single gallon anywhere in Docketford district.** He writes *Yes* in the box asking whether the applicant has an interest in the matter, and the examining officer tells him the office will call the record thin and puts *thin* in the third box herself. On the fourth day he is sealed a Continuance Examiner on the seal stair in front of nine people. **The card's last line is the only sentence in the Registry's instruments that says *the written agreement of the claimant*,** and it is the fifth part of a requirements sheet a ward hall in Fetter Lane has been filling in by hand since the third week of the month of rain, and it is not a cure. **Claimant one is Ivy Sarr's household, a person who cannot be asked, and it is his Volume 01 named duty and the register will not carry a person twice under two heads.** Wenna Sarr puts his four lines on the table and makes him say the rest of it. **Claimant two is Mavis Rudge of Witherow**, fifty-four, who wants a review and does not want to win it: three premises, eleven households, five of the eleven on no list at all including her sister, and *I want a line in a file with my name at the top of it that says I asked and I was answered and the answer was no.* He tells her the review will fail and enters it anyway, with both names on it, and she thanks him in nine words **for the entering and not for the finding**, and it is the worst thing that has happened to him since the month of rain. **Claimant three is Ned Callow of Quill Bank**, who asked for nothing in thirty years and asked for a person in the room; he refuses with the same sentence he gave Kade on Friday night, and Ned Callow says: *you have been in this city thirty-two years, and the first thing anybody from the Registry has ever offered me is a reason why they cannot come* — and Owen understands, at the fourth minute, that **a principle which is only ever a reason somebody is not in a room is not a principle, it is a form.** Ada tells him in the shed that he has never once decided, before answering, what kind of question he was being asked. **Pell is offered the count's district correspondence and refuses it in one sentence — I would be the district's face for a count I have not looked at** — and warns him that four people in that ward are already on files about themselves. On the Sunday afternoon the prepared correction comes up on the tray with a bundle-slip: *this correction is examined as to arithmetic and procedure and may be read out. The correction is correct. The appeal lies to the Glass Stair and will take a season. It will be attested on the twelfth.* The reason in the box is **four**.

**Chapter 60 — The Reason (488 Days 7–12, Monday–Saturday). The batch climax.** There is no form for asking for a figure to be printed in public, and there is a form for a request by an examiner at item fourteen, with four lines — settlement, instrument, publication applied for, authority — and he writes *I have no authority for it* in the fourth line and signs it, and it comes back at twenty past four on the Monday with the word **granted** in pencil and no condition and no note. The woman who maintains the board, nine years, no name on the board by her own request, does not want to know why, and says *four columns is a lot for a board that is nine feet* and *I have wondered about it every season and I have never said so, because it is not a thing a person says out loud to a counting ground.* The board goes up on the Wednesday with four columns and the figures **nine, four, six, eleven, two, nine, seven, three, eleven, nine, six** against reasons **four, four, six, eleven, two, nine, seven, three, eleven, nine, six** — and the reason for that is not a secret and not a policy: **there is no reason yet.** The ten have not been reconciled, and a reason is made at the end of the fourteen by a warden in a box the size of her hand, and until then the office has nothing to put there and has put the figure it already had. And **three of the eleven rows are figures belonging to corrections that are not yet attested**, and he published them on the word *granted* written in pencil, and if a warden in a room with a lamp in it had got one wrong the error would have stood on a public board for four days under his hand. **The row against Lower Lock is eleven, and eleven is a correct figure of the doors of Lower Lock and a figure of nothing else, and he is the officer of the record of the district returns and cannot say what a figure of eleven is a reason for.** He tells the ward on the Wednesday evening, with the stove on: the fourth column, the row with the eleven twice, and what an eleven is a figure of, in plain words, to about fourteen people. Then he tells them the receipt, in the wrong order, which is the order a thing like that has to be told in: its period run out on the thirty-fourth and disposed of on the ninety-first under item nine, no person required to be told, the fifth of five, still owed, and there is now nothing on any desk in this city that says so. And that he took it off the spike on the seventy-second day and put it under a weight on his own tray and that it made no difference whatever, and that he did the arithmetic wrong at the time. Bray rules that no person in the hall is to be blamed, that item nine is correct, and that the fifth line is now the only one of the five that cannot be paid at all. Ada: *so there are four left and none of them has moved and one of them cannot be moved, and nobody did anything to it. That is the count.* The granary was empty on the fourth and is at a fortnight on four tons of meal put in against a paper that is not money. The sixteen are sixteen and Stability Order 114 is in appeal and **none of it touches any of that.** Bray asks for the eleven again and Ada writes the ward's answer in the delivery book that night, in her own hand, the ninth line of the ward's answer to the Count and the only one of the nine that is not a schedule of what the ward has done: *the Count wants premises. The paper of this district says four words. The four words are not premises. This district performs and is not counted and says so itself, in this hand, and does not intend to stop.* **Lower Lock's own reconciliation is on the day-book for the third week of the season**, and Ada reads it off the board and says one word. On the Friday **Cobb Rennie** comes down the hill on his own two feet to stand in front of a board, reads the fourth column twice, and asks what the eleven is: not the pitch, not his two rooms, not the girl of twelve on the standpipe at four in the morning. *You have written me down twice in a month. Two years and one month, and that is the first time anybody has stood in a field and told me what a number is not a number of. It is worth less than money and more than nothing, and I am not going to say thank you, and I am going to walk up the hill and stand at that board again tomorrow.* The day-book has **four lines** against a quota of eleven and an eleven-day notice that is on no form anywhere and is Owen's own arithmetic from a field; the district is reported short by seven, and the man of sixty, thirty-one years in the work, says a district that runs behind is not a district where a warden was careless. A man Owen does not know asks whether the schedule is the same as the stability order, and is told no, in a sentence and a half, in a field: two offices, two dates, two lists, neither can move a line on the other, and the difference between them is a column. **The reading is at about four in the afternoon, about thirty people, fourteen of them from Lower Lock, and nobody at all from Nether Sill, because the form does not provide for a service.** He reads the settlement, the year, the nine recorded, the four produced, the four struck by name, the reason twice, the date, the attestation, the water, and the appeal — and then reads the fourth column, all eleven of it, and the Lower Lock row twice, and says out loud, in the ordinary voice, in a field, that ten of the reasons are the number of doors written twice, that the fourth row is eleven, that it is a correct figure of the doors of a ward of two hundred and forty people, that he cannot tell them what a figure of eleven is a reason for, that it took an afternoon and nothing required it, and that it is not a remedy and he does not know what it is. Kade listens from forty feet off at the west gate and says it is the first true thing said in front of her count in nineteen seasons and she will not be able to use any of it, and that she asked for it and did not sleep, and that there is now one public column in this county where a person can stand and see that a figure is correct, and **she does not know what to do with that and is not going to pretend to.** She gives the true answer four times this season and says she would like somebody in this city to be angry at her about it. Then the board stays up, and underneath the schedule at the foot of the frame is a standing extract the count room has kept every season for a hundred and forty years, four hundred and six rows, headed **PLACES STRUCK FROM THE COUNT OF THIS DISTRICT.** The year **461** is in the third row from the bottom, and the place in it is a ward officially closed in that year, struck out of the count with a figure against it in the same ink in the same ordinary hand. **He reads the heading and one row and stops, and does not read the column, and the reason is that a fourth column on a board and a drawer under black cord are the same decision in two places and he made it at the top of a stair in the last month of the last year and a man does not get to make it twice in a season.** Eleven settlements, eleven reasons, ten of them the premises column written twice, a girl of twelve on no column of anything, ten settlements on the list not yet corrected, and one of them four streets from where he sleeps.
