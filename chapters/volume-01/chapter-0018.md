# Chapter 18 — The Signature

He worked out the hour before anybody had to tell him, which was the only thing he got right that day.

The Council's counter takes the week's instruments on the Friday at ten, and had taken them on the Friday at ten for as long as anybody in the building could remember, and an instrument that is to be at a counter at ten on the Friday has to be signed on the Thursday, and the Deputy Registrar had signed the Thursday instruments at ten past four in the afternoon for nineteen years, in a room with a door that shut properly, with a runner waiting.

So the signature would be at ten past four on the twenty-fourth, and everything that had to be in the file before the signature had to be on the countersignature desk before four, and there was a form of words for a thing arising after entry, and it was the first time in a month that Owen Rusk had wanted to write something down more than he had wanted to sleep.

He got up at five. He wrote the amendment before the light, in his own front room, at the table with his mother's good plate on it, and then he did what he had learned to do in the first week of the month, which was to go and look at the physical act before he put the description of it on paper.

At nine he went up to the seal stair, which is the correct route, and asked the clerk on duty — the one who had been there twenty years and who had made him read a requirements sheet at arm's length — a question, and it was a proper question and she answered it and wrote nothing down.

"Does the Council's counter take the week's instruments on the Friday?"

"At ten. It has taken them at ten since before I came."

"Then an instrument for Friday is signed on the Thursday."

"The Deputy Registrar signs at ten past four. He has signed at ten past four for nineteen years and there is a pot of tea on the tray at a quarter to and it is not a ritual, it is just that he has never once been late." She looked at him. "Why."

"I am trying to know the shape of a day," said Owen, "and I have found out that you are not obliged to tell me and that you have, and I am grateful."

"You are welcome. And you are also going to be in this building at a quarter to five this afternoon, and I shall not see you, and I do not care what you do between now and then as long as you understand that the door shuts at five and the stamp goes on the front of the instrument and not on the back."

He went upriver at a quarter to eleven.

---

Ada Rusk had got to the hall at eight.

He found that out at a quarter to eleven, at the top of the hill, from a boy out of the mill, who had heard it from the factor, who had heard it at the counter on Tuesday when the queue was long and people talk. He was two hours late to his own warning, and the reason he was two hours late was that he had spent from ten until a quarter to eleven standing in a hall on the second floor listening to a thing he had to hear, and he would have gone on listening to it until the day after tomorrow.

The ward hall was full and everybody was in it and nobody was working, and Ada was standing at the long table with a slate and chalk and the delivery book open, and she had told them. He came in at eleven and the hall stopped talking for about four seconds and then started again, and he understood that it had been going to start again whatever he said and that his coming in had cost it four seconds and that he could not get those four seconds back.

"You are late," said Ada.

"I am."

"Sit down. You are not going to stand at the end of that table and say it. I have said it, and I have said it properly, and if you stand up there now then for the rest of this week the only thing anybody in this room will remember about the warning is that the Registry sent a clerk to deliver it."

He sat down.

Here is what Ada Rusk had done between eight and half past ten, and he got it out of her in pieces over the following two days and never entirely, and what she had done was this. She had gone to the hall and got the stove going and put the delivery book on the table where everybody could see it, and had said that there was going to be a thing signed this afternoon at the Deputy Registrar's office, that it was called a stability order, that it was a real instrument with a number on it, that she had got the number from Owen Rusk on Wednesday night, that she did not know what was in it, and that when anybody found out she would write it in the book in front of them.

"Somebody asked me how I knew," she said. "Somebody had to ask me how I knew, because otherwise it is a rumour and I am a woman talking. So I said: on Wednesday night at about nine o'clock the Registry's own clerk came up this hill to tell me that there is an instrument and that it is drafted and waiting for one signature, and that he told me because he thought I ought to be told and because he was wrong, and that the reason he was wrong is that he cannot keep a thing off a person he is trying to help."

"You said that."

"I said it in front of thirty people," said Ada, "and I would say it again, and it is not because I am kind. It is because if I had not said it then the first version of this that anybody in this hall heard would have come from somebody in a good coat at that table, and I have known what that does to a room since the seventh day of the month."

---

The amendment was finished at ten past one.

The ward had a meeting at half past twelve on the Thursday because Ada had called it at eight, and the standing order of a meeting called by the crew leader at eight o'clock in the morning is that it is a meeting of the return, and there were eight branches left, and the eighth was a man of forty-four who had a badly mended roof, and all eight of them came, and Marguerite Bray took the minutes because the minutes were a delivery book.

Ada dictated it standing at the end of the table with her hands behind her back, and it took her about forty minutes, and it was four clauses, and Owen Rusk wrote it out in longhand on the form and then read it back and she made him read the third clause twice.

*AMENDMENT TO DISTRICT RETURN 487-LL. Entered the twenty-fourth day of the month, from the hour of the signature of the instrument named in the second clause.*

*First. The district records, as a fact arising after the entry of this return and not caused by any act, omission, silence or failure of the district or of any person in it, that an instrument was signed this day at the Deputy Registrar's office for Docketford under his solvency authority, upon sixteen named returns of the present quarter, of which this return is one.*

*Second. That instrument is Stability Order 114. It closes or suspends the returns named in it together. The district does not know at the hour of this entry whether its return is closed or suspended, and this amendment is not conditional on which, and is entered either way.*

*Third. This amendment alters no act, no scope, no term, no observer and no duty recorded in this return. It is a new entry from the hour named above and it erases nothing. The duties recorded in this return are performed and are to be performed at the same interval and in the same manner as they have been performed, and the ward asks that any officer considering otherwise record that position in the review.*

*Fourth. This return claims nothing as to the safety, condition or use of the crossing, and nothing in this amendment, and nothing in the first clause, is a claim that the crossing is unsafe, and the district asks that the office not read one into the other.*

"Third clause," Ada said. "Again. Slowly."

Owen read it again.

"That is you," she said, "and I want to know whether you can say what it is for."

"You want a reviewer who thinks a duty stops when a status does to say so on the record, so that when somebody reads this file in a year they can see that the ward knew the question and asked for it in writing."

"Whose duty is it, though." She had not let go of him and she was not going to. "Say it in one sentence. Whose duty is the marker on a Tuesday?"

"Yours."

"Then a reviewer who reads my duty as a status has made a category mistake and it will be his for ever and it will be on a piece of paper and I do not have to be in the room." She turned round to the hall. "That is the amendment. Is there anybody in this room who wants to change a word of it."

There was not, and Marguerite Bray recorded that there was not, in the delivery book, with the day and the hour, and then recorded the hour it was recorded.

Junia Pell witnessed it at the long table and Wend Voss witnessed it at the door, and then Pell took it, folded in four, in the inside of her coat, and Owen did not ask her to, and she did not explain, and she was out of the door at twenty past three and gone down the hill in the rain, and Owen stood in the hall and understood that he had arranged a woman to run down a hill on his account and that she had not needed arranging and that this was becoming a habit on both sides.

---

The mill's factor's version came first, at about six.

It came by a boy on a bicycle with no mud on him, which told Owen it had been in a bar, and it was that an order had been signed closing the return of sixteen wards in the district, and that the ward's own was one of them, and that the Ninth Weir had gone with it, and that Little Fetter was one of them, and that is all anybody at the factor's counter had.

The hall made a noise Owen had not heard in it before. It was not a shout. It was about forty people finding out at the same time that the thing they had been afraid of for a month had happened, and it is a specific noise, and he had never heard it in this room because in this room people had never before had a date.

"Listen to me," he said, and the room did not stop, and then Ada Rusk put her hand flat on the table and the room stopped, because in a hall of this kind that is what a flat hand on a table has meant for sixty years.

"They have it wrong," Ada said. "Sit down and let him read it."

It was the countersignature desk's acknowledgement slip, and Pell had sent it up with a runner at ten past six, and on the front there were four lines about an amendment received at four minutes to four and initialled and filed, and on the back, because a countersignature desk writes what it has done on the back of the slip, there was a list.

Sixteen lines. A number against each and a name against each and one word against each, and the word was the same on all sixteen lines, and the word was:

*suspended.*

And at the foot, under the sixteenth line, in the same hand, two sentences: *Filed for the Council counter, Friday, ten o'clock. This instrument is public and an appeal lies to the Glass Stair, docket on application.*

Owen Rusk read the back of a slip standing in a hall in Lower Lock and did not say a word for a while, and the reason he did not say a word was that a man who has been telling a room that something is going to be closed has just been handed a piece of paper that says it is not, and the room knows it, and the only thing left in his mouth is the fact that it has been suspended instead, which is worse, and he has to say that out loud, and there is no version of saying it that does not make him look like a man taking something away.

"It is not closed," he said. "It is suspended. Read the word down the page. It is the same word sixteen times."

"Suspended what?" said somebody at the back.

"Your return's standing. Your standing as a district. Taken alongside fifteen others." He put the slip down on the table so that the back of it was face up. "There is a difference and it is the only difference and I am going to be exact about it, because I have been exact with this room before and it did not go well. Being closed is a decision about you. Somebody looked at Lower Lock and decided about Lower Lock. Being suspended with fifteen others is a queue. You are in a column of sixteen and the column is what is being acted on, and the fact that you are sixth on it does not matter this afternoon and the reason it does not matter is that it would not have mattered whichever row of the column you were in."

Nobody said anything. Somewhere behind him a woman said *fourteen* to somebody, and the somebody said *what*, and the woman said *there's fourteen on this page that I can read from here.*

Then Ada said, in a completely different voice: "Mr Rusk. Read it out from the top and read the names."

So Owen Rusk read sixteen names out loud in a ward hall in Lower Lock at ten past six in the evening on a wet Thursday, from the back of a countersignature slip, in a hand that was not shaking, and when he had finished he had not one feeling about any of them except that he could not say any of the other fifteen aloud in a room without a person in the room, and that Mrs Haldane's sister was in Little Fetter, and that the number fourteen was Coal Hill, and that there was a woman in Arbour Kitchen he had spoken to twice losing a case in Coal Hill and he had not known until that second that the case was about anything at all.

"You are not sorry for it," said Ada, later, at half past seven, with the stove going low and the hall emptying.

"No," said Owen.

"You are not glad of it either."

"I am going to have to write about that on Friday and I have not decided which it is, and I have had since six o'clock and I have not managed it." He put the slip back down on the table. "Fifteen other wards lose exactly what we lose this afternoon. The same thing, on the same afternoon, in the same half-hour. And in about four days nobody will notice, and then somebody will notice, and the route that opens when somebody notices is the public one, because an instrument that cannot be filed quietly has been filed loudly, and it has been filed loudly because the Deputy Registrar is a careful man who would not sign something he intended to bury." He looked at her. "I am relieved, Ada. That is what I have instead. I am relieved that it is public, and I am ashamed of the relief, and I want to say both of those out loud to a person who does not need me to, and I cannot work out whether doing it is honest or whether it is the only way I have of getting it out of my hands."

She stood with her arms folded and looked at him for a while, the way she had looked at him across a hall table four weeks before.

"That is the first true thing you have said to me in a month," she said. "And I want you to understand that I already knew it, and that I have been waiting for you to work it out, and that I was not going to be the one to tell you, because if I had told you it would have been worth nothing and you would have said something civil back."

She put the hand that had the chalk in on the table and turned it palm up.

"Now the other thing. Sit down, because I have been up since seven and I have got to do the Thursday marker at eight and there are two of us on the top path because Dory cannot hold a board yet." She was already walking when she said it. "You are going to sit at that table and write the thing that the box wants, the one for the first review, the one about a person who may object, and you are not going to be able to fill it in, and I have watched you not fill it in for three days. So write it and bring it to me and I will read it and I will tell you what a person who has said no can be called in a document, and it will not be what you have written, and you will not like it."
