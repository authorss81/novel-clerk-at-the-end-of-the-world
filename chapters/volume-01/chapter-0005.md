# Chapter 5 — Nobody Else Will Perform It

He had two days of nothing, which was unusual, and he spent the first of them at the Fourth Desk copying a batch that ran clean and the second of them in a state he would not have called worry.

On the second day a clerk he did not know brought a bundle of returned papers down from the seal stair and put it on the wrong desk, and among the returns was a stamped copy of the Lower Lock transfer, and the copy was closed. The office's own copy, returned because the review was over and the file had been made up. Owen looked at the closure stamp on it and did not touch it and did not think about it, because there was nothing to think about yet, and because a man who is on the fourth page of a case has no business being pleased.

Then it rained hard, and it was the sixth day.

---

Marguerite Bray's hall had the stove in the middle and the table against the wall and a row of chairs that did not match one another. Owen came in at eight with a sheet of paper and the terms Nell had given him written out in his own hand, and he was so full of the case that it took him about a minute to notice that the room was not in the shape it had been in ten days ago.

The table had been cleared. Bray's ledger was on it, open, with a bundle of loose leaves squared up on top of it and a second bundle beside that, and the two bundles were not the same size.

"You've brought a form," she said.

"I've brought terms."

"You've brought a form," she said, "and you want to read them out, and then you're going to want somebody to say yes, and I want to tell you the thing about the hall first and then you can say as much as you like."

"Say it."

She put her hand flat on the nearer bundle.

"Sixth of the month. Copy comes back." She said it as though she were handing him a coal scuttle. "Your office sent the ward its copy. That's ordinary, that's what happens, you get your copy back when the file is made up. It's in the drawer. It came back on the sixth and it has been in the drawer since I showed it to you."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She pushed the other bundle across. "This is what the hall has. This is what has been done here since the letter came, and I wrote all of it down, and there are twelve people in this ward who can stand up and say to their own knowledge what they did and who watched them do it. That is what I can give you. Acts. I have never in my life been able to give you witnesses, and you know why that is before I say it, and if you make me say it you can get up and go home."

"I know why," Owen said.

"Say it anyway. You will say it badly and then you will remember you said it badly for the rest of your life."

"Because every person who could witness the ward performing a duty is somebody the return would be about," Owen said. "They are interested parties. The act has to be seen by somebody who is not carrying the return, and the only people in Lower Lock are Lower Lock."

"Twelve hundred people would say the same thing about a church," said Bray, "and I have watched them say it for forty years, and the church does not care. That's the whole trade, that is, and you may have it, and welcome."

"Then I have no witnesses, and the wards of this city that file anything at all get them from outside the ward, and the outside of Lower Lock is Mill Ward, which is the party that took the crossing in the first place."

"Correct."

"That is the most hostile arrangement anybody could have designed."

"It is the arrangement we actually have," she said, "and I'd rather you had it plain than neat."

Owen stood at the table for a moment with his paper in his hand and understood that the case had just told him the price of itself, in the correct currency, in a warm room, from a woman who was not being cruel to him. Then he put the paper down, because he had come here to be answered and he could see that this was not going to be that kind of night, and he said the only question he had left.

"What was delivered to this ward? Not what the Office did — I've read what the Office did. I want the ward's copy of what arrived, and when it arrived, and who put it in whose hand."

Bray did not move for a moment. Then she opened the ledger past the loose leaves, to a page with a half-inch of entries in it, and turned the ledger round so that it was facing him, and did not let go of it.

"This is the back half of the roll you've been copying off all night," she said. "It is a delivery book. It isn't the Office's and it never was. Everything that comes to this hall with a stamp on it, I write down the day it came and who brought it, because in fifty-one years that is the only time anybody has ever come back and asked me anything."

He looked down the column. Granary weights. A pump part. A rate notice. A widow's name. A roof notice with a different stamp. And eleven months back, near the bottom of a month, four lines.

*Received 14th day, C.Y. 486. One folded sheet, being the entry and its two leaves, sealed. Brought by T. Sarr of this ward, in person, at the door. Said it was his own business and that it was for the ward's good. Not countersigned by the steward because he said it was not a ward act and did not need one.*

"Tal Sarr," Owen said.

"You know him."

"He signed it."

"He signed it." Bray took the ledger back and closed it with the flat of her hand. "Came to the door in the wet, a year ago, nearly a year, and gave me the sheet to look at, and I read it and I said to him, Tal, this moves the crossing out of our hands, and do you know what he said to me? He said the crossing is going to be looked after by somebody who can look after it, and we get the roof money. And I said, that is a ward act, and you cannot do a ward act for the ward, and he said it is not a ward act, Marguerite, it is a receipt, and a receipt is a thing a man signs for his own house."

"And you believed him."

"I was tired," said Marguerite Bray, "and I had four hundred weight of granary coming in that week, and he was the only man in the ward who could read the shape of the thing, and he had gone to the Office beforehand and had it stamped and witnessed and done, which at that point in this city's history was the entire available method." She squared the ledger. "So the ward received a receipt. Eleven months ago. That is what was delivered. Not a proposal. A receipt, entered as an acceptance, on the strength of one man with one household, and nobody ever put it to this hall, and I did not put it to this hall, because I was tired and because he told me it did not need one and because he was right, formally, on every point he made."

She said *he was right* the way a woman says a thing about a dead man that she will be saying for the rest of her life.

Owen sat down on one of the mismatched chairs.

"Where is he?"

"He drowned in the spring," she said. "At the second lock, in March, with the water up. He went in after a boy. They got the boy out." She stopped. "So you can take that face off. I have watched you build it for two days. He was sixty-one and he was a fool and he was frightened about the roof, and he was alive, and he signed it, and the Office took it, and it was good."

"It was good," Owen said.

"Say the rest of it."

"It was validly made." He said it to the table, because it was easier to the table. "It was signed, witnessed, sealed, and received while he was alive and the Office received it then. A receipt does not require its signer to keep breathing. His authority covered his own household and nothing else, and it was used for the whole ward, and there is not one single thing in that file that is a lie."

The rain came on the roof.

"Somebody didn't lie," Ada said from the doorway. She had come in behind him and had been standing there. "That's worse. If somebody had lied I could take it to the hall and the hall would know who to be angry at, and then we'd be done. Somebody didn't lie. It's all correct. A man signed a thing about his own house and the Registry did exactly what it says on the tin, and now we have got a crossing that we keep and cannot pay for and cannot prove and cannot leave."

She came the rest of the way in and took her coat off over the back of a chair.

"And they were kind about it, too," she said. "Two officers came in the autumn before, the autumn the receipt went in, and they sat in this hall and they asked what we did about the crossing and they wrote it down. Both of them. Nobody had ever come here and asked us that. They wrote it down and they thanked us and they went out, and eleven months later the crossing's gone and we're not to blame, because they came and they asked and we told them, and they were only ever writing it down."

---

Owen took out the notebook and opened it on the table beside her ledger, and for the first time in a week he wrote a thing down that was not going into an Office book at all, and then he tore a page out and put it on top of the Form 9-C that was in his coat and said, "I want to read you what I am about to do, because I am about to do it deliberately, and I would like it in a second person's ear."

They both looked at him.

"My inquiry alleges error. I proved an error. The annex schedule is dated two days before the earliest day any part of it could take effect, and that is provable in a morning and it is unanswerable and it is the only thing in the entire file that anybody in that building can put right." He kept his finger on the page. "I am withdrawing it. From my own filing. Tonight. In writing, on the record, with my name on it, because the moment that Office can correct a date they will correct a date, and then my case is a clerical matter, and a clerical matter gets a memorandum, and the memorandum will say the schedule was amended, and the three duties will still be in Mill Ward, and the thirteen names will still be on the second leaf, and I will have spent a year getting a date changed by two days in a month nobody will look at again."

The stove ticked. Outside, the pump house dripped.

"Those two days are also the only thing I have," Ada said. "Are you telling me you're giving them up."

"I am telling you I'm giving them up *in this filing*. They're in my own book. They stay in my own book. And when I put the schedule down, the three requests that were refused inside those two days go down with it, and I will not be able to find out what they were about, and that's the price, and I'm paying it." He looked up. "Because the error is the cheap part. It has a floor under it — a person can be put back, the clerk said so himself. And what I actually need to say is that a duty was validly received *for* this ward without this ward ever being asked, and that no instrument anywhere in that building lets you take a receipt back off a man because he was only ever entitled to give you his own house."

Ada Rusk stood very still with her arms folded and considered him, and her sister and mother and the whole of what he was doing passed across her face in about a second and a half.

"Then write it down as that," she said.

He wrote it down as that.

A continuance inquiry may be amended by its own filer while it is open, and the amendment goes on the same docket above the original entry, and nobody is struck from the file and nothing is erased; that is the only mercy in the procedure and Owen had never been grateful for it until that night. So at ten o'clock on the sixth day, in the plain words Nell Arbour had taught him, with two witnesses who had no reason to help him, he drew a line through the words *error in the effective date* in his own allegation and wrote underneath:  *the entry is validly made. The complaint is of its scope. A duty has been received for a district which did not give it, on the authority of one household, and is claimed to have been performed by a return which has not performed it.*

And then, because he could not help himself and because it was the only honest thing left in the room, he read the amendment out loud to the hall, and Bray wrote the date of it in the delivery book, and the first thing his sister said to him afterwards, in the doorway, in the rain, was not about the case at all.

"You've gone quiet," she said.

"I've gone careful."

"You've gone quiet and you've started a list. I can see the list in your coat." She looked at him. "You drafted the letter. No. You didn't. You *copied* the letter. You were on the counter the day it went out, all right? And when we find out later that it was you—"

"I know."

"I want to know now whether you can live with that."

"Yes," Owen said.

"Twelve people," Ada said, "have been telling the truth to that Office for nine years and the Office has been writing it down. Tal Sarr told the truth. That's the whole thing that happened here, in the end. Nine years of people telling the truth and one day the truth turned out to be a door out."

---

"Ivy Sarr is twelve," Marguerite said, from the table, when the hall had emptied.

Owen stopped.

"You never read the second leaf out to me," she said. "You read the dates. I have read the whole thing twice because I have nothing else to do, and I have a list of the thirteen in my head, and one of them is a child who was Tal Sarr's granddaughter and whose aunt keeps a stall two doors from mine." She had not moved from the chair. "Six of the thirteen can't answer for themselves. Six. Three of them are children and three are people who can't be got to a counter. There is no form in that building for them — the deputy registrar told you that himself in his own mouth, in his own office, and he told you the day you walked in there, which means he has told you because he wants somebody else to hold that particular thing."

"I know," Owen said.

"You don't. You know the words." She put her hand on the delivery book. "A child cannot be carried in a household return. That is the rule and the rule is right, because a household return is adults deciding about themselves and a child is not an adult deciding about anything. So a child needs something else, and the something else needs two people who are not related to the child and are not living in the same house, and somebody doing something for the child this week, and a road to go down if somebody wants to complain. Four things. I have got one of them, which is me, and I have got no route and no second witness who does not live with the first, and no act, because the aunt has the child and I have a ledger."

She closed the book.

"You can have the ward's duty. You can have your crossing and your dates and your two days. The six are not yours. The six are a thing this city has decided not to have, and if you win your crossing in March you will still not have the six, and somebody very like me will still be sixty-one and still be the only person in the ward who has written any of it down."

---

He walked back downriver at midnight in weather that had stopped being rain and started being a warning. He had a district return's terms in his notebook and a scope amendment on a docket and a two-day defect he had given away on purpose, and he had a thing he had not admitted to anybody including himself, which was that giving away the two days had made the case better.

Not easier. Better. Because as long as there was a date to correct, he was a man with a clerical grievance, and every clerk in the building could help him with a clerical grievance. The moment he took the date away, he was a man saying that a valid instrument had been used past the edge of what it could carry, and there was no memorandum in the world that answers that, and the Office would have to answer it with the ward or not at all.

He was frightened, and he was also, for the first time since he was twenty-one, doing the correct thing, and the two of those facts sat in him side by side and he could not make one of them smaller.

Then the last thing that happened that night happened in the doorway of his own house, and it was a question from his sister, who had walked down with him because she had a wrench to return to a shed near his.

"What was the two days worth," she said. "Mrs Bray's going to ask me, because Mrs Bray asks everybody everything, and I would like to be able to say it."

Owen stood on his own step with the rain coming off the gutter onto the back of his neck.

"Two days," he said, "and three requests that somebody refused inside them, and I do not know what the three requests were about, and now I am not going to find out."

Ada Rusk looked at him in the dark for a while.

"That is a terrible trade," she said.

"Yes."

"I'm not saying you were wrong."

"No," Owen said. "Neither am I. That's the part I have not got used to yet."
