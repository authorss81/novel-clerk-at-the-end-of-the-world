# Chapter 13 — Saturday

On the Friday Owen drafted three separate attacks on an answer he had never read, in a notebook, between seven and nine in the morning, and all three of them were good, and one of them was genuinely unanswerable, and at twenty past nine Junia Pell came round the end of the second desk and said nine words and went back to her tray.

"An answer that gave nothing would be appealable," she said, "and this one cannot be appealed, because nobody can say it is wrong."

He had put the three drafts in his notebook and written *Pell, Friday, nine-twenty: check this* beside the best of them, and then he had not checked it, and the not-checking was the only thing he was proud of in the whole of that Friday.

What he did instead was go and find out whether there was anything in the answer's *making* that was free to object to, and there was, and it was four lines, and it went in at the Glass Stair counter at half past ten in the morning, and it is the form a docket clerk fills in when a document is presented to him in a shape he is not satisfied with, and it is free, and there is no office in Docketford that is more scrupulous about the fact that it is free than the Glass Stair.

*Petition 487-GS-19, Lower Lock ward, answer of the district office. The answer is in a standard form used for petitions of this class. The petitioner submits that the form is inadequate to the petition, in that it does not answer the question asked. Petitioner asks that the answer be reconsidered and answered in the district office's own words.*

The clerk on duty was the man from the ninth day of the month, the one in his sixties with the manner of a person who has learned that everything said to him at that counter is a thing somebody will be sorry about. He read it twice and then asked, with genuine interest, whether Owen had anything against the answer.

"I have nothing against the answer. I have never seen it."

"Then you are objecting to a shape."

"Yes."

"Then I will sustain it," said the clerk, "because it is the correct form and because a thing that is correct and free ought to be used, and because in nine years nobody in this city has used it, and I have begun to think that is on purpose." He stamped it. "It goes back. You will get an answer in the ordinary way and it will be a different answer, and I want you to understand that you have done something to it, and that you will not be thanked for the something."

"I know what I have done to it."

"Do you," said the clerk. "Because you have not. You have given a district clerk one more day, and in that day he will write four more paragraphs, and he will write them himself instead of out of a drawer, and the four paragraphs will be his, and a man writes differently about a thing he has had to think about than about a thing he has copied, and you will not be able to tell where the improvement came from." He put the objection in the docket. "Go up and do something else for a day."

Owen went up and did something else for a day. He did not attack the answer. He had wanted to, from about eight o'clock, with a clarity that frightened him, and he had three drafts of it in a notebook and a marginal note saying *check this*, and he had written the marginal note and then obeyed it, and obeying it was the entire moral content of his Friday.

---

It came down on the Saturday at twenty past eleven, and it was longer than the first one, and it was in the district office's own words.

The counters open on a Saturday for matters with a date on them, and the copying floor does not run, and Owen was in an empty hall with a cold press and his hand in a glove because the cut had gone yellow at the edges and he had decided that a glove was cheaper than an explanation. Marguerite Bray came in at eleven, in her own coat, without asking, having walked twenty minutes in the rain with a ledger under her arm, which she has done four times in her life, all of them for this.

The Glass Stair clerk read the clause that refused a receipt into plain words for her, because she asked him to and because he said, before she asked, that he would.

"Madam. Read that one with me. *The receipt of the ward and of the witnesses named by the ward is a receipt and is not a hearing, and no person is admitted by it and no person is bound by it.* That means the Deputy Registrar will see you on the day named and see your witnesses, and it will be a meeting, and there will be a clerk taking what is said, and it will go into a file, and at the end of it he will not have to decide anything, and you will go home, and if the file says he has granted nothing, then nothing was granted." He put his hand flat on the counter. "And it is the fourth clause of a document that could have said it in one line, and I have written every answer that has gone out of that office for six years, and I am telling you that the four lines are the ones that were left in."

"Thank you," said Marguerite Bray. "I have four questions I would have put to the ward if I had known."

"You still may. It is not a hearing and it is also not a wall. It is a date with a clerk in it."

Owen read the whole answer in the hall, standing, with his good hand flat on the back of a chair. He read it once for the substance and once for the shape, because he could not help it and because the shape was where the work was.

It was in four parts, and every part of it was correct.

**One.** The district office acknowledges the receipt of District Return 487-LL, entered this Office on the fifteenth, of Lower Lock ward, comprising nine separately consenting adult branches, one person to a page, with a recorded dissent of four and an express right of withdrawal in the branch and not in the return.

**Two.** The district office has reviewed the return and accepts it **for review**. It records that *accepted for review* is not a binding continuation of any status and confers no right in respect of the safety, condition or use of the named work, and that the Office's acceptance is subject to the question of the separability of the observers, which is not determined by this answer and is not waived by it.

**Three.** The district office has considered the petition and the documents filed with it, and notes that the load-restriction suspension of the tenth of the month, which closes the Ninth Weir public crossing to loaded traffic and to vehicles above four tons, is not mentioned in, and is not affected by, this answer.

**Four.** The Deputy Registrar will receive the steward of Lower Lock ward, the ward's witnesses, and such persons as the ward may name, on a day in the last week of the rain season to be named, at the district office, on an hour to be named.

Owen stopped reading at the end of that and stood there.

"Go on," said Marguerite Bray.

"That is the whole of it. Four paragraphs. Four hundred and eleven words." He put the sheet down. "There is no fifth paragraph."

"There is a good deal in it," said the clerk, "for a man who came in here with a form."

"He came in here with a form," said Owen, "because I came in here on Wednesday and told a clerk in plain words that the form was inadequate, and she stamped it, and it went up, and somebody in the district office spent a day of his own on it instead of opening a drawer. That is why there are four paragraphs instead of one." He said it to the room. "I want that written somewhere, in this building's memory and mine. I made the answer longer. I made it *better*. And it is worse, and I want everybody in this room to understand that both of those are true at once and that I did it anyway, because the one it replaced would have been shorter and I could not have got a date out of it."

Nobody said anything. Marguerite Bray picked up the sheet and read the fourth paragraph again, twice.

"Say that last one to me slowly," she said.

"Read it yourself, madam."

"I have read it four times. I want to hear whether you are frightened of it, because you have a way of going very quiet when you are not."

Owen had the notebook out by then, and he copied all four hundred and eleven words into it in his own square hand, with the date at the top, and the docket number, and the stamp. And then he came to the bottom of the last paragraph and stopped and did not write anything next to it, and shut the book, and the not-writing was the hardest single act of his month.

Because here was the thing, and he had worked it out on the Friday and had not been able to say it to anybody until this room.

"A day in the last week of the rain season," he said. "To be named. That is not a date, madam. That is a *category* of date. There is no instrument in this city that fixes the end of the rain season. The engineer's finding says what the river did on the ninth. The roll says the fourteenth. Nothing in Docketford says when the season stops, and if you ask a clerk at that counter he will tell you when the rain stops, and he will tell you it was the year before last, and he will be right, and that will be the number you get."

"So the day is to be fixed by the weather."

"The day is to be fixed by whoever is sitting in that office on the day the rain stops, and he will fix it, and he will not fix it in advance, and if you turn up on the wrong day you will be told the day has not come." He put his hand flat on the counter, on the same counter the clerk had put his hand flat on. "And that is worse than a refusal, and I want to be exact about why, because I think I have it and I want it tested. A refusal has a date on it. A refusal says no on the eighteenth of this month, and if you disagree with the eighteenth of this month you can say *your date is wrong*, and a date can be wrong. A date nobody can compute cannot be wrong. You cannot miss it, because it cannot be set in a place you failed to reach. You cannot complain of it, because nobody agreed to it. And you cannot appeal it, because there is nothing in it to appeal against — it grants nothing, it refuses nothing, it simply says that one day in a period nobody has defined will be named later by a man you have not met."

The Glass Stair clerk had stopped pretending to work.

"That is the most accurate description of a date I have heard at this counter," he said, "and I have been at this counter for six years, and I would like you to know that I am not permitted to agree with it and that I have not."

"I know," said Owen. "I am not asking you to agree. I am asking you to have heard it."

He read out the third paragraph again then, because it was the one nobody in the room had looked at, and it was nine words long and it was the only sentence in four hundred and eleven words that anybody would remember in a year.

"It does not mention the load restriction," he said. "It has the number of the return in it and the date of the return and the docket of the petition and the name of the crossing in paragraphs one, two and four, and in paragraph three it records that the Office has considered the load-restriction suspension of the tenth and that it is not affected. That is a sentence about a bridge in a document about a ward, and it is a sentence that costs the Office nothing, and it is the only sentence in four hundred and eleven words that anybody will remember."

"What does it mean?" said Marguerite.

"It means the Office has declined to say that the crossing is unsafe," Owen said, "and it has declined to say that the crossing is safe, and both of those are refusals to make a finding, and the reason it has declined both is that the first one is a finding and the second one is not. The first would put the ward's claim at risk and give somebody a document. The second would be a lie. So the Office has put both of them outside the answer, and what is left is a ward and a date." He shut the notebook. "It is the most correct piece of paper anybody has written about us in a month and I would like somebody in this room to be angry about it, because I have run out of ways to be angry about it myself."

They looked at him.

"Right," said Marguerite Bray, and picked up her ledger, and said the thing that ended it. "I came here for an answer and I have got one, and it is a piece of paper, and a piece of paper is a thing you can carry upriver, and I am going to carry it upriver. Owen Rusk, you will come to the hall tonight and read it out in your own voice, all of it, including the sentence that is a date and not a hearing, and I am not going to read it and neither is Ada, because the moment this ward hears it from one of its own it will be a thing it has been told and the moment it hears it from a Registry clerk it will be a thing the Registry has said about it. Ninety minutes. Can you read for ninety minutes?"

"I can read for ninety minutes."

"You can read for ten and then you will start to explain it, and the explaining is where you lose them." She put her hood up. "Ninety minutes of reading. And then go home, and do not come back up the hill afterwards, because I know what you are like and I have watched you walk back down a river in the rain to work out what you have done, and you have done enough for one week."

---

The ward hall at half past six on a wet Saturday had twenty-eight people in it, which was a good number for a Saturday, and Owen Rusk stood at the end of the long table and read four hundred and eleven words out loud at the pace of a man reading a menu.

He read it once, all of it. He read it a second time because Marguerite had asked for ninety minutes and there was only nine minutes of paper in it. He answered questions for the rest of the hour, standing, and answered them badly, and Ada corrected him four times, and each time he was glad to be corrected and each time he noticed that he was glad to be corrected.

He got to the fourth paragraph eleven times. He did not get tired of it. He said it out loud in a hall in Lower Lock eleven times, in a good clear voice, with the date in the last week of the rain season in it, and by the ninth telling he had the words so exactly that he could hear them without hearing them, and by the eleventh he was hearing something else underneath them, which was the sound a document makes in a room where twenty-eight people have understood at the same moment that they are not going to be told anything for a week.

Then Ada Rusk put her hand on the table.

"Who wrote it?"

"I don't know," said Owen.

"You are a clerk in that building."

"I am a clerk at the tray of unfiled correspondence and I saw the answer for the first time at eleven o'clock this morning at the counter of the Glass Stair, and before eleven o'clock this morning what I knew about that answer was that it was being written out of a form." He put the notebook down on the table where anybody could see it. "I have got the name of the form in here. I have not got the name of the man, and I have not gone to look for it, and I am not going to, because the moment I go looking for a man's name in a building like that, the man hears about it, and then the next document that comes down that week is written by a man who knows I am looking."

"So he is protected."

"Yes. By me. And I would like somebody in this room to understand that I have been protected by a procedure for a month and I have not once said so out loud, and I have been aware of it every single day and I have let it stand, because it is the procedure and because it is the only thing that has kept me in a building where I can be handed the next thing." He shut the notebook. "That is the honest answer and I am not proud of it, and the alternative was to lie to you, and I am not going to do that either, and both of those are the same man, and that is the whole of what I can offer you tonight."

Nobody in the hall spoke for a while. The stove ticked. Somebody at the back said, at some point, *well*, and it was not agreement and it was not anger, and it was the noise a room makes when it has been handed a thing it cannot be angry at and cannot be grateful for.

Marguerite Bray wrote the date in the delivery book. It was the first thing she had written in it in a fortnight, and she wrote it the way she wrote everything, in pencil, in her own hand, with the day and the weather, and she did not say anything to Owen at all, which was how she said it.
