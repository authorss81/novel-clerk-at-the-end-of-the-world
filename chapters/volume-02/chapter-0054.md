# Chapter 54 — The Board at Cutter's Fields

Cutter's Fields is a flat piece of Docketford behind the tanneries, about nine acres, and for about four months of the year it smells of the tanneries and for the other eight it smells of the river, and it is the only ground in this city where a person can stand and see the water gate at the upriver end from where they are standing.

On the Monday morning of the seventy-seventh day of the month there were forty-one sheets of paper on a board.

Not a queue. He looked for the queue first, the way you look for a queue, because the last thing that had been nailed up in this city in public was a comparison sheet with a queue in front of it and a form on a table beside it that granted nothing, and he had spent four minutes of his life in the eighth month of the year explaining to a hall what that was.

There was no queue. There were nine men and two women putting up a canvas wall on the west side of the field with a rope and eight stakes, and there was a trestle table under the slope of the tanneries with a ledger on it the size of a door, and there was a wooden case about the size of a bread crock sitting on the table with a hasp on it, and there was a board about seven feet by four made of three planks nailed to a frame, and on the board there were two printed sheets and one shorter one and eleven lines of names.

Owen Rusk stood at about two paces from it for about four minutes and read it.

At the top, in the ordinary hand of a printing shop:

**SCHEDULE OF SETTLEMENTS TO BE RECONCILED. DOCKETFORD DISTRICT. Posted for notice, in advance of the opening of the season, under the rule at item nine of the district office's Schedule of Instruments in Force. Persons who consider that a settlement, a work or a household of theirs has been wrongly included or wrongly omitted are invited to make enquiry of the count room at the Glass Stair at any hour the room is open.**

And then, because a notice that names nobody is a notice nobody can answer:

**Season of the Concord Year 488. Eleven settlements. The Count of the Concord Year 487 was suspended in the month of rain and was not taken. This schedule is made of the settlements from which no return of premises was received for the Count of the Concord Year 487, together with the settlements returned late, and the year of the last reconciliation is given against each.**

Three columns. Name. Year of last reconciliation. Premises of record.

And eleven rows.

He read the third column first, because he is a man who reads columns, and it took him about ninety seconds, and by the end of the second minute he had stopped being a man reading a column and become a man standing in a field with a thing in his hands.

**Nine.** **Four.** **Six.** **Eleven.** **Two.** **Nine.** **Seven.** **Three.** **Eleven.** **Nine.** **Six.**

Eleven rows, eleven figures, and the figures are the district's figures, and the district's figures are the figures the district office has carried since the Count of the Concord Year 486, and nobody has been to any of these places since then, and there are two rows on that board where the year of the last reconciliation is **479**, which is nine years ago, and there is one row that says **481**, and those are the three that a warden would go to first, and he marked them without meaning to.

The fourth row was **Lower Lock, 486, eleven.**

Eleven. That is the number of premises of record the county carries for Lower Lock, and it is the correct number, and it is the number on the Return of Premises that came down to the ward on Thursday, and it is the number Ada Rusk filled in on the Friday in a slate hand with two households in one building and one household that is a pitch and has no door.

Owen Rusk stood at about two paces from a board in a field behind the tanneries at about a quarter past nine in the morning on the seventy-seventh day of a season that does not open for eleven days, and read the fact that the largest settlement in his own life was on a list of eleven places that did not answer, and that nobody in the seven weeks of the case had ever thought to write a sentence about it, because the case had been about whether the ward was a place and the answer had turned out to be yes.

---

The tent went up along the rope at about half past ten. It is a wall of canvas on eight stakes and it took four men about forty minutes, and when it was up it was a room, and there was a trestle table inside it and two stools and a lamp and a board on a nail and a **stone** in a wooden case on the table, and the stone is a hand's breadth across and flat on one face and rounded on the other and it is the same stone that has been used for every reconciliation in this county since the Census Guard was constituted in the Concord Year 351, and it has been pressed on more instruments in this county than there are houses in Docketford, and it is not a special stone and nobody chose it.

The man who put it on the table was about sixty and he did not say anything for about ten minutes, and then he said, to nobody, in a voice with a flat end on it:

"Two settlements this morning and the district opens in eleven days, and if it does not rain."

A woman of about thirty came out of the tent at about a quarter to eleven and read the board from about nine feet off without coming nearer, which is a thing field people do, and she wrote four figures on a slate in about twenty seconds, and Owen Rusk watched her do it and thought: *she is not reading the names. She is reading the third column and she already knows the eleven.*

Then she looked up.

"You are the officer of the record of the district returns," she said. "At the Glass Stair. On the ground floor, with the book on the table anybody may read."

"Yes."

"Then you will be able to tell me whether a county figure is a figure about a settlement or a figure about a settlement's premises."

It is the fourth gate of a form and there are four columns and she had found the fourth gate in a fortnight. Owen Rusk had been in the building eight years.

"The column is headed *premises of record*," he said.

"Then it is a figure about premises and not about people, and it is eleven, and the county's roll for this district has thirteen household references in it, and I will tell you what I think you are going to find when you go and look, and then you may tell me I am wrong." She put the slate down on the trestle table. "There are two of your thirteen that are not a building, and one of them is a pitch, and a pitch cannot be on a column about premises, and a person standing in a field behind the tanneries can see that about a third of this county without leaving the ground."

"That is correct," said Owen Rusk.

"You did not go and look."

"I did not go and look," he said, "and I have been in this building eight years, and I did not go and look because for eight years that column was not a column anybody in this district had to answer to, and I want to be exact about that, because it is not a defence and it is the truth and the truth is worse."

"Then say the other half of it."

"The other half of it is that I did not go and look because for eight years **the whole column was about a thing that did not exist**, and there was no Count, and nobody was going to count anything, and the ward I live in was arguing about whether it was a place, and it won that, and it cost seven weeks and a flood and a woman's four words, and at about ten to seven on Monday evening I read a notice on a post that wanted premises out of me." He put his hand flat on nothing, which is a thing he has never done. "I would like to record that I am not proud of the order those two things happened in."

The woman picked the slate up.

"Three o'clock is the best time," she said. "There is a woman with a book at the hall at Wexford Landing who has not got a premises of record and has not had one since the year before last, and if I take a form out to her I will have to write something in the last column, and I would rather somebody who can read a form told me what the last column wants before I stand in front of her with a pen."

"What is your name," said Owen Rusk.

"It is not on the board," she said, "and the board is for the settlements and not for the staff, and if my name is on a form in a hall at Wexford Landing this season it is because I put it there and not because a schedule put it there, and I should like it that way, and I have asked for that twice before in other districts and got it twice before."

---

Arbour Kitchen is in Mill Ward, which is the district that received the transfer receipt which took the crossing off Lower Lock, and the door is on a lane and there is a queue outside it most of the day, and Owen Rusk had been in that room three times in seventy days and had been wrong in it once.

She was not outside the door. She was inside, at the table by the wall, with a **count sheet** in front of her, and it was a page about four thousand years old in the way that all paper in this river valley is about four thousand years old, and it was the reconciliation of a settlement for the Concord Year 486, and it had eleven rows on it and it was the only record Owen Rusk had ever seen of what a place called **Ossery** was.

"How many families," he said.

"Twelve households and two people over the age of seventy in houses that are not households, which the sheet calls premises, because that is what it calls them."

"How many rows on the sheet."

"Eleven. And there is a twelfth house and it is not on the sheet and the man in it is seventy-nine and he has been there since before the counting was made a thing this county does, and I asked him in the spring and he said he has never once been asked and would not know the form if it came up the lane."

Owen Rusk sat down on the other side of the table, which nobody had offered him and which is how you are offered a thing in that room.

"Nell."

"Do not," said Nell Arbour.

"I have not asked you for anything."

"I know you have not," she said, "and that is the difference between this room and every other room you have been in since the spring, and I am not going to make a speech about it. What I need is a person who can read a count sheet quickly, because I have been reading this one for four days and I keep reading it as a story and it is not a story, it is a form, and there are eleven rows and nine columns and two of the columns are numbers of persons and one of them is a number of premises and **all three of the numbers were written in a different hand in February** and I cannot tell you which hand is which because one of them is the Warden's and the other one is a clerk's and I do not know the clerks in that office."

"May I look."

"You may look and you may not have it, and you may not have it because I am not going to have this sheet in that building, and you know exactly why I am not going to have this sheet in that building and it is not that I do not trust you."

"I do know," said Owen Rusk, and looked at it for about four minutes without touching it, and did not write anything down, which was the single hardest thing he did that day and the only one he told anybody about afterwards.

"Right," said Nell Arbour. "The two of the numbers. Persons and premises. In February. The premises column is the county's figure and the county's figure is what they counted in 486 and they have not been back. And the persons column is not nine and it is not eleven and it is **twenty-six** and it has been twenty-six for three seasons and the last two seasons the number of persons at Ossery has been twenty-nine and twenty-eight and the number of premises has been eleven both times, and the sheet does not know that, and the sheet cannot know that, and the sheet is going into a Count on the first of the month."

"And the twenty-six is a figure about people who can be asked."

"The twenty-six is a figure about people who can be asked," said Nell Arbour, "and the eleven is a figure about buildings, and there is a column in that form for the three children at Ossery and there is a column for the two men over seventy, and every one of those five is a person who cannot be asked, and the form says *enter none* if there is no such person."

"There is a person," said Owen Rusk. "There is a person for two of them, at least. The lime kiln is worked by a family and the family feeds the two old men and has done for longer than the sheet has been a sheet."

"And the county's form has a column for who is keeping them," said Nell Arbour, "and I know what that column is, because I have been in this business nineteen years and I have never once been able to fill it in, and neither can you, and the reason neither of us can fill it in is that a person who keeps an old man is not a person who is keeping the old man. He is feeding him. Those are not the same and the form does not know they are not the same and the form is not lying, and the form is going to do a number of arithmetic in February that has a woman of eighty-one in it."

She put her hand flat on the sheet.

"Seven days ago I had a case I could win," she said. "A man of nineteen in Coal Hill whose family has been at a premises of record for four generations and whose premises went on a list in the spring for a drawing error and lost a year. I have the act, I have the family, I have four people who will stand up and say what they saw. And I have been sitting here for four days with a sheet from a different district, because somebody decided which eleven places on this river are going to be looked at this season, and it was not the twelve households at Ossery who decided, and it was not me, and it was not the field, and I want to know on what."

Owen Rusk did not say anything for about four seconds, and then said, carefully, and to himself as much as to her:

"That is the second time this week somebody has said a sentence to me that I have not got a box for."

"Take the box," said Nell Arbour. "I have a claimant household at Ossery who are in the third week of a fourteen-day suspension that began before I had heard of it, and I have a woman at Wexford Landing whose premises of record went out in the spring and who has never once been asked about it, and I have neither of them because I cannot get in front of a season that has already decided where it is going to look. Take the box, and come up the river on Sunday, and read me a form."

"Eleven days is the notice," he said.

"Then come on the eighth day and be early," said Nell Arbour, "and do not come on my account. Come on the account of the people in the third week of a suspension, because a suspension is fourteen days and it does not wait for a notice, and I have been sitting here for four days and I have only just worked out that it does not wait for a notice."

She did not offer him anything else, and she did not say anything else, and at about one o'clock he got up off the bench on the other side of the table, and at the door he stopped and said the thing he had come near once at the end of a long evening in the last month of the previous season and had not said then.

"You are working," he said. "And I am asking you for a skill and not for anything else, and I noticed that you noticed the difference, and I am not going to say anything about the difference."

"Neither am I," said Nell Arbour, and went back to the sheet.

---

He went back up the hill and across and down to the field at about four o'clock, because the man of sixty had said that at half past four the board gets its second sheet up, and Owen Rusk is a man who has been in this city his whole life.

There was a second sheet up. It was smaller than the first and it was in a different size of type and it said, at the head:

**QUOTA OF RECONCILIATIONS. DOCKETFORD DISTRICT. Eleven. Set at Concord Hall on the twenty-eighth day of the last month, and posted for notice, and the quota is a public figure and is posted whether or not the settlements on the schedule are known to the person who sets it.**

Eleven against eleven. A quota of eleven and a schedule of eleven and a season of eleven days, and the quota set on the twenty-eighth day of the month before, and Owen Rusk stood in a field behind the tanneries at about twenty to five in the afternoon on a Monday in the eighth month of the Concord Year 487 and read a public figure that had been made before eleven settlements had been told they were on a list, and worked out that this was not remarkable, and worked out that he did not know why it was or was not remarkable, and that not knowing was the part he was going to have to be careful about, because he had spent the summer learning that a thing he could not see was a thing somebody had chosen not to show him.

Then he read the foot of the first sheet, which he had read at two paces and had read again from nine feet and had not read at twenty feet, and it was one line, and it was in a smaller hand, and it was the line that was going to take him four batches to get to:

*The year of the last reconciliation is given against each settlement, and a settlement whose last reconciliation was not a reconciliation of premises may be reconciled of something else.*

Owen Rusk read it twice.

Then he went home and did the tray, and it took him until eight o'clock, and he was forty minutes late, and he did not mind.
