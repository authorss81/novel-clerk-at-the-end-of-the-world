# Chapter 65 — Three Holds, and a Fourth

The standing extract at Cutter's Fields has four hundred and six rows in it and it is renewed on the first working day of every season by a person in the count room at the Glass Stair, and on the Saturday morning of the twenty-sixth day of the month the woman who maintains the board took twenty minutes off it with a scraper and did it herself, and nobody had asked her to and she told Owen Rusk about it afterwards in the voice she uses for a price.

"There," she said. "**Four. Four.** And then a new line under it in the printing hand, because I do not have a printing hand and I got the man at the second counter to cut me a strip, and it says *season of 488: no figure entered, and no correction of 488 exists.* And I will tell you what the strip is, because you are going to stand there and work it out and I would rather hand it to you. It is not a column. It is four words and a stop. **I have been doing that board nine years and I have never taken anything off it and I did not know I was allowed to.**"

"You are allowed to. The determination is a determination and it is in the file."

"Yes," she said, "and the file is two floors up and the board is here, and I do not have a file, and I have a scraper." She put the scraper in the bucket with the nails in it. "Twenty minutes. Nine years. I have wanted to take something off that board since the second week of the month and I would not have done it on my own and I would not have done it for you either, because you are an officer and officers ask, and that is not the same."

---

Elda Varn was at the frame at about a quarter to eleven on the Saturday morning and she had come down from the Wexford Landing on the Friday's market road in a cart, which takes most of a day and which she had not told anybody about, and she stood in front of the smaller board for about a quarter of an hour with her hands behind her back in the way of a person reading a price.

Owen Rusk stood about six feet off and let her, which is the only thing he has ever been good at and which he is not good at.

"There it is," she said at last. "Four and four. And a year." She put her finger on the year without touching the paper. "**That is the first good thing anybody in this county has done for me in four years and it is a number on a board and it is the number of the people who were on the slip and it is correct and it is a number of four and I am one of them and I am seventy-nine and there are no premises on that board with my name against any of them and there never have been.**"

"I know."

"You will not know, and I am not being sharp, I am being seventy-nine and it is half past eleven and I have come round the flat above the weirs in a cart." She took her hands from behind her back. "I want to say one thing and then I want you to take me back up the hill, because I have not been asked anything for four years and I have had four things in about a week and I have not finished any of them and I am not a person who enjoys that."

"Say it."

"**You have made me the reason and I am not the reason.**" She said it quite flatly, without any weight on it at all, which made it heavier. "In nine years somebody is going to write down that a correction was overturned in this district, and the season of 488 will be the one where it happened, and it will be a good thing in a book, and there will be a person in the story and she will be a woman of seventy-nine who kept a book at a landing and asked for a figure. And I will be the person who asked for a figure, because you put it in a room, and I did not ask for a figure. **I asked for eleven. The figure is four and it was always four and it was four before you came and it will be four in a hundred years, and it is the only thing anybody has ever put in a column about that reach, and it is the wrong one.**"

Owen Rusk stood in a field behind the tanneries in the sun and found that he had no sentence at all, which had happened to him four times in eleven weeks and never once in a room.

"I am not going to argue with you about it," he said, "because you are right and I wrote it down on Thursday in front of about thirty people, and I said the column can be printed and cannot be filled, and you are standing in front of me telling me the same thing in a different hat and it took you four years to get to it and it took me eleven weeks and I did it in a field and you did it in a chair."

"It is the same thing in the same hat," said Elda Varn, "and I would like it noted that you have not offered me anything in a week and that everybody else in this county has offered me something in four years, and I would have taken the cup of tea, and I have not said no to the tea because I was proud, I have said no to the tea because I was seventy-five and somebody had come to my door with a stone in a case and I have not got over it and I am not going to pretend to have got over it for the sake of a column."

Then she asked him for it, which she had said on Saturday in a hall she would not do.

"Not a hold," she said. "I am not going to be a woman who is owed a hold. **A name.** Some person in this city that I can walk to and say the thing to, and who is answerable for hearing it, and who cannot send it to somebody else. I have had a stone in a case at my door twice and a woman with a slate and a man with a folder and a man from the district office with a good reason why he cannot come, and I have not got anybody, and I am seventy-nine and I do not need anybody to fix a thing. I need somebody to have to hear it."

"The card is three," said Owen Rusk. "And I have three, and one of them is a girl of twelve in Fetter Lane who cannot be asked, and one is a woman at Witherow who asked for a no and is going to get it on the thirtieth, and one is a man at Quill Bank who asked for a person in a room and I gave him a reason why he could not have one, and the reason was good, and it is my third hold and it has cost him a thing this week that I have not been able to look at yet."

"Then I have not asked for it," said Elda Varn, "and you have told me and I am not going to argue about it, and you may go and be sick about it, and I am going to stand here for another ten minutes and then I am going up the hill because I have not been in a hall in Fetter Lane since I was seventy-five and I would like to be in a room in my own lifetime where nobody asks me anything."

She did stand there for another ten minutes. He let her.

Up the hill on the Sunday morning of the twenty-seventh day of the month there were about thirty people in the ward hall and the stove was on and Marguerite Bray had done an unscheduled count of the granary bins at about seven in the morning and had ruled the figure in the delivery book with the day and the hour, in her own hand, and under it she had written a second line, which is the thing she has done four times in a season and not once before that.

**Nine days. Two of the bins are down to the last half-breadth and I have been writing the write-up and not counting since the fourth of this month, and the figure of a fortnight on the twelfth was a write-up and not a count, and I am striking it, and the last honest count of this granary was on the fourth day of the month and there was nothing in it.**

Cobb Rennie asked whether the four tons the mill's factor had put in were in that, and Marguerite Bray said they were, and he said how long, and she said **nine days, as of this morning, and it is a fortnight and three days less than it was on the twelfth, and I have been writing it up and not counting, and the only thing that has come into that granary since the fourth of the month is air.**

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Ada Rusk said, from the wall, without turning round, "Then it goes in the book that we are going to be hungry in the second week of the next month, and it goes in the book tonight and not on the day, and nobody in this hall is to buy anything and anybody who brings us something we cannot eat is to be told so at the door."

"What do you want us to do," said a man at the back, "vote about it?"

"Watch the bins," said Ada Rusk. "That is all four lines of it and I have been doing it since I was nineteen. Wil watches the bins on the Thursday and writes it up and Ruth writes the copy at the weir path and the copy is what the count could read if the count ever wanted to read anything. And a bin is not a column and a bin is not an instrument and a bin is the only thing in this ward that nobody has ever struck off anything."

Owen Rusk read the ward's four terms out at about eleven, from the top of the card, because he reads things from the top, and the room let him, and he read the second one twice and the room let him read it twice, and Ada Rusk did not look at him once while he was doing it, which is the correct way for a sister to behave in a hall.

Then he told them about the hearing, and he told it the way he has learned to tell things in this volume, in the ordinary voice, with the shape of the thing before the feeling of it, and he was about four minutes and he was not proud of a word of it.

"Then the second ground is not argued," he said, "and the third is not argued, and I put on a printed form in a public room that I had struck two of my own grounds out and written under them that I was ashamed of them, and it took four minutes to read out and the room was quiet. And the first thing in this season that anybody has ever had a form say out loud in a room is that **I do not want to argue this.** And a man from the count stood up in about the fourth minute and asked whether a good part could be said, and the warden said no, and the reason she gave is that a good part in a room becomes the test and a test has to live somewhere, and if it lives in a warden then in four years a different warden will be asked about a wall and she will be twenty-nine and certain of herself in a way Kade was in 484, and nobody in the building will be able to say no to her, because the good part was in the room in 491.

"And nobody defended her," he said. "Not the count, not the district office, and not me, and I want it ruled that I had my hand up and I put it down and the officer asked me twice and I said no both times, and I have not told that hall why, and I am going to tell her now and then I am not going to say it again."

"Out with it," said Marguerite Bray.

"Because a defence is a gift to the office," said Owen Rusk. "It says a deletion may be defended, and so it may be made again by anybody in the same kind of room, and it does not have to be right. She refused it before I got there. If I had got there first it would be in a record of mine in four minutes, and I have had one finding on a card that this office has called thin and I wrote *thin* about it myself, and I am not walking into a second one carrying a sentence about a wall."

Marguerite Bray ruled that down in two lines and read it back and Ada Rusk said, "Correct," which from her is a decoration.

The review came up on the Monday morning of the twenty-eighth, on the tray, in an envelope with a docket on it, and it was the first thing that had come to the second desk in nine days that he had not known was coming, and the envelope had Mavis Rudge's name on it twice, once in her own hand and once in a hand at a counter, and it was **set for the thirtieth day of the month at ten in the morning, before an officer of the count having no district in the matter, at the count room at the Glass Stair, in the long room with the high windows.**

"There is no room," said the counting clerk at the second counter, before he had asked.

"There is a room. The determination is on the file and the long room is free on the thirtieth and I can have it for you at ten."

"The room is free at ten and it is not free at ten **for this**." She put her hand on the docket. "That is a review of a reconciliation of a settlement of three premises of record and eleven households, requested by a person of the district, heard by an officer of the count having no district in the matter. There is no room in this building for that. There is a public room with a rail round it and a public stand on it and about nine people can stand at the rail, and the officer will sit at the table and hear it in the open, and that is what a review of a three-premise settlement gets, and it is not anybody's contempt and it is what the rule provides."

"Then it happens at ten in the morning in a public room with a rail round it."

"**Then somebody must be found who will give her a room,**" said the counting clerk, "because the rule does not say she may have one and it does not say she may not, and the only body in this county that could provide one is the Registry, and I have asked, and the answer came back on the Friday in a form, and the form says that accommodation for a hearing is not a Registry function, and the form is correct, and the form is in the same book as everything else that is correct and that nobody has ever looked at."

"She will be in a kitchen, then," said Owen Rusk.

"She will be wherever somebody who wants a person heard puts her," said the counting clerk, "and I would put her in a kitchen and I would not put her in a kitchen, and the difference between those is about four hundred people in this city and not one of them is a room."

Nell Arbour had eleven families on her list and one of them was at Witherow and had not come to her for two years because Mavis Rudge did not go to a kitchen where there was nothing to be done in a kitchen, and on the Monday afternoon of the twenty-eighth of the month a woman of about fifty-four came up the hill to Fetter Lane and asked for the office of the Continuance Examiner of the Count, and Owen Rusk was in the hall and she asked for him and he said the second desk and she said she knew, and he took her down on the Tuesday morning of the twenty-ninth because the Tuesday is the day the Arbour Kitchen is quiet and he had not asked her and she had offered in about four words through a man of about twenty-five who takes a boat.

She read his file before he got there. That is the standing of a claimant advocate and it is not a courtesy; she reads the file or she does not take the case, and she had read it, and when he came in at about half past ten on the Tuesday she had his review, his applicant's card, the determination of Thursday, and the form about accommodation, all on a table, in that order, and she had underlined two things in his applicant's card with a pencil of her own.

"Read the whole of that out loud," she said, "the part about your four grounds."

He read it out. It took about a minute and a half and she did not interrupt.

"Right," said Nell Arbour. "**You are going to be asked inside nine minutes what your village is on that list for, and you have not decided now what you are going to say, and if you have not decided now you will decide in the room, and the thing you decide in the room is the thing that goes on the sheet.**"

"I have decided. I am going to say I cannot produce it and that I have not got a column for it and that I know that is a failure and not an answer."

"That is right," she said, "and I would have said something cleverer, and the cleverer thing would have been about you, and you are not going to need it." She put the card down. "Now sit down, because here is the whole of what I have got, and it is a kitchen."

She had already said no to him twice this season and had once given him a thing she was not asked for, and she said the next four sentences with a pencil in her hand and looking at the table.

"A kitchen is not a room. I have had four of these in this kitchen and three of them were a man and a woman in front of a table and about eleven people who came because they came. It is a room. It is the only room in this county that will have a hearing in it, because we do not have to be asked. **The officer has a right to refuse to sit in a kitchen and she will not, because she has been in this county once and the hot water is on the same pipe as the count room's and she will want to see the count room.** And the difference between a hearing at the count room with a rail round it and a hearing in a kitchen with a stove in it is that in a kitchen the people come. In Docketford with a rail, nine people would come and all nine would be clerks. In here, forty people will come and thirty-one of them will be from the mud flats at Witherow, and they will come because I will not send a word round, I will put it on the door of the mill and the mill has a yard that forty people stand in, and they will come because it is on a door and not in a letter."

"That is a good argument."

"It is not an argument, it is a kitchen," said Nell Arbour. "And here is the part I want you to hear and then we will not discuss it again this morning. **It is going to fail.** The officer is going to find that Witherow cannot show a current local act performed by the settlement, and it cannot, and I have been to that village twice and there is a woman there with a good hand and a bad leg and there is no act in any column that Witherow itself performs and records, and the file is going to say *review requested, heard, reconciliation sustained, no current local act shown*, with both your names on it, in a hand at a counter, in Concord Hall. And Mavis Rudge asked for that file. She asked for it out loud, in your office, in the second week of this month, and she said she had had thirty-one years of nobody asking her anything and she would like one document in which somebody asked her and the answer was no."

"She did."

"And she will get it," said Nell Arbour, "in a kitchen, on the thirtieth, in front of forty people, and she is not going to enjoy one minute of it, and she will not say so to me, and I am not going to ask her. And you are going to stand in a kitchen in your own card and be asked about a form you may not ask about, and you are not going to be able to help, and I have watched you in a room four times this season and every time you have been able to help, and I want you to know in advance that this is a room where you will not."

Junia Pell was in the corridor outside the second desk at about ten past twelve on the Tuesday of the twenty-ninth day of the month with a letter in her hand, and she had been in the building three months and four days and she was twenty-two years old and she was the fourth name on a roll of nineteen and she was still, by her own account, on it.

"This came to me on the Friday," she said. "From Concord Hall. It is a form and it is not addressed to me, it is addressed to the district office, and my name is on it because somebody in Concord Hall has decided that the officer who answers challenges in a district should be somebody who can be reached."

She put it on the corner of the desk. It is one sheet and it is headed **DISTRICT OFFICE, DOCKETFORD. OFFICER TO ANSWER CHALLENGES TO INSTRUMENTS OF THE COUNT, FOR THE SEASON.**

"It is a step up," said Junia Pell. "It is the fourth posting I have been offered in eleven months and it is the only one of the four that anybody in Concord Hall has written down that I am capable of. I have read it twice and I have not signed it and I am going to refuse it in one sentence and I would like you to be standing here so that you can hear the sentence and be disappointed by it in front of me."

"You do not have to be—"

"**I would be the person who tells a man in a field that his challenge is out of time,**" said Junia Pell. "I have done that on a counter in this building to about forty people since I was nineteen and I did it for a notice about a culvert and I was right and I was kind about it and there is a man on the flat above the weirs who got two of them last winter and did not sleep and has not mentioned it to anybody. And the whole of what you did on Thursday was a woman of seventy-nine standing up in a room and asking for a figure, and it is going to be talked about for a year, and in the fourth month somebody will be told that the officer who answers challenges in Docketford is a woman of twenty-two who was **refused three things in eleven months in this building and took none of them**, and I have not taken this one either and I am not going to take this one, and I would like the reason to be that I have not been the one that says no yet, and not that I am too good for it."

She picked the letter back up.

"Also they have addressed it to the district office and not to me, and that is the second reason and it is the one that decides it. **A person is appointed to a thing and not a building.** You know that. You have been writing it in the fourth line of a request form for a week."

Then she went down the corridor, and she did not explain it, and he did not ask her for anything else, and he stood at the end of the second desk for about two minutes afterwards and thought about the reason, and it was a better reason than the one she had said out loud, and she had not said it, and he was not going to ask her for it either.

---

He did the tray on the Tuesday evening of the twenty-ninth day of the month and it took him until about eight, and he was on it at about ten past seven, and at about the third tray of the evening he worked out the thing that the whole of the last nine days had been walking towards and that he had been refusing to look at, which is that **there are three holds on his card and there are four claimants**, and the fourth is a woman of seventy-nine at a landing who does not want a hold and wants a name, and a hold is the only instrument he has that gives a person a name to go to.

A hold may be given up by the holder at any time. That is on the card and it is the second sentence.

A hold may not be transferred to any other officer without the written agreement of the claimant. That is on the card and it is the last sentence and it is the only sentence in the Registry's instruments Owen Rusk has ever read that says the words *the written agreement of the claimant*, and it took eleven weeks and a flood to get a card in this city with a sentence like that on it.

He went through the three one at a time on the back of a sheet from a tide table, in pencil, in the order they were entered, and by the time he had got to the bottom of it it was about ten past nine at night and the sum was the same whichever way he added it up.

**One. Ivy Sarr, of two rooms in Fetter Lane, a person who cannot be asked.** He cannot ask her; there is nobody in that household who can be asked, and the form says so in his own words in a box for the claimant's own words, and the box says *no person in her household is to be asked to be named.* And he cannot ask Wenna Sarr, because Wenna Sarr is one of the four who voted against the transfer on the seventh day of the month of rain and the class of persons who may object to the ward's paper is **eight, and any person the district office names in writing**, and she is not one of the eight and the office has never named her and the ward has never asked the office to, and so she is not available. **It is not available. It was made unavailable by the ward's own paper in April, correctly, on purpose, and there is no instrument in this city that puts it back, and he could not even be angry at the ward, because the ward's paper is four words long and the four words are *Duty, and no status*, and he did not write them and he is not going to unwrite them.**

**Two. Mavis Rudge, of Witherow.** He entered her request on the fourth of the month in his own name and hers. It is on a public file in Concord Hall in two names. It is set for the thirtieth at ten. He may give the hold up at any time and the request does not fall over — the request is entered, and the name on it is on it, and a request is entered once and it is not withdrawn by an officer. **If he gives up the hold on the morning of the thirtieth, then Mavis Rudge goes into a kitchen as a person with a name at the top of a document in another city and no officer in this one, which is exactly what she said she wanted and has never once said she would settle for, and the only reason he would be doing it is to make room for a woman at a landing he met on Wednesday.** He wrote that down and read it back and it was true and it was not admissible.

**Three. Ned Callow, of Quill Bank.** Nine premises, eleven households, reconciled on the Saturday of the twenty-sixth with the day-book line earned and the figure in the reason box, and the reconciliation held, and the draw at the bottom of the drainage district came off it. He had asked for nothing in thirty years and he had asked for a person in the room and he had been refused with the best sentence anybody in the Registry has ever given anybody, and he was right to be given it, and **the only reason the culvert at Pegler's Cut is in a note on a file is that Callow was in the room and Callow was in the room because nobody from Docketford was in it.** If the hold goes, that note has no officer in this district and Callow will not ask a second time, because a man who asked once in thirty years and was told no and did not argue is a man who will not ask again, and he will be right not to.

He put the pencil down.

**There is no fourth hold. There will not be a fourth hold. Three is a card and the card is the answer to the question and the answer has been in his pocket since the fourth day of this month and he has been carrying it as though it were a formality for twenty-five days.**

On the Monday morning of the fourth of the month, in a room on the second floor of the River Desk in front of nine people, a woman of about fifty from Concord Hall had told him that he would spend his whole term finding errors in arithmetic and that every one of them would be about a figure and not one of them about a person's water, and he had written *thin* about himself in his own record, and a clerk of twenty-two had told him at the top of a stair that a person who is on a file about himself is a person who is expensive to befriend, and a man of fifty-eight had stood in front of a board in a field and told him that what he had done was worth less than money and more than nothing.

And on the Wednesday of the sixteenth of this month a woman of seventy-nine had said **I have been asked one question in four years and it was the right one, and I have not been asked a single one since and I am not asking you for a cup of tea**, and this morning a clerk of twenty-two had refused a posting that nobody in Concord Hall had thought to address to her by name, and at about eight o'clock tonight a hall in Fetter Lane had had a bin count read out to it in a steward's hand and had been told the truth about a granary in one sentence.

And the three of the three were the three people he had met who had not asked him for anything, and the fourth was a person he could not hold, and **that was the whole of the shape of his term and it had nothing to do with arithmetic and he had found it out with a pencil on the back of a tide table at ten past nine at night.**

He tore the sheet in half, which he has done about nine times in eleven months, and put the two halves in different pockets, and walked down to the field at about a quarter to ten because a board is a thing a man can look at instead of a person.

The day-book on the trestle table had six lines on it and the quota of eleven at the head of it. The fifth column on the schedule above it had a week against each of the eleven names, and every week printed on that sheet was either gone or nearly gone, and there is no form in this county on which anybody may move a name from one week to another, and the Warden may return a schedule and may not amend it.

The strip of paper was under the fourth row from the bottom and it said four words and a stop.

Tomorrow was the thirtieth of the month, and there was a review of a reconciliation of a settlement of three premises of record and eleven households, requested by a person of the district in her own name, heard before an officer of the count having no district in the matter, and there was no room in this city for it, and somebody was going to have to go and find one.
