# Chapter 58 — What the Warden Knows

The tray on the Monday of the ninety-first day took him until about seven, and he was on time, and he noticed that he was on time at about ten past six and stood there afterwards for a minute doing nothing at all with the fact.

Then he walked down to the field.

It is a mile and a quarter from the ground floor of the River Desk to the west gate of Cutter's Fields and he had walked it five times in a fortnight, and it is downhill for the first three-quarters of a mile and then level along the tanners' back wall, and the smell is on the level part and not on the slope, and he had noticed that in the first week of his life and had never had a use for it.

He had told himself four times in nine days that he would read the board. He had not read it, and the reason he had not read it was a woman of about thirty with a slate, who had stood at a tent door at ten to seven on a Sunday evening and asked him five questions about arithmetic, and the last of the five was: *whether there is one figure on that board that appears eleven times out of eleven, and whether that figure is a draw.*

He read it on the Monday. He was there from about ten past seven until the light went, and he read the whole thing twice, and the second time was slower.

---

It is nine feet long and it is two printed sheets and a shorter one on a frame of three planks, and it is nailed up outside a tent in a field behind the tanneries, and it is the most-read object in Docketford district and there is no queue in front of it, because a published list does not need one. The people who read it are people who have walked past it four times in a fortnight and have begun, on the fourth or fifth, to want to know what the two columns mean.

He took the names first, because he could not help it, and there are eleven and he knows six of them from the winter's returns and has heard of three more.

**Nether Sill. Wexford Landing. Gallants Reach. Lower Lock. Marl Dykes. Quill Bank. Rush Halt. Witherow. Ossery. Otter Stair. Ashlocks.**

Nine of the eleven are outside the town. Four of those are up the river, above Fetter Bend, which is a working crossing and the last good bank for nine miles. One of the four is off the water entirely, up a cart track, and Ossery is it, and is forty minutes off the nearest water. Four are on the flat above the weirs. And the two that are inside the town are **Lower Lock**, which is fourth on the list and carries the largest figure on the whole board along with the ninth, and is nine tenths of a mile from the tanneries, and **Ashlocks**, which is a group of six houses on the flat below the tanneries and which is the eleventh name on the list because its premises were returned late in the month of rain by a man who apologised for the lateness in writing.

He stood there for a while with that, and then for a while longer with the eleventh.

Then he read the second column, which is the year of the last reconciliation, and it is the column that decides the season's order in a way no column on that board says it decides anything. Three rows say 479. One says 481. The other seven say 486. And the Count of the Concord Year 487, for which this schedule exists, **was suspended in the month of rain and was not taken**, so that the third column has not been checked by anybody in this district since the year in the second column of each row, and there is one row on that board whose figure has not been looked at by a person in nine years.

Then he read the third column, which is premises of record, and it is eleven figures, and they are the county's carried figures, and the county has not been to any of these places since the reconciliation named in the second column, and the two columns together mean that the figure in a row is a count of doors made in the year in the other column, and that the doors may since have come down, and a door that has come down does not write to anybody.

He read it and got nothing new from it, because he is the officer of the record of this district's returns and he has had those figures in a book on a table that anybody may read for eight years.

And then he read the fourth thing, which is that **there is no fourth column.**

There is no column of water. There is no column of draw. There is no column anywhere on that board of anything a person drinks, and the season's whole output is a share of the district's main that nobody at that field would be able to state if you asked them, and the count's own notice on the post at the top of Fetter Lane says in the first line that the Count is a count of draws and of premises and of works maintained, and the board carries two of the three.

Owen Rusk stood at a board in a field at about half past eight in the evening on the ninety-first day of a year and worked out, standing up, in the cold, with his hands in his coat, that a woman with a slate had asked him five questions and he could answer four of them off that board in about six minutes.

*Whether the eleven are the ones that have never answered, or the ones that answer worst.* The answer is in the printing at the head of the sheet. It is the ones that did not answer, together with the ones that answered late. And the county has a form for asking, and the form has a line for the name of the person who keeps a person who cannot be asked, and a settlement with a man of sixty-eight in a chair by a window cannot produce a return of premises at all — not because it is wrong, but because there is nobody to put in that line who wants to be there.

*Whether the twenty that answered are the twenty that are small.* He did not know. That answer needs a different book and he had not gone for it and was not going to that night.

*Whether the eleven are all upstream.* No, and that is worse than no. The count does not go where the people are. **It goes where the figures are stale, and the figures are stale everywhere at once, and the two places in this county with the freshest premises figures are a ward of two hundred and forty people and six houses that apologised for being late.**

And the fifth question. *Whether there is one figure on that board that appears eleven times out of eleven, and whether that figure is a draw.*

He read the third column over and over for four or five minutes and got eleven different numbers out of it. The answer to the first half of her fifth question is **no**. The answer to the second half is that the figure is not on the board.

And the column it is in is a floor below the counting clerk who put *demolished* in pencil across a returned notice in the spring, in a book called the **schedule of draws**, in a building two miles up the hill where Owen Rusk has worked for eight years and has never opened it.

He did not go and open it. It was ten to nine on the last night of a year, and there is a kind of tiredness that is not tiredness and is the knowledge that a thing you have found is a thing you have decided to find, and it does not go away by going to bed.

---

The tent had a lamp in it and the case was shut and the man of sixty was asleep on a cot with his boots off, which Owen Rusk had not known was in there and decided not to think about.

The woman with the slate was on a stool at the tent door with her knees up, doing nothing at all in the way of a person who has finished a day and is waiting for a day.

And Warden Kade came in from the west gate at about a quarter to nine with river mud to the knee on the leg that had been to the knee in the morning, and she had been to **Wexford Landing**, and there is a woman of seventy-nine there who keeps a book and has not been asked anything since the year before last, and Kade had been in her house for two hours.

"Second of the eleven," she said. "She is answered. Four premises, and the four are correct, and the household count in my head is nine and the return says seven, because two of them are at a daughter in Ashlocks, and I have written the two of them in the note and not in the column, and that is what the note is for."

She put the ledger on the trestle table and did not open it, which Owen Rusk noticed, because she had opened it four times in his presence in three days and had not once closed it in a way that was a performance.

"March," she said. "I was in that house at the count in the year before last and she made me tea and asked me whether the count takes the dog."

---

He had come down the hill to ask one question and he asked it at about ten past nine, standing up, in the way he asks things.

"Can you be dealt with."

Warden Kade sat down on the end of the trestle table, which she had not done in four days.

"No," she said. "And I am going to tell you why in four things, and then I am going to bed, and the first of them is the reason you are asking and it is a good reason and it is the reason everybody asks, and it is the wrong one.

"**One.** I cannot deal with the schedule. I have told you that and I have been in a tent for nine days and I have not found a way. A Warden who is not satisfied with a schedule may return it and may not amend it. I returned it in the second week of the season before this one. It came back with the same eleven and a date four days later. There is no second door and I have looked for one and I did not find it and I have been doing this for nineteen years and I do not think there is one.

"**Two.** I cannot deal with a figure. I do not make them. Every figure I have ever put in a column came out of a return somebody else filled in, and I have said that to you in a field and I am saying it again in a tent at nine o'clock at night because you will use it and I would rather you had it from me than worked it out of me. A warden who could deal with a figure is a warden who would be asked for a different figure next season by somebody with a reason.

"**Three.** I can deal with one thing and it is not a thing anybody in Docketford wants, and I am going to say it plainly because you are thirty-two and you have been in this city all your life and you have earned a plain sentence once this week. **I can decide which losses I am willing to accept, and I decided that on the first morning of the tent, before I had been to a single one of the eleven, and I did it in about nine minutes, and the reason I could do it in nine minutes is that the arithmetic is the arithmetic and there is no person in the column.** I have done that nineteen times. I do it every season. I did it this season with eleven names in about nine minutes and I have not changed my mind about one of them, and I am telling you that I did not change my mind **before** I had heard whether the people at them would rather be struck than counted, and that I have never once asked them, and that I know exactly what that is.

"And **four**," said Warden Kade, "and then I am going to bed, and the fourth is the only one of the four that you came down here for, and I have known it since Wednesday, and I am going to say it because you have not asked and I would rather you had it than worked it out of me at four in the morning in a fortnight.

"**I keep a list.**"

She took a folded sheet out of the inside pocket of the field coat and put it flat on the trestle table, and it was a single sheet with a pencil column down the right-hand side, and there were eleven names on the left in ink and eleven short things in the pencil column, and Owen Rusk read it, because she had put it down flat and facing him, which is an invitation and is not an accident.

The names were the eleven. The pencil column said, against each: *held. Held. Held. Held. Held. Wrong and I knew. Held. Held. Held. Held. Held.*

Ten of them said **held**.

"That is the list of the settlements I was right about," said Warden Kade. "It is not the Guard's. There is no column for it in the ledger and there is no form for it and it does not go up the river. I have kept it for nine seasons. On the eighth season I wrote the other thing against a place and I have not done it since, and I am telling you about the eighth season because you will hear about it eventually and I would rather you heard it from me on a Monday night than worked it out of a form in a fortnight.

"**And it is the reason you cannot deal with me.** Because a list of the ones I was right about is a list of the ones I was wrong about, and if it is a list of the ones I was right about then the ones I were wrong about were on a schedule somebody else made, and if I were to be dealt with — by a letter, by a paper fight, by a man from Docketford who reads a column for a living — the first thing anybody would want from me is that list, and I would give it, and I would be dealt with, and I would be a person who can be dealt with, and I would not be a warden. I have been a warden for nineteen years and I have never once been a person who can be dealt with, and that is not a virtue, it is a **position**, and I am telling you where it is so that you stop looking for the lever."

Owen Rusk looked at the sheet for a while. The pencil was hard and the writing was small and there was no signature at the bottom, and it had been folded and unfolded a great many times.

"Wrong and I knew," he said. "Which one."

"No," said Warden Kade. "You will not get that out of me this year, and if I gave it to you this year you would have it in a book by Thursday and it would be in a room by the eighth month, and I have a season to run and eleven settlements to do in it and I am not going to be a woman who handed a warden a list on the first Monday of a season because a clerk asked her nicely. Come back when I have finished the season, if there is still anything to come back for."

---

"What happens on the twelfth," said Owen Rusk.

"You have been in four rooms with me and you have not asked me that, and I have been waiting for it since Wednesday, and I am going to give you the whole of it, because the whole of it is printed and the printing is on that board and you have read the board, and because you are going to spend the next three days trying to stop it and I would rather you spent them on the right thing."

She turned the sheet over and wrote four lines on the back of it in her own hand while she talked, which she had not done in four days and which told him that she had wanted the four lines written down for some time and had not been asked.

"**One.** Fourteen days of emergency suspension, per person, with a date and a care act named on each notice. **Two.** At the end of the fourteen, a count correction, made by the Provincial Counting Office and attested by me, with a schedule of premises attached and a reason. **Three.** Thirty days from the suspension before no supported living restoration is possible, and no office in this county can move that figure by a day. **Four.** And a reconciliation may be reviewed, and this is the one you want, so I am going to give it to you slowly."

She put the pencil down.

"A review of a reconciliation is requested by **a person of the district**. Not an officer. A person. The request must **show a current local act performed by the settlement** — and by a local act I mean an act the settlement itself performs, this season, at a place of its own choosing, and recorded, and not an act somebody has claimed for it from two miles away. And the request is heard before **an officer of the count having no district in the matter**, which in this district is a woman from Concord Hall who has never been to Docketford and is not going to be. And the request must be made **within seven days** of the reconciliation, and the seven days run from the hour the stone goes down on the sheet, and the person requesting it is **named on the request**, and the name goes on the file, and the file is a public file, and that is the whole of it. There is no fourth thing and there is no discretion and I did not write any of it and could not change any of it."

Owen Rusk stood very still.

"Seven days," he said. "From a quarter past eleven on Sunday the ninetieth."

"To a quarter past eleven on the seventh of the month, which is a Monday, and by then the season will have been open a week."

"And a person of the district. Named."

"Yes."

"And the current local act. Performed by the settlement."

"Yes." She looked at him. "And there is one, and you know there is one, because you stood in a sluice house on Saturday and a woman told you that a cut is held twice a day and that it is the only place in this valley where anybody has written down that that bend is held. That is a local act. It is current. It is performed. It is at a place of their own choosing. It is the best answer to that clause in this entire district and it is in a column written by somebody else in the last year of the last Concord year, and if the settlement had asked for a review on Sunday afternoon instead of you asking a man in a boat to ask, it would have gone to Concord Hall on Monday morning and there would be a woman from Concord Hall in a room on the seventh with a file on the table, and I would have to answer her, and I would rather answer her than answer the sheet, and I want you to understand that about me because I am not being kind, I am telling you the shape of the door so that you can be angry at the right thing."

"The door is shut," said Owen Rusk.

"The door is **locked from the inside and has been for two days** and I did not lock it and neither did you and neither did the woman with the sluice, and she told you what it costs to put her name in a column and she told you on Saturday and she was right, and the man in the mill told you the same thing in a different shape and he was right, and I have been in five settlements this season and three of them have said a version of that to me in those words and **every one of them was correct**, and I would like it written down somewhere that the reason there is no review at Nether Sill this season is that two people who cannot read very well declined to put their names on a piece of paper, and that they were not frightened, and that nobody has done anything to them."

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"I am sorry," said Warden Kade. "That is not an apology for the count. I do not apologise for the count. I have never apologised for the count and I am not going to start nine days in with a season to run. I am sorry in the ordinary way, and I am going to bed, and there is one more thing and it is small and it is the only thing I have ever wanted anybody to ask me for, so I am going to say it before you ask it and then you will not have to."

She stood up.

"**I want the reasons printed.** Not the figures. The figures are a column and a column is where figures live. The reasons. There is a column on the correction form and it is a box the size of my hand and it says *reason (a figure)* and that is the whole of what this service is allowed to put in it, and the form is a good form, and the form was made by careful people in Concord Hall, and it is a form for a machine to read, and a machine that reconciles four hundred districts does not need to know why a place has four doors. And I have put a figure in that box twice for this district in two days and I have nine of the eleven to go and about nine days to do them in, and every one of them will be correct, and not one of them will say anything, and I would like somebody to make it say something, and I have wanted that for nineteen years and I have never once said it out loud to an officer of the Registry because I have never once had one who was in a position to do anything about it, and you are the first one who has been, and I have known that since the Thursday I met you, and I did not use it on the Thursday because on the Thursday you were a man with a tray and a card with four lines on it and a man with four lines on a card cannot make a column say something."

She put the folded sheet back in her pocket.

"You have seven days on that too," she said, "and the day to use them is a Friday, and I am going to Wexford Landing in the morning and then I am going to Marl Dykes, and Marl Dykes has two premises of record and nine households, and if you want to come and stand in a room and say nothing, come at about ten, and do not bring a paper, and do not tell anybody in that room who you work for."

"I could not come," said Owen Rusk.

"Why not."

"Because I am the officer of the record of the district returns," said Owen Rusk, "and Marl Dykes is in this district, and if I stand in a room at Marl Dykes then whatever that room says about itself becomes a document of this office, and Marl Dykes will not be able to say what it thinks for nine years without a man from Docketford in it who can be cross-examined about it later. That is not the same as saying I would not come. It is the reason I cannot come, and it is a good reason, and it is the reason I have been eight years on a floor with a book on a table that anybody may read, and I would like somebody to write down that the reason is good."

Warden Kade looked at him for about three seconds.

"That is the best answer anybody in the Registry has given me in nineteen years," she said, "and it is the reason I have told you to come and not come at the same time, and I am going to sleep, and **do not write to me**, and I have said that before and I am saying it again, and if you write to me this week I will not read it until the season is done, and I am not saying that to be cruel, I am saying it because I have a quota and eleven settlements and about nine days, and I have never once in this work been able to look at a thing that was not the work."

---

He walked up the hill and it was after ten and the last night of the year was cold and clear, and he stopped at the top of Fetter Lane under the two notices on the post, and read the first of them for the ninth time because it is a habit and he was tired, and did not read the second one at all because he has read it four times and could say it.

*REPAIR OF ABUTMENT, NINTH WEIR. Four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds. Any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it.*

There is a man in this city who knows exactly what a district office does with a settlement it cannot account for, and he is alive, and he is unpromoted, and he is the first name in a register of contested closures, and he was not in his office on the Thursday, and Owen Rusk had not gone to see him and was not going to go and see him this week, because there were three days between the Thursday and the seventh and the seventh was a Monday and the seventh was the last hour of a door.

---

Hark Holm was the thing that happened at about ten past eleven and it happened because Owen Rusk asked a question he had been carrying since Friday, which was: *have you ever been wrong.*

It was not a good question. He knew it was not a good question while it was in his mouth, the way you know a thing is going to come out wrong and go on asking it, and the reason he asked it was that the woman with the slate had said *the first one is always first, because the first one is the one nobody has had a night to think about*, and Owen Rusk has spent eleven weeks in a ward hall learning that there is one question nobody ever asks and that the answer to it is always interesting.

"Seventeen times," said Warden Kade.

She did not soften it and she did not put it in a frame. She was standing in the doorway of her tent with a lamp behind her and the case at her feet and river mud on her boot, and she said:

"**Hark Holm. The Concord Year 484. Four households.** A ferry hamlet on a low bank eleven miles up. It was not in my quota in the year before and I went up out of the season because the notice on it had come back twice and I went up because a notice that has come back twice is a person who cannot be asked and I have never been able to leave one of those alone in nine years of telling myself I could.

"I did the reconciliation on the fourth of the month. I found four premises of four and the four households could not say for themselves what I asked them, and there was a man of about seventy at the end of the slip and his sister, and a young couple with a child, and the four of them were on a slip that a ferry needed and the ferry was a work and the ferry's upkeep was not counted anywhere because the work of a hamlet of four was not in the count.

"And I struck them," said Warden Kade. "That is what a reconciliation is. You go in with a stone and you count what is there and the count is the count, and I counted four premises and I could not count the four households, and so the four households came off the count of that settlement for that season and the ferry's work came off with them, and it is a correct correction and it is dated and it is attested and my name is on it and I did not lie in one word of it.

"And the slip was rebuilt," she said, "by the people who lived on it. Out of what is left of the ferry's timber and the money the two of them got for the work and about eleven days. And the low bank came down on the fifteenth and it came down the whole reach, and it took eleven households off it that had been on the count the whole year with their premises and their works and their draw, because the four that had come off it were not on it and the wall that held eleven households up was not on the count and **there was nothing on the count that said the wall was there.** The four rebuilt the slip in eleven days and the wall that rebuilt it was a wall that was not in a column anywhere in this county. That is the whole of what I did in the Concord Year 484 and it worked."

She stopped for a moment.

"It worked, and it is the best thing I have ever done, and I have never told anybody in a field on a river and I am telling you at eleven o'clock at night on the last night of a year because you have earned it and because I have had a season in front of me and I would like one person in the Registry to have the true version before somebody in Concord Hall produces the other one."

"How many," said Owen Rusk.

"I do not know," said Warden Kade. "I have never known. I did not count them. **I could not count them, because the correction form has a schedule of premises attached to it and the reason is a figure, and there is no box on it anywhere for the number of people in the eleven households on a reach.** Eleven dead is a number and I did not have the number, and I have had four years to go and get it and there is no office in this county that holds it and I have asked twice, in writing, and been told in eleven weeks that the matter is closed because the premises are recorded as struck and the premises are the count. Which is true. And the four people whose names went onto that slip and the eleven people whose names did not are both correct, and I have never been able to hold them in my head at the same time and I have tried about nine hundred times, and I do not think I am able to."

"Then it saved them," said Owen Rusk.

"It saved four," said Warden Kade. "It may have cost eleven. **I do not know that it did and I do not know that it did not and I have not permitted myself to say either, in nine years, out loud, to anybody, and I have said it to you and you will do with it what you do with everything else in this city, which is put it in a book.** One of the four is alive, and she is at Wexford Landing, and she is not one of the four premises at that landing. She is the fifth. She is seventy-nine and she keeps the book, and I did not know that until this afternoon when I was two hours in her house, and I have not said that name out loud to you before now and I am saying it because I would like there to be one person in the Registry who knows it, and she asked me whether I had remembered the dog."

---

Owen Rusk walked up Fetter Lane in the dark and got to the top of it at about ten past midnight on the last night of the Concord Year 487, and there was nothing whatever happening at that hour in any street of that town, because the Concord year does not end at midnight. It ends on the ninety-first day. There is no ceremony and there is no bell, because there is no bell in this city, and the year turns the way a page turns, and the first thing the Concord Year 488 did in Docketford was put a sheet on a board in a field behind the tanneries.

He went down to look at it at about twenty past six in the morning, because he was not going to sleep, and it was there.

It was a printed sheet about two feet by three, and it was wired to the board beside the schedule with two loops of tarred cord, and it was headed:

**DAY-BOOK OF THE COUNT. DOCKETFORD DISTRICT. To be posted on the first working day of the season and added to on every day of the season. A settlement reconciled before the opening of the season is carried onto this sheet with the date of its reconciliation. One line for each settlement reconciled, with the day, the settlement, the premises counted, the figure of the reason, and the seal. The quota for the district is at the head of this sheet. Quota: eleven.**

Owen Rusk stood in front of it in the grey light with the tanneries behind him and read the whole of it, and then read the lines.

There was one line on it.

**Ninetieth. Nether Sill. Premises counted: four. Reason: four. Sealed.**

That was the whole of the first day of a season, and the quota was at the head of the sheet in a heavier hand than the rest, and the season's notice was the first day of the month, and the fourteen days were per person, and the district had eleven settlements on a board and one line on a day-book, and Owen Rusk did the arithmetic standing in a field at about ten past six in the morning on the first day of the Concord Year 488 — the first day of a season in which he would be, for nine days, the only person in this city who could read a count sheet quickly enough to be useful, and had been in this city his whole life.
