# Chapter 3 — Two Witnesses

On the third day the rain settled into the kind that does not stop and is not therefore remarkable, and Owen went into the copying hall at seven with the second leaf of the annex in a sheet of oiled paper inside his coat and the question already formed, because a formed question is a privilege and an unformed one is a mood. The second day had been the Office's short day, which is why the leaf had sat in his coat until then.

The question was: who stood behind this.

That was all. It was so small that he felt slightly ashamed of it as he hung his coat, and then he said the rest of it out loud in the stairwell, the way he had taught himself to when a case was starting to feel clean. *Who stood behind the assertion that thirteen named people have no local record at a bridge, and on what authority, and would the same officers say the same thing about a place I liked better.*

The second half was the part that would keep him honest. He had a ward, and the ward was in the file, and a man with a ward in the file is a man who will find a reason.

The interview alcove was on the ground floor, six feet by four, a bench, a table, a gas bracket that smelled of burnt hair. Owen had sat in it twice in three years, both times as the person being examined. Halden Pike came in and sat down on the other side of the table with his hat on his knee, which was a courtesy and not a warmth, and read the leaf.

He read it in about forty seconds. He was a big man with a dry, unhurried way of moving paper that made it look as though the paper had been waiting for him. He turned the leaf over, looked at the blank back of it, and put it down.

"Ask me what you came to ask."

"Two Registry officers witnessed this leaf, and the same two witnessed the transfer entry. I want their names."

"They're on the leaf."

"They are. I'd like to know whether they're Registry officers or whether they're officers of some other office, and I want to know what they were doing at the annex counter, and I'd like their particulars from the current review roll rather than from a file I could be handed in a year." Owen heard how it sounded. He left it sounding that way. "It's an ordinary request. Anyone may make it."

"It is." Pike put his hat on the table. "Both are Registry officers. Both sat the current review standard, which is the countersignature review standard, and both are on the roll for the autumn quarter. One of them is in Fetter Bend this week. The other is at the seal stair and I can have her down inside the hour if you want to ask her yourself, though I would tell you beforehand that she will not remember Lower Lock, because she did not go to Lower Lock. She signed for the leaf in a room with six other leaves in it."

The answer was correct. Owen had known it would be. He had spent an hour the previous night constructing the version where it was not, and it was not, and being right about that felt like being handed something back with the label still on it.

"Then she witnessed a statement about Lower Lock without going to Lower Lock."

"She witnessed that a document was executed in her presence by persons competent to execute it. That is the whole of what an officer's witness attests and it is the whole of what it is meant to attest. If she had gone to Lower Lock she would have been attesting to something else, and it would have been a worse attestation, because she would have been a party to it."

Owen looked at the column of names. Thirteen. Six of them with nothing but a name and an age against them, and seven with an occupation after it.

"Sir, this says no local record. It does not say the people are unknown. It says they have no record *at the named place*. That is a claim about a bridge."

"It is."

"I can produce a witness for every one of the thirteen who can answer, and a care act for the ones who cannot, and I can produce the ward roll, which is kept by hand and is the only roll for that ward that has never been wrong—"

"Then produce it." Pike's voice did not change at all, which was the frightening part. "Produce it through the proper counter and I will have it read before the end of the week. I would rather have it than not. What I will not do is take a ward's private book off a clerk who happens to live in it. The moment I do that, the book is evidence in a case about the ward, and the people who wrote in it can no longer be witnesses in it, and you will have cost the ward the only thing it has."

Owen sat very still. He had the notebook against his ribs and had not shown it, and had told himself it was because a private book is not evidence, and the sentence had been true in the copying hall two days ago and was not true now.

"That's fair," he said, and it came out rougher than he meant.

"It's practical. I've got a quarter to close." Pike stood up and then sat down again, which Owen understood to be the physical shape of a decision. "Rusk. Look at where you are."

"I'm at the Fourth Desk."

"You're the best copy on that floor and you have been sat there for ten weeks without once asking me for anything, and this week you have asked me for the witness roll, the review standard, and — I'd put money on it — the receiving counter's rotation for a fortnight last autumn. You want to know why that rotation isn't kept."

"Yes, sir."

"Because it isn't a rule. Nobody ever decided not to keep it. It just was not decided to be worth the paper." He said it without embarrassment, and that was worse than embarrassment, because embarrassment would at least have meant he knew. "That's the whole of this building, when you strip it. Nothing here was decided. Things were allowed to keep going until somebody needed them not to, and then somebody needed them not to, and here we are with a ward on a leaf."

He took a folded slip out of his inside pocket and put it on the table between them.

It was the review standard. Owen had seen it a hundred times stapled to the back of review files, a single printed side, dense, and he had read it a hundred times without reading it, which is what a standard is for. Now he read the head of it.

*No district or transfer entry in the Docketford district office shall be acted upon without a countersignature of the standard below, applied by an officer of the current review roll.*

There was no Docketford number on it. He had known there would not be. He had been in this building nine years and he could not name one person who had issued this, and Pike could not either, and neither of them pretended otherwise, and that was the actual state of the thing: a rule with the force of law on a slip of paper that answered to nobody, in force in Docketford, right now, over both of their heads.

"I don't know who issued that," Pike said, as though Owen had asked it out loud. "I have never needed to know, because it has never once been the reason a thing I wanted did not happen."

He put the slip away.

Then he said: "Sixteen."

Owen waited.

"Returns failing this quarter." Pike said it the way a man reads out a shopping list he is tired of. "Sixteen districts, Docketford and outlying, whose returns are late or short or unsupported at the review. I've got the list in the office. I can recite it, I've recited it four times this month to men who need to be told there's a problem, and every one of them hears the number and not the names, because the number is what makes it a problem and the names are what makes it a person." He recited it. Owen did not have his notebook out. He listened with the flat attention he had trained into himself for batch days and he let the list go into him in order.

Lower Lock was on it, sixth.

Then, having read the list out loud to a man who had not asked for it, Pike did the thing Owen had not anticipated at all, which was to try to move him.

"District records," he said. "Second floor. Two shillings on the Fourth, and the post is permanent, and there is a man in it who is going to reach retirement without ever once hearing the word *crossing*." He put the hat on his knee. "You are wasted here. I mean that as a compliment and also as a diagnosis. You are the best copy on this floor and you have been sat there for ten weeks proving it, and if you stay there another quarter you will be the best copy on this floor for the rest of your life, and this Office will spend you."

"That's a transfer."

"That's a lateral posting at better pay, and I will put it through in a day, and you will not have to know that this conversation ever took place." He said it evenly, and it was, Owen understood, a completely serious offer from a man who was not wasting anything. "You want the record. Fine. There are four times as many records on the second floor and not one of them is on a bridge that might kill somebody. Take the ward, Rusk. Take the two leaves, take the thirteen, take the whole autumn and learn every holding this Office has on that river. You will be the most useful clerk in Docketford by the spring."

"No," Owen said.

Pike waited.

"I have a continuance inquiry to file."

"You have an inquiry you filed on a thin basis in your own name, which will be read by a clerk in the district office who will be delighted to have something to do about it, and which will end in a memorandum inside a fortnight." He waited too, and then said, more dryly: "I don't dislike you, Rusk. I have signed four hundred of your countersignatures. I would rather have you upstairs where you're expensive than here where you're wasted."

"Thank you, sir."

"That was not a kindness, it was a budget."

"No, sir," Owen said, which was the last time in his life he said anything to Halden Pike that the man did not immediately account for. "I'll stay."

It was not noble and he did not think of it as noble at the time. It was the first time in his working life that he had chosen to be poorer on a principle, and the whole of the principle was that he was not going to be the man who reads records about a crossing instead of the man who stands on one.

"I've got to close the quarter," Pike said. "And the instrument that closes it is a stability order, under my solvency authority, and it has to be signed by Friday, because Friday is when the Council's counter takes the week's instruments and a thing that misses Friday is a thing that is next week, and next week is when the river's worst. Every district I stabilize is a district that does not drown somewhere else."

It was a man holding a number he could not put down, using his own conscience as a lever because his arithmetic had no other handle — and the thing Owen would still be arguing with in a year was that it was probably also true. Somewhere behind that Friday there was a flood, and a choice, and a district, and the district was not going to be Lower Lock, and would be somebody.

Owen said: "Sixteen, and one of them is us."

"Lower Lock is a ward, not a district, and the roll takes them together, so yes." Pike stood. "You've got the leaf. You've got the standard. What are you going to do with them?"

"File a continuance inquiry."

Pike stopped in the doorway with his hat in his hand and laughed once, without any pleasure in it.

"In your own name."

"Yes, sir. Not against the district office. Against the second leaf. The no-local-record schedule."

That was the whole of Owen's plan and it was a thin one, and he held it up anyway: a continuance inquiry is filed in the filer's name, it is public, it is on a visible docket from the moment the seal takes it, and it suspends absolutely nothing. It is a record of a Registry clerk formally doubting the Registry, which is a thing that happens perhaps twice in a decade in Docketford. It does not give him a single power. It puts a clerk's name in a place where a clerk's name has consequences.

"The no-local-record schedule," Pike repeated. "Not the annex schedule. And not against the receipt."

"The receipt is sound. I have not said otherwise and I will not."

"Then say what you are alleging, Rusk, because you have not said it and I have been listening to you not say it for ten minutes." He came back and sat down. "Are you alleging that thirteen people were struck off a roll? Nothing was struck. I have the roll in front of me in my memory and nothing was struck, and if you put *struck* in a filing I will have it returned to you in a day."

Owen felt the ground move. It was the exact ground he had not known was there.

"No," he said. "No. Nothing was struck. Say what I mean. Thirteen named people have a positive entry against them saying that there is no record of them at the Ninth Weir. I allege that entry is false, and that it is false without cause, and I would like it read before the end of the week by somebody who can go and look at the bridge."

Pike looked at him for a while.

"That," he said, "is an error case."

"Yes, sir."

"Fine. It's the right form and it'll take the docket. Error cases get read, because an error case has a floor under it — a person can be put back. You'll want two witnesses for the seven who can be asked, and for the six who cannot you will want something this Office has not got. There is no form for that. There is a sealed thing on the seal stair for a person who cannot answer, and it needs a custodian, a route, and two witnesses who do not live together, and this ward has none of them." He stood up again. "File it thin. And don't let anybody tell you it's about the ward's existence. It's about thirteen lines on a leaf. If you make it about the ward, this office will answer you about the ward, and you will lose the thirteen for the sake of two hundred and forty."

It was good advice, and it cost the Office nothing, and Owen went upstairs and wrote the filing exactly as instructed: error only, thirteen lines, not the ward.

---

Pell was at the second desk on the floor and had been there for the two weeks of her posting, and she was twenty-two, and she had the desk clean.

He had noticed her the way you notice a new lamp in a hall: as a change in the light. She wrote faster than he did and keyed the register better than he did, and she asked questions that were not about the work, and every one of them had made him feel as though he had come in with his coat buttoned on the wrong side.

"Before you say anything," she said, without looking up, "you have been mourning the inspection log for two days. It is not held. It never was. It is called the district works inspection roll. It is a public document at the Front Desk and you may have it by asking for it by that name, and you asked for the weir papers."

"How did you—"

"You went away looking like a man whose house has burned, and the man at the counter wrote *no such*." She dipped the pen. "What are you filing?"

"An error case on a no-local-record schedule. Thirteen entries."

"Against the ward?"

"Against the second leaf."

"Mm." She went back to the register. "Rusk. Who consented?"

"Consented to what?"

"To Lower Lock being in the Mill Ward return." She said it as though the sentence were obvious and she was sorry for him. "You keep writing *thirteen lines*, because thirteen lines is a nice small thing to be right about. Nobody struck thirteen lines. Three duties moved out of a ward of two hundred and forty people and I have not seen one signature from any of them. Thirteen lines is where you'd start if you were cross-examining. It isn't where the thing is."

She put the pen in its cup and squared her sheet.

"You'll want the Form 9-C for an error case," she said. "And both leaves have to go in with it, whole, or the clerk will return it. And if you're filing it in your own name—" She tipped her head slightly. "Then it's not an error about a schedule. It's a statement by a Registry clerk that this Office is wrong, and everyone who reads it will know that's what it is, including him." She nodded once at the stair, where Pike had gone. "So make sure it's worth it."

He filed it at four o'clock. The Form 9-C is a thin blue sheet, and the filer's name goes in the top box in the same size letters as the subject, and Owen wrote *Owen Rusk, Copy Clerk, Fourth River Desk* and sat looking at it before he put it in the tray, which is the only moment in the procedure at which a man can still take it back.

He did not take it back.

The docket number came back on a stub. Notices went out at the bottom of the day: to the ward, to the receiving annex, to the two Registry officers on the leaf, and to the district office, which was a formality, since the district office was the Office.

Then, because he had a notebook and a bad clasp and nothing else to do with an hour, he went back upstairs and put the list Pike had recited to him into it. All sixteen, in the order Pike had given them, spelling as spoken and taking the risk on his own memory, because the man was not going to write it down for him and had told him so by not writing it down.

*Lower Lock. Coal Hill. Saltpan Row. Nine Elms. Corvie Wynd. Ash Wharf. Tarn Bank. Meadowgate. Broad Rigg. Little Fetter. Havelock Reach. Stonecross. Wick End. Fetter Bend. Gallants Row. Copperfield Lane.*

He read it back and could not find anything wrong with it, and that was the trouble. Sixteen was the size of the quarter's trouble, it had been the same size last quarter and the quarter before, and the same men had stood in the same offices and heard the same figure read out to them, and the figure had never once been a list of anywhere you lived. He had written the list down. That was the new thing, and he did not yet know what it was for.

He walked home by the river road with the rain thickening, and the last thing he did that night was read the docket stub, which had a number on it and a date, and the date was the day after tomorrow, and a thought arrived in his chest with no force at all and stayed there all night like a man in a doorway at a party.

The notice to the Mill Ward annex had gone out on the afternoon docket. It was a nine-minute walk downriver from Arbour Kitchen, through the Fetter Bend gate, on a street with a queue outside a counter most of the day.

Three of the Lower Lock households were already sitting in that queue. They had been there since Tuesday morning, before the notice went out, before anybody knew there was going to be a notice, and they had not gone there because of Owen, and they had not gone there for any reason he was ever going to be able to write down in a filing.
