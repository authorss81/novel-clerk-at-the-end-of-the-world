# Chapter 33 — The Application

The arithmetic was not the hard part. He had done the arithmetic on the Friday night, in four lines, and it was in the register, and it was the first sentence he had written in six weeks that was not a fact about a document: **an application is not his to make, because a co-petitioner is a petitioner and not an advocate, and a petitioner is a person who asks for a thing for themselves or for a thing they are inside of, and he is not the injured party, and a county sub-table refused nine things against a named place and he was owed none of them.**

What took forty minutes on the Monday night into the Tuesday, at his own table, with the good plate on it, was the next question, which is the one the arithmetic does not answer.

If it is not his, then whose is it.

There is one petitioner in the matter and it is Marguerite Bray, and her name is on the docket in the first line of it in her own hand, and she filed it in her own words on the twelfth day of the month. He may gather. He may fetch. He may read a document out in a hall because Ada Rusk has told him to, word for word, with his hand on the page. He may put a question to a Deputy Registrar in a corridor with a runner's pencil going. He may enter a filing on a tray he is supervising and cover a bundle going to the stair.

He may not, in his own name, in his own words, ask this city for a hearing — and there is no rule in this city that says he may not, which is the part that took the forty minutes. It is not a bar. It is a fact about who a person is, and it does not need a form, and no clerk at a counter could refuse him and no form exists on which the refusal could be recorded, and if he went down there on the Tuesday morning and put the form in he would be a co-petitioner who has applied, and it would be perfectly regular, and the docket would take it, and the ward would be in a room that a Registry clerk had put it in.

He has been in that room. It is the interview alcove in the first week of the month.

And there is one person in Lower Lock who is not in that position, and it took him forty minutes to be able to say her name without wanting to protect her from it.

She was sixty-one years old. She had ruled a delivery book by her own hand for thirty-six years, in a book that had been in her family for fifty-one. She had stood at a Glass Stair counter in the rain four times in her life, all of them for this, and on three of those four occasions she had gone to a building two miles downriver because a thing in her ward could not be moved without somebody carrying it, and on the fourth she had gone because she chose to.

He had been trying to file this thing for a month. She had not mentioned it once.

---

He got to the ward hall at eight on the Tuesday morning, and Marguerite Bray was already in it, and there was no stove on yet and no reason for the hall to be open at eight on a Tuesday except that a woman of sixty-one had decided it would be.

He said it in about ninety seconds, standing up, because she had told him in the first week of the month that a man who is going to explain something should do it standing and get it over with.

She let him finish and then she squared the delivery book in front of her and said:

"I have been in that building four times in my life. I am not going in a fifth time to hand them a thing a man in Docketford has written."

"You do not have to write anything at all. You have to—"

"I know exactly what I have to do." She had the form out. She had had it out, and it was not clean, and there were pencil marks on the back of it, and Owen understood that she had gone to the Mill Ward annex counter and asked for it and had been given one, which is a thing a person may do, and which nobody had told her she was permitted to do, and which she had done anyway because she has been keeping a hand roll for thirty-six years and the only way to keep a hand roll is to insist on forms. "I have read it four times. It wants a petitioner, and a respondent, and a subject, and a term. And it wants the date asked for, and it does not say a date, it says a *week*, and there is a box for it, and the box is half an inch deep."

"I can fill in the subject—"

"You cannot fill in anything." She put the pencil down. "Here is how this goes and I am going to say it once and then we will not discuss it. I write it. In this room, on this paper, in my own hand, and anybody in this ward who wants to hear it read is going to hear it read before I take it anywhere, and anybody who stands up in this room and says *that is not what the ward wants* stops it going. Then I put it in the post myself, or I carry it, and it goes in my name and nobody else's, and you will not write a word of it and you will not tell me what to write in it, and if I ask you a question about the form you will answer the question and you will not answer it with a sentence."

"Agreed."

"And the third thing, and I have thought about whether to say it in the hall or in the doorway, and I am saying it here because in the hall you would be able to argue with me in front of people." She looked up. "**Do not come on the day.**"

Owen Rusk stood in a ward hall at eight in the morning and did not have an answer ready, which almost never happened to him, and the reason it had not happened was that he had about nine answers and every single one of them was wrong and he had been in the habit for six weeks of reaching for the first one that would hold together.

"Do not come on the day," she said again. "Not at the back. Not in the corridor. Not in the queue at nine in the morning reading a sheet over somebody's shoulder so that I can see you did it. I have watched that trick in this ward for thirty-six years and I know every one of its shapes, and the shape it takes when a Registry clerk does it is the worst one, because it is the one where the room can see the clerk being near."

"It is not a trick—"

"It is a thing you have been doing since the first day of the month and you have stopped noticing that you do it," said Marguerite Bray, "and I am not going to insult either of us by pretending I do not know why you have said yes to it so fast. It is because a room with you in it is a room that is looking at a Registry clerk instead of at a steward, and if they look at you they will hear an office, and this week there is going to be a woman from this ward in front of that counter with nothing in her hands and a thing she wrote in this room, and if a man in a good coat is standing at her elbow then what happened at that counter is that the Registry attended a ward."

He stood there for about four seconds.

"That is not an insult," he said.

"No," said Marguerite Bray. "That is why I have said it. Go and do your work."

---

She read it into the hall at nine that morning, standing at the long table, and there were twenty-six people in the room, and she read it at the pace she reads everything, and there was no performance in it at all.

*Petition 487-GS-19, already entered in this hall by me, Marguerite Bray, steward of the ward of Lower Lock, on the twelfth day of this month. I apply for a hearing in that matter, and I apply in my own name and not in the name of any office, and I ask that the hearing be held in the last week of the present rain season, and in a week of the coming month to be named of it, and not in a period to be named, because a period nobody has fixed is not a date and a week is a date. I ask that the ward's witnesses be heard and that the ward's own record be produced and that the return be read in full including the page of the four who said no. I ask for no money. I ask for nothing about the crossing except that the instruments already in this file be read as they stand. I do not ask that the office explain the crossing, because the office has told this ward in writing that it does not, and this ward has read that sentence four times, and this ward is not going to ask a question it has already been told will not be answered.*

Twenty-six people, and nobody stood up.

And Owen Rusk stood at the end of the table and watched the whole thing not happen — watched twenty-six people hear a request for a hearing read out in a ward hall on a Tuesday morning and not one of them move — and understood at about the fourth sentence that Ada Rusk had said nothing at all.

She was at the end of the table with her sleeves rolled and her hand on the back of a chair, and she had been in that hall since half past eight, and she had not said one word, and Owen looked at her once, in the middle of the reading, and Ada Rusk met his eye and gave him absolutely nothing at all, and it was the most eloquent thing he had ever seen a person do in a room.

He knew what it was, and he did not do anything about it, and he went down the hill.

---

The Glass Stair counter on the Tuesday morning at twenty past ten was a young man with a good memory, and he had a queue, and he said, before Owen had got the docket open:

"You have not come to this counter in a fortnight and I have had a quiet month."

"Neither has the petitioner."

"No," said the clerk. "She has not, and I have noticed that, and I have been thinking about it about twice a day since the Friday, and I have come to the conclusion that either she does not want a hearing or she wants one very badly, and that the second is likelier, and that in either case she is not here yet." He turned the docket round. "And you are here, which means it is today or it is never, and that the petitioner is upstairs, or in a hall, or on a hill, and that you have been sent."

"I have not been sent. I have come to a public counter to ask a question about a form, because the person who can answer it is not in this building and I am not going to write her a letter about it."

The clerk looked at him for about two seconds.

"That is either the truth or the best version of it, and it does not matter to me," he said, "because the rule is the same either way, and the rule is that a petitioner may apply and that a co-petitioner has a different place on this form, and the different place is not the place that applies. And I said that to you at this counter in the third week of the month, and you agreed with me, and you have spent the fortnight since building a plan to do it anyway, and I would like you to know that I noticed and that I am not going to make a speech about it."

He put a blank form on the counter.

Marguerite Bray came in at twenty-five past ten. She had walked, and it was raining, and she had a waxed coat and a leather bag and no umbrella, because a person of sixty-one who has ruled a delivery book for thirty-six years does not lose her place at a counter. Owen had come in at twenty past and had gone and stood at the far end of the hall and had stayed there for the whole of it, and he was not in the room when she put the paper on the counter, and he made himself stay at the far end of the hall, and it was the hardest thing he had done all week.

It took nine minutes.

She was asked for the matter number and gave it. She was asked for the subject and gave it in her own words and did not read it from anything. She was asked for the term and said she did not know what a term was and wanted to know, and the clerk explained it, and she wrote it down on the back of her hand, and then rubbed it out, and asked for it again, and wrote it in the box. She was asked for the week asked for, and she said the last week of the present rain season, in the week beginning the fifty-seventh and ending the sixty-third, and the clerk wrote it in, and stopped.

"That is a week," he said. "Nobody in this building has ever written a week in that box."

"Then it is a first," said Marguerite Bray.

"**It is entered.**" The clerk stamped it, and squared it, and gave her the counterfoil, and then did the thing that a good clerk does and told her what it meant before she left the counter. "Madam, listen to me once, because you have been in this building four times before today and I would like this one to be a different one. It is entered. It is not granted. Entering is a clerk writing a line in a book under a day, and a clerk can be asked to strike it, and it costs nobody anything to strike it, and if you want it kept you have to be looked at. And the docket takes what you asked for unless somebody objects in writing within the period, and the period is a period of days and not a week, and I cannot tell you how many because it is not my column. And if nobody objects, then what you have is *a claim to be heard on a named week*, and the day itself is still somebody else's to pick, and the hour, and whether it happens in the rain in a building with a leaking roof in the last week of a season nobody has fixed the end of."

"I have been in this building five times in my life," said Marguerite Bray, "and I have been told on each occasion that a thing is not what I wanted. I would like it written on my copy that you told me before I left the counter rather than after, because on the twelfth of the month I had to ask somebody to read a receipt clause into plain words and it took me four times."

The clerk wrote it on the counterfoil, and initialled it, and gave it to her.

"Thank you, madam."

"One more thing." She put her hand flat on the counter, which Owen had watched her do on a delivery book and in a hall and had never once seen her do on a counter, and had learned in four weeks what that meant, and what it meant on a counter was that she was about to ask a question whose answer would cost somebody a day. "I am told an objection may be made by anybody. Is that so."

"That is so, and it is free, and it must be in writing, and it goes in the docket, and the objector may be any person named or any class of persons, and a class is cheaper than a name and a name is worth more than a class, and everybody in this building knows that and the form does not say it."

"Somebody will object," said Marguerite Bray. "I should like to go home and tell my ward that on the strength of a sentence from you at a counter, because I am not going to sit in this hall until it happens."

"It may be in the file before you reach the hill," said the clerk. "I would not walk."

---

Owen Rusk was at the end of the hall and then he was in the rain outside and then he was on a step, and the copy came out of the press and came down the stair in about four minutes, and it had a stamp on it, and the docket number was on it, and under the docket number the clerk on the seal stair had written out in the small print what the entry had done, and Owen stood on the step of the Glass Stair in the rain and read it in the ordinary way because he had learned in a month that the small print is where everything is.

*Petition 487-GS-19. Application for a hearing entered by M. Bray, steward, in her own name and in her own words, this day. A week asked for: the fifty-seventh to the sixty-third day, being a week and not a period. An objection may be made by any person or class of persons within the stated period. No date is fixed. No hour is fixed. No sitting is appointed.*

No date. No hour. No sitting.

And so, because he could not help it, and because he had promised himself in a warm room in the second week that he would go and look at the physical act before he put a description of it on paper, Owen Rusk went in out of the rain and asked the counter clerk one more question, and the clerk answered it without being asked twice, and the answer took about a minute and a half and it changed the shape of the next three days.

"Nobody in the district office has objected," said the clerk.

"Nothing at all?"

"Nothing at all, and it came down at half past nine this morning, which is quicker than a considered objection and about the speed of a considered *agreement*." He put the docket down. "You are surprised."

"I am."

"Do not be. An objection costs this office a day and an argument and a paragraph, and the office has a quarter to close and sixteen returns in a column, and what the office would like, if I may put it in a sentence a man put it to me in, is to be *in the room*." He pulled the docket half an inch towards himself. "They have not objected to the week. They have also not agreed to it. They have done the cheapest thing available to them, which is to hold still, and a clerk cannot tell the difference between an office that is agreeing and an office that is waiting, and that is the same shape as your date in the last week of a season and it is about a foot apart in this building."

"Somebody will object," said Owen.

"Somebody will object," said the clerk, "and it will not be us, and I will tell you how I know it will not be us. If this office had wanted a hearing it would have applied for one in the third week, before the request came, and it did not, and I have been here six years."

---

Arbour Kitchen at half past four on the Tuesday afternoon had six people in it and a woman asleep on the bench by the stove, and Nell Arbour was at the back with her ledger open and her left hand held slightly away from her body, and she did not get up.

"You have come to tell me something that is in a queue by Wednesday morning," she said.

"An application for a hearing was entered at about half past ten this morning in the steward's name, in her own words, asking for a week and not a period."

"And you were standing at the end of the hall."

"I was at the end of the hall."

"And you have told me in a kitchen in Mill Ward, and not in a hall in Lower Lock, and the reason is that in a hall in Lower Lock you would have stood up and told twenty-six people about it before the steward had got home, and you have worked out that the steward's day is hers and not yours." She turned a page. "That is the whole of what I have to say about it and I am not going to say the rest of it, because you did not come to ask and I am not going to help you with any of it."

"I know."

"Do you." She put the pen down. "Then here is one thing and it is a fact about my own work and not advice, and I am giving it to you because you will get it wrong otherwise. When an application is entered and nobody objects, the office has not agreed to anything; the office has declined to spend a day. A hearing that nobody objects to is not a hearing, it is a room with a date in it and a clerk in the chair, and the whole of what is won or lost happens in about ninety seconds of whether anybody asks the right question. That is not Docketford, Mr Rusk. That is every county seat in this delta and it is why advocates exist and it is why I gave you four names in a warm room and told you that when I send somebody they are mine." She picked the pen up. "You want somebody in that room who is the ward's own and is not the Registry and is not an advocate and cannot be briefed, and there are four people in this delta who can do it and you have been carrying them in a column for five weeks and you have not asked one of them anything, and I am not going to tell you which one, and I am not going to say a word about it again."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair, it is a standing," said Nell Arbour, "and you will find out the difference in about a fortnight, and it is the whole of the difference between a person helping you and a person standing somewhere, and I would like you to notice that I have corrected you on a thing you have not said out loud yet, which means you are about to say it out loud, and I would like you to notice that as well."

---

He got back up the hill at six and the ward hall was shut and the light was on in the room at the back, and he stood in the doorway for about a minute and a half.

They were not reading anything. They were arguing.

There were four of them at the long table and none of them was a branch of the return, and the argument was not loud, and the reason it was not loud was that they had all said no once already in a room with witnesses on the seventh day of the month and they had all got up and left the thing they were part of, and they were extremely good at being quiet in a room.

He heard his own name and then he heard Ada Rusk say: "He is at the door, he has been at the door for a minute and a half, put him in or tell him to go, and do not do it to keep him out."

They put him in. He sat down at the end of the table. Ada Rusk pushed a sheet of paper across at him with two fingers, and the sheet was in her capitals, and it had a heading on it, and there was no Registry stamp on it anywhere, and at the foot of it in a slightly different pencil was Marguerite Bray's second-column hand with the day and the hour and the words *read into this book before it went*.

"Read that," Ada Rusk said, "and then I am going to tell you what I have done and you are going to tell me why I should not have."

Owen read it.

*Objection, in petition 487-GS-19, from A. Rusk, of this ward, one of the four who on the seventh day of the month voted against the transfer, and not a branch of District Return 487-LL, and inside its scope only through the acts of a crew I lead.*

*The petition asks this office to explain a public work. A hearing on that footing will put this ward about the Ninth Weir. Not one witness in this ward is competent to speak to the condition of the Ninth Weir, and this ward has said so in its own return in the same words for six weeks, and it will be asked about a thing it has already said it cannot answer, by its own neighbours, in a room with a clerk taking it down.*

*Therefore the petitioner is asked to be confined: the hearing is to be had upon the return and the performance of the duty claimed in it, and the safety, condition and use of the crossing is not to be a subject of it, except by way of the instruments already in this file and to be read as they stand. I ask for that in my own name and not in the ward's, and I know why.*

*I am asking this because an objection made by a ward is an objection the ward may withdraw, and this objection is not the ward's and cannot be taken off me by anybody in this ward including the person who led the meeting on the seventh day.*

Owen Rusk read it twice and then put it down on the table with the flat of his hand on it, and the thing he felt in his chest was not a professional judgement at all.

"You have filed an objection to your own ward's case," he said.

"Yes."

"On the ground that the hearing would ask you about a bridge, which is the one thing you could answer better than anybody in this city, and which you have been arguing about for six weeks."

"I have been arguing about a bridge for six weeks," said Ada Rusk, "and I have not once been able to say out loud that the abutment is a foot of masonry and I am the only person alive who has had both hands on the crack at four in the morning, and if I say that in that room then in six weeks the Office has a witness who vouches the condition of a work, and the ward's return is a return about a bridge, and the roster is a piece of paper about hours." She turned the sheet round. "I am not having it. I want that hearing to be about a duty. I have said since the seventh day that this ward is owed a duty and not a status, and I am not going to get a status in a room by being competent about a bridge, and that is the whole of the argument and I have had it since August and I did not need you in it."

He looked at the sheet again.

"You have put yourself in a public file under your own name," he said. "In a building where somebody reads these. And there is a box in the return's form that names a person who may object, and your name is on a piece of paper in the same building this week for the first time, and I cannot un-ring that, and neither can you, and I have been sitting on a stair for two days trying to find a way to put this in the ward's name instead."

"You have not found one."

"I have not found one because there is not one, and because I did not want one, and because the reason I did not want one is the reason you have written in the last line." He took his hand off the paper. "You have made yourself unwithdrawable. A named objector cannot be overruled by the people she is objecting for. I have been trying for two days to protect you by making it the ward's, and I have been trying to protect you by making it smaller, and both of those would have put it back into your own hands where you could be talked out of it in a hall in about four minutes by anybody who wanted the ward to have a hearing on a crossing."

"Mr Rusk."

"I am not able to do that," said Owen Rusk. "I have thought about it since Monday night and I have got about nine ways of doing it and every one of them is a way of overruling you without saying so, and you told me in a hall in the fourth week that a man who keeps a thing alive by holding on to it has no business telling a ward what it may perform, and I have thought about that sentence every day since, and it is the reason I am going to sit here and not touch it."

Ada Rusk looked at him for a moment.

"Good," she said. "Now go home. You are not doing anything here on a Tuesday night and I have four people to tell something to and I am not doing it in front of the Registry's man."

---

At the bottom of the lane, on his way up, the standpipe was running and there was a light in the pump house and the granary book said two weeks and a bit, and the crossing was shut, and the sixteen were still sixteen, and the return came up on Friday, and there was a week named in a docket for the first time in a month, and an objection in it in a woman's hand, and Owen Rusk walked up the hill in the rain with a sheet of paper in his coat that he had been forbidden to write and had not written a word of.
