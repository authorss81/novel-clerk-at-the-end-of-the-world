# Chapter 64 — The Long Room

The survey boat came down the reach on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-third day of the month with a woman of seventy-nine in it and a man of about fifty who had rowed, and Owen Rusk was on the boards at the Wexford Landing slip at about eleven o'clock because he had been there since a quarter to nine.

She was cold and she was pleased about it and she would not have either of them say so.

"There is nothing wrong with me," said Elda Varn. "I am seventy-nine and I have been in a boat on this reach four times in my life and three of them were for a funeral. Hold that bag. That is my mother's and it is not heavy and I have carried it up that stair about nine thousand times and I am not going to be helped with it in front of a man from the district office."

He carried it. She let him.

The man of about fifty said four words to him in three days. He said them on the Thursday morning at the door of the long room, and they were *she wanted to come*, and Owen Rusk said thank you, and neither of them said anything else to each other at any point for the rest of that day, and he did not learn the man's name, and there is no form anywhere in this county on which a man sitting outside a room is asked one.

---

The long room at the Glass Stair is on the first floor and it has high windows on two sides, and it is where the ward's petition was heard in the last month of last year, and it is where a woman of about fifty from Concord Hall examined an application for a qualification on the third day of this month. It holds about a hundred and there were **about thirty** in it, and there was a queue at the door, which nobody had told the woman who maintains the board was going to be there and which she had mentioned at the field, once, in the voice she uses for a price.

There was a stone on the table. It was not the season's stone; it was a plain flat one, four inches across, with a Registry punch on the side of it, and it was the officer's own and it was for taking minutes and not for attesting anything, and Owen Rusk noticed that and was grateful for it in a way he could not have explained to anybody.

Warden Kade came in at about five to ten and did not sit at the front and did not sit at the back. She sat at the side, on a chair against the wall, with the case on the floor at her feet, and she had a slate of her own and no coat, and she had not been in the room before and did not go to the front when the officer called the matter.

**HARK HOLM. A count correction of the Concord Year 484. A challenge to the currency of the instrument. The applicant: Owen Rusk, Continuance Examiner of the Count, Docketford district.**

The officer was the same woman who had examined his application on the third of this month, and she had no district in the matter and no coat, and she had read the challenge on the Monday and had taken about four minutes over it, and she began by putting it face down.

"Before I hear anything, applicant. I am going to do the thing I did to you on the third of this month, and I am going to do it in front of thirty people, and you are going to answer it in the same words you used with me, and if you use different words I am going to stop you."

"**State the figure, state what the figure is a figure of, and state who put it there.**"

"Four," said Owen Rusk. "It is a figure of premises of record struck in the season of the Concord Year 484, being the four premises of the settlement of Hark Holm, the sluice house, the ferry house, the drying shed and the smithy. It was put there by the Provincial Counting Office on a correction attested by the Warden on the fourth day of the Concord Year 484, and it has been carried into the count of the Concord Year 488 of this district in the standing extract of places struck from the count, which is renewed every season in the count room of this building, and the entry was made in the season of 488 in a hand I have not identified and expect to identify, and the person who renews the extract is a member of the staff of the count room and not an officer and I expect to name them in the room and I expect to be refused, and I intend to try."

There was about a second and a half of noise in the room, which is what thirty people do.

"Sit down," said the officer. "Now the form. Your form has four lines and you have written four grounds under the third one and I am going to read them all out because the room is entitled to hear a man refuse to argue three things, and there is no form in this county that requires a man to argue three things and refuse one, and I have wanted somebody to do it since I came into this room."

She read all four grounds out, including the struck-out ones, including the words underneath them, including *I do not argue this and I would be ashamed of it* and *I do not argue this and I would like it recorded that I have thought about it for forty minutes*, and it took her about four minutes, and the room was completely silent for the whole of it, and the man of about fifty from the Wexford Landing did not move at all.

"You struck out three grounds and told me why on the form," said the officer. "I want the room to understand that this is not modesty. Ground one is that the four premises should have remained on the count. It is not argued. Ground two is that the correction of 484 was made without authority. It is not argued, and the correction is valid. Ground three is that the four households ought to have been asked what they wished. It is not argued, and the applicant says the method does not permit it.

"**That leaves one ground and it is the fourth and it is about a season.** I am going to hear the fourth ground and I am going to hear the fourth line, and then I am going to hear the office, and the office is here."

---

The office was here in the person of a man of about forty with a folder and no coat, and he was not a Docketford officer and had not been in the room before, and he gave a name that was not a name so much as a thing that sounds like a name, and Owen Rusk did not write it down and would not have been able to swear to it in a room.

He was very good. That is the part Owen Rusk thought about afterwards.

"The count room renews the standing extract on the first working day of every season," he said. "The extract is a record of places struck from the count of the district, and its purpose is that a place once struck is not quietly returned to the count because a clerk in a field cannot find the row. The entry against Hark Holm was made in the season of 488 in the ordinary way, on the first working day, by a person of the count room in the course of renewing the record, without any person having read the year, because the row was on the sheet from the year before and the year before, and the figure has not moved in four seasons.

"The office does not say that is right. The office says it is what happened, that the entry was made without authority, and that the office accepts the applicant's fourth ground without argument. The office further says that the count room is not a person and the office will not name a member of its staff on the record on the ground of a renewal of a standing record in the ordinary way, and that if the office named a person today it would name every person who has renewed that extract for a hundred and forty years, and the office does not have a list of them and neither, it is understood, does anybody else."

Nobody said anything.

"I want to say one more thing," said the man, "and I have been told I may. **The officer is a member of the count.** Her position is that a reconciliation is a statement about premises and that the recitals bar is a recitals bar, and she will not be drawn on whether the correction of 484 was a good correction, and the office has not asked her and will not."

The officer wrote for about a minute and a half.

"Mr Rusk. **Is that your fourth ground and is that your fourth line, and do you want to add anything before I determine it.**"

"Yes," said Owen Rusk. "One thing, and it is against my own interest in this room, and I am going to say it because a form that carries a thing a man says against his own interest is worth reading later."

He stood at the side of the table with the plain stone on it and he said it in about ninety seconds.

"**I want it found that the eleven households that went off that reach on the fifteenth of the Concord Year 484 are not in this challenge, cannot be in this challenge, and are not affected by whatever I say this morning.** A reconciliation is a statement about premises. I have examined a correction with a schedule of premises attached to it and a figure in a box, and there is no box on that form anywhere for the number of people on a reach. There is no figure in this county for those eleven. I have looked. And the reason the reason column on the public board is a figure is not laziness and is not policy — it is that the form is made for a machine that reconciles four hundred districts, and the machine does not need to know why a place has four doors.

"And I have spent a week in this season being pleased with myself about a fourth column. I want it entered that the fourth column is a column of figures, and that I asked for it, and that it cost the county nothing, and that on the twelfth day of this month I said in a field in front of about thirty people that it was not a remedy and I did not know what it was. **I know now what it is. It is a column that can be printed and cannot be filled. And this morning is the first time in this season that anybody in a room has wanted a figure out of it, and it is the figure I have been the most proud of and it is not in it.**"

Then the officer determined, and the determination took about four minutes to read out and he wrote it all down afterwards in a register that leaves the building and has never been read by anybody but him.

**Sustained, on the fourth ground only.** A count correction has effect for the season in which it is made, by the words on the face of it. No instrument of the count provides for the effect of a correction to be carried into a later season. The entry made in the season of 488 in the standing extract of places struck from the count, against Hark Holm, upon the correction of 484, is made without authority, and is not made. The figure of four belongs to the season of 484 and is entered against the season of 484, and the season of 488 has no figure against the settlement of Hark Holm and none is to be entered. The practice of the count room at the Glass Stair of carrying an entry in that extract beyond the season of the correction is ended in Docketford district from the day of this determination, and the officer notes that the extract is a record of a hundred and forty years and that four hundred and six rows in it are now rows about seasons that have gone, and that this is the office's problem and not the applicant's.

"And the correction of the Concord Year 484 stands," said the officer. "It is unamended. The settlement of Hark Holm is not restored to the count of this district. No household is restored. No draw is reserved to it. No household of Hark Holm has been restored to anything by this morning and none will be. **What has been done this morning is that a figure has been put back where it belongs and a season has been put back where it belongs, and that is the whole of what an examiner's fourth limb can do, and I would like the room to understand that I have been doing this for eleven years and this is the first time I have determined anything on the fourth limb and it is a season.**"

---

They let the man of about fifty speak at about twenty past eleven, and it was not a fact and it was not a request and it was not arranged.

He stood up where he had been sitting for an hour and a half and said about four sentences.

"**Then say the good part out loud.**" His voice did not go up. "You have found out that her paper is old. You have not found out that she did the wrong thing. Four households came off a count and eleven days later there was a wall where there was not one, and a woman on a ferry who has not slept since 488 could tell you that the wall held. You have come up in a boat twice in three days and the second time you told her the truth, and the truth was that the four came off and it worked, and she is seventy-nine and alive, and **if an old paper is a wrong then tell us in what column the good part was supposed to go.**"

And Warden Kade said, from the chair against the wall, before the officer had got a word in, and she said it fast and she said it to him and not to the officer:

"**No. Do not say that in a room.**"

"Ma'am —"

"Listen to me for ninety seconds and then I will answer the applicant for as long as he wants and I will do it in the ordinary voice and I will not do it once." She had not got up. "That is the reason the form is what it is. Every person in this room has understood that the reason the reason box is a figure is that the machine does not need to know why a place has four doors. It is not that. It is that **the second a room has a good part in it, the good part becomes the test, and the test has to live somewhere, and if it lives in a warden then in four years a different warden will be asked whether a wall in 484 counts, and she will have to be able to say, and I will not be here, and whoever is will be twenty-nine and will be certain of herself in a way I was in 484 and she will be wrong about a different reach and there will be nobody in the building to say no to her because the good part was in the room in 492.**

"So you may say in this room that the four households came off a count and there was a wall where there was not one. You may not say that it was the right thing to do, and I will not say it either, and I am the only person in this room who could and that is the whole of what I have got."

The man of about fifty sat down. Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

Then Elda Varn said it.

She got up out of the chair by the side wall, where she had been sitting for an hour and a half with her hands in her lap, and she did not go to the table, and she said it in the ordinary voice, and it went to Warden Kade and not to the officer and not to the room.

"I asked a man in a hall on Saturday what I wanted," she said, "and I have not slept since Saturday either, and I will say what I said, and I would like it said in a room with high windows, because this is the only room in this county where anybody has ever said anything about a reach.

"**It was not the four.** It was never going to be the four. I have been four for four years and I am seventy-nine and there is nothing to put back. **It was the eleven.** I want the eleven in a column somewhere that is kept, beside the four, so that in a hundred years there is a book that says there were eleven households on that reach and that four of them came off the count and that seven of them were never in it, and I do not care in the slightest what anybody does with the four.

"And I am not asking the warden to be blamed and I am not asking her to be praised and I want it entered that I have not asked her for either, and that I have not asked her for anything at all, because a person who comes into a room to say *you are a monster* or a person who comes into a room to say *you are a saint* is doing it about themselves, and I have been in this room for an hour and a half and I have watched about thirty people look at her and I have counted them and I would like it entered that I counted them."

Warden Kade looked at her.

"**Then I will answer it and I will answer it in the one place I am able to, which is in the open, and it will be a small answer and it will not be a good one.**" She still had not got up. "There is no figure. There is no figure for the eleven and there has never been a figure for the eleven and I did not count them and I could not count them and I have been asked twice in writing and both times the answer came in eleven weeks and both times the answer was correct, and the answer is that the premises are recorded as struck and the premises are the count.

"Three letters is the most I have ever had about that. I have had four letters this season and two of them were from men who want me to say that the schedule of settlements to be reconciled is a mistake, and one of them was from a woman who writes to me every third Wednesday, and one of them was a person at this district office who asked me for the reason column in an afternoon and it was granted in pencil and it has been on a board ever since and I have looked at it every day and **it has never once contained anything I wanted.** I have wanted eleven on it since 484 and there is no column in this county that will hold eleven, and a man in a field has proved that in public, and he is right, and I am not going to pretend otherwise in a room with high windows.

"And I am not going to be relieved of this and I am not going to be thanked, and I would like the officer to enter that the number of the eleven is unknown to the service of which I am an officer and that I have asked for it twice and been told no twice in the correct way, and that **if the officer thinks that ought to be on the record, it is on the record, and I will not be examined on it today because I have a season and I have four settlements to go and a district that is short by seven.**"

---

**Nobody defended her.**

The officer asked whether any person present wished to speak on the question of the determination, and there was a silence of about three seconds, and Owen Rusk was on his feet in the middle of it with his hand half up before he had decided to do it, and he put it down, and the officer saw it and said his name and he said no, and the officer said it again and he said no, and the officer wrote **no person offered** and read it out and asked him to confirm it, and he confirmed it.

She was not defended by the count, because the count does not defend anybody; she was not defended by the district office, because the district office's man with the folder had put the office's position on the record in four minutes and was not going to improve it; and she was not defended by the only other person in the room who had an answer to give, and he did not give it, and the reason is the best thing Owen Rusk has thought since he came into this building, and he gave it out loud in about four sentences on the Friday morning, to a woman with a slate, at a counter, in a public room, because he wanted it in a record and there was no form.

"If I had defended her in that room, then the finding would have said that a count correction may be made or not made according to the person making it. That is worse than the thing I challenged. It is worse because it is a practice, and it would be a practice invented this morning, by me, in a room with high windows, and it would be the first one anybody has ever had, and in four years somebody would be asked which losses the warden was willing to accept and there would be nothing to point at but a finding about a season and a memory of what a man said on a Thursday. **A defence is a gift to the office. It says: this is the kind of decision that can be defended, and so it can be made again by anybody in the same kind of room, and it does not have to be right.** She refused it before I could give it to her, and if I had got there first it would have been in the record inside four minutes, and it would have been in a record of mine, and I am the officer of the record of the district returns of the district that holds the return, and I have had one finding on a card that office has called thin and I have written *thin* about it myself, and I am not going to walk into a second one carrying a sentence that a deletion may be defended."

"You have had a good week," said the woman with the slate.

"I have had one good morning and it is a third of a challenge and the other two thirds are printed on the form with a line through them," said Owen Rusk, "and I would like the fourth column out there to have my name taken off it, because as of this morning it is going to be used for something I did not get it for, and I did not get it for a column of figures and I have said so in a room and it is on the determination."

---

The bar came up at about ten past one in the afternoon of the Thursday, and the officer lifted it in a sentence and no longer, and it was in the determination and it was the last line of it.

**The recitals bar upon the reconciliation of settlements in Docketford district is lifted from the hour of this determination and the settlement of Lower Lock may be reconciled on any day the Warden names.**

Which was a fortnight away from being any day at all, because the third week was gone by three o'clock that afternoon and the fourth week was the week after that, and Kade came out of the room at about twenty past one and stood in the corridor and said one sentence to Owen Rusk before she went down to the field.

"That is your Thursday gone," she said. "**Yours and mine and hers. Two of us are standing in this corridor and the other one is in a room behind that door and the week that came out of a printed sheet on the twenty-eighth of the month before last is the week she is sitting in, and I have not written the fourth week down and I am not going to, and I will tell you why and then I am going to go and do Rush Halt and it has fourteen households and a child in it.** Because in about nine years the only thing anybody will be able to say about Lower Lock is that the Warden came and counted it, and I am not going to be the warden who counted the ward that a clerk fights for. It is not yours, it is not mine, and it is going to be a week in which you had a good morning."

Owen Rusk walked down Fetter Lane at about half past two in the afternoon of the twenty-fourth day of the month and the hall was shut and the stove was out and the long table had a card on it that said **ROSTER READ THURSDAY TEN PAST SIX — ANYBODY MAY READ IT AGAINST THE WARD'S OWN BOOK ON ANY DAY**, and under the card, on the card, in Ruth Mapp's hand, because Ada Rusk was in a room at the Glass Stair and would not be back before four and would not have spoken to anybody about it beforehand except to say *Ruth, you are reading it and not me, and here is the hour and here is the sheet and if anybody asks you why it is me at the table and not her, the answer is that her sister is in a room and I am not going to say a word about it.*

At about ten past six that evening Owen Rusk read it from the top, because he read things from the top, and there were about twenty people in the hall, and the card on the table did not have a list of names on it that week; it had his name, in Ada Rusk's capitals, at the foot, with a line under it that said **read by R. Mapp in Ada Rusk's place and Ada Rusk was at a hearing and will not say a word about it and did not ask me to say anything.**
