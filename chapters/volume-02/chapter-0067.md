# Chapter 67 — Two Floors Up in Another Building

The Water Office of Docketford district is two floors up in a building across the cut, and it has a counter, a rail with three sides, a window that does not shut, six people, and a smell of wet paper that Owen Rusk recognised from four rooms of his own and had never once connected to a river.

It shut at three. He was in the room at ten past eight on the Friday morning of the second day of the second month of the Concord Year 488, because a woman who maintains a board had told him on the Thursday that they shut at three and that the only person who is there in the morning is one man, and that he does not know he is entitled to the sheet because nobody has ever come.

The man was about fifty-five and had a bad knee and a cold and twenty-two years in the room. He was behind the counter with a sheet of a form in a frame in front of him and a cup of something on the counter, and he looked up once, and then he looked up a second time, which was the second time in a different mood.

"District office," he said.

"Docketford."

"That is the district office," said the man. "Nobody comes from the district office. Come in, sit down, that stool is better than that one, the other one has a leg."

Nobody in the room looked up. There were pigeonholes along the far wall with forms in them, and the forms were in eleven different colours, and there was a shelf of ledgers with the years on the spines, and on the shelf above the window there was a piece of board with a list painted on it, and the list was of the districts in the county, and against Docketford district somebody had put a card with a date on it, and the date was the twenty-eighth of the month before, and the card said **RETURNED**.

Owen Rusk looked at the card for longer than a person ought to look at a card.

"What is that," he said.

"That is your return for last season," said the man. "It comes back. It always comes back. The Counting Office sends it back with a note and we file it and we do the next one, and I am the only one who does the next one, and the next one goes out on the twenty-eighth of October and it has gone out on the twenty-eighth of October for twenty-two years except the year they stopped the Count and we did not send it because there was nothing to send, and that was a strange autumn."

"Twenty-eighth of October," said Owen Rusk. "Last October."

"Last October, and the one before, and the one before that."

"Do you build it from scratch?"

"No," said the man. "Nobody builds anything. **You take last year's and you correct it.** The district office sends us their schedule of premises of record every season — that is the book, it is a bound book, it is the district's, we do not keep it — and we take the figures that go with the premises and we send them on. Four columns. Settlement, premises of record, authorised draw, and the year of the last reconciliation. It is four columns and it is the same form since before the flood wall."

"And the third column on the board in the field behind the tanneries is the first of those four."

"The board has three columns," said the man, "and the third is premises of record, and yes, that is our second column and it is the district's book and not ours, and the district's book is the only part of this that has anybody's name on it."

Owen Rusk had a pencil in his coat and he took it out and put it on the counter.

"**Which column chose the settlements,**" he said.

The man of about fifty-five looked at the pencil for about two seconds and then at Owen Rusk, and it was the first time all morning that his face did anything at all.

"I will answer that," he said, "because I have got twenty-two years and a stool and nobody has ever asked me that, and because you are the first person out of that building in this room since a man came about a lead pipe." He put the cup down. "**I did not choose them. I have never chosen anything. I make a return and I send it, and about four hundred settlements in this county are on it every year whether anybody looks at them or not, and the third column of that return is a draw, and a draw is a quantity, and a quantity is the only thing in this building that anybody can add up.** The schedule of settlements to be reconciled is printed from my return, and it is printed in Concord Hall, and I do not see it and I am not asked to see it, and I could not change it if I saw it, and I have wanted to see it every year for twenty-two years and I have never asked, because a man who asks a question about a schedule he does not make is a man who is told about a schedule he does not make."

---

He let Owen Rusk read the stand.

It is against the wall by the door and it is the oldest public document in the building: **THE WATER OF THIS DISTRICT, BY SETTLEMENT, AUTHORISED**, a sheet a yard wide in a frame, revised every season in October, and anybody may read it, and about four people a year do. The figures are authorised draw in hundreds of gallons a day, and there are one hundred and ninety-four lines on it, and against each line there is a settlement, and there is no column for the year because the year is on the board.

He read it standing up, with the pencil, and it took him about forty minutes, and the man of about fifty-five let him and then offered him the stool and then took it back and got a second one for himself and sat down on the wrong side of the counter, which is a thing a person does when they have decided the conversation is going to take an hour.

At about eleven Owen Rusk had the eleven off the board in his head, because he has read that board nine times since the ninth of the month and could say it, and he wrote the eleven authorised draws on the back of his own hand in pencil in a column, and he added them.

Then he added the one hundred and ninety-four lines, which took him until twenty past twelve and which required him twice to start again because the sheet is a yard wide and the figures are not in order, and then he took off the district's allocation, which is printed at the head of the sheet in a box, and which is the quantity the river is required to hold to this district and not one drop more.

**Nine thousand four hundred.**

He wrote it down and looked at it and then added the eleven again, and it took him about a minute and a half and he did it in his head the second time and then again on the back of his other hand.

**Nine thousand four hundred.**

"Say that out loud," said the man of about fifty-five. "I have watched you say a figure to yourself four times and I would like to hear it."

"The eleven settlements named on the printed schedule draw nine thousand four hundred a day between them, authorised," said Owen Rusk. "And this district is over its allocation by nine thousand four hundred a day. And the two figures are the same figure, and they are the same figure to the unit, and I have had it twice."

"That is not a coincidence."

"It is not," said Owen Rusk, "because a shortage is a round thing. A district is short because somebody has rounded it, and a rounded thing is round. That is not round. **That is a sum somebody put together.**"

The man of about fifty-five sat quite still on his side of the counter and did not say anything for long enough that a person coming in at the counter had to ask twice for the form.

"Twenty-two years," he said at last. "I have sent that return two hundred and sixty-four times. In the year the wall went I got it wrong by about six hundred and I wrote to tell them and they wrote back to say the correction was not material. I have never once been asked what the figures are for. **What they are for is this.**"

---

"Coal Hill," said Owen Rusk.

The man put his cup down.

"That is on your reach, up above the mill. It is not a settlement. It is a landing. It has four houses on the quay and eleven people on the books and a draw for a landing with four houses on the quay that is four times what the same eleven households draw at Wexford Landing eight hundred yards down the same reach, and I have had that figure since the year before the flood wall and I have never known what it is, and I have asked twice and been told it is not material."

"Who asked you."

"A person in this building, in October, in the last month of the year, and he did not give a name and I did not ask him for one, and he wanted to know why a landing with four doors draws what it draws." The man looked at the stand. "And then he went out to the field and stood in front of the board and read your third column for the best part of an hour, and he asked about the Coal Hill figure in front of about thirty people, and your third column does not have a Coal Hill in it, because Coal Hill is not a settlement and is not on the schedule, and he wanted to know what the board said and the board does not say."

"He is gone," said Owen Rusk.

"He is not gone. He is a man in a building. I will not tell you his name and you did not ask me and if you had asked me I would not have told you, because he asked me not to be named and I am twenty-two years in a room and I keep the things that are said to me at this counter." He straightened a form in a frame. "What I will tell you is that Coal Hill draws nine hundred and forty a day and there is no figure in the third column of any board in this county that is a figure of Coal Hill's water, and I have looked, and there is nothing to look at, and that is why a man came out here in October and stood in a field."

"And that is not on the schedule."

"**That is not on the schedule, and Coal Hill is not the only one, and I have never been asked about any of the others,**" said the man of about fifty-five, "and if you want the others you will have to have the sheet, and the sheet is not mine, and I cannot give you the sheet, and you are entitled to the sheet, and the sheet goes to Concord Hall and comes back on the twenty-eighth and I file it under RETURNED and nobody in this city has ever asked me for a copy of one in twenty-two years."

---

Owen Rusk filled in the form for a copy of a document on the counter at about ten past one on the Friday afternoon. It is one sheet and it wants the office, the document, the date of it, and the reason, and the reason is a box and the box is not long.

**OFFICE: THE WATER OFFICE OF DOCKETFORD DISTRICT. DOCUMENT: THE RETURN OF AUTHORISED DRAWS FOR THE SEASON OF THE CONCORD YEAR 488, MADE IN OCTOBER AND SENT TO THE PROVINCIAL COUNTING OFFICE. DATE: the twenty-eighth of October, the Concord Year 488. REASON: to show that the settlements named in a printed schedule were not selected by the figure printed against them.**

He read it four times and did not change it, and it was the truth, and it was going on a file in a building he had never been inside, and he was aware while he was writing it that it was the first sentence he had written in a month that could be read by somebody as an attack on the schedule, and that he may not examine the schedule, and that the sentence did not examine it, and that a man in a building in Concord Hall may read it any way he likes.

The man of about fifty-five stamped it, which nobody had asked him to do, and gave Owen Rusk the counterfoil, and then said the thing that made the rest of the afternoon.

"It is made and delivered within fourteen days," he said. "So it comes on the sixteenth, and I will put it in your hand myself if you are in this room, and if you are not in this room it goes to the district office and sits on a tray."

"Thank you."

"**And I will tell you the thing I have not said,**" said the man, "and then I am going to do the October return for next season, because I do it myself and it takes nine days and I do it in September. I do not have to be told which settlements are on the schedule. I am told which of them are struck, once the corrections are attested, and in October I take those out of the next return. That is the whole of my part in your season. A man in a field was right about a correction, and a strip of paper is on a board at the foot of the frame, and in three weeks one line of my return will be a line shorter, and nobody will tell me why, and I will not ask, and I have never asked, and I have got very good at not asking, and it is the only skill I have got and I would like to be asked about it before somebody in a building finds out that I have it.**"

---

The records room of the count at the Glass Stair is one floor below the counting clerk and it is a public room, and on the Friday afternoon of the second of the month its door stood open on a latch because it is open to anybody who asks at the counter and the season is in its last week and there are people in and out of it all day.

Owen Rusk stood in the doorway at about half past three and looked at it for about four minutes and did not go in.

On the table by the window, under a weight, was a bound book with the covers off. He knows what is in it and has known since the Wednesday of the sixth week. **The schedule of draws.** A column for every settlement of record in this district, and the quantity, and the year, and the gate. It is one floor below the counting clerk in a building he has worked in for eight years, and he has identified it twice and walked away from it twice, and on the first morning of this month he wrote on the back of a blank challenge form that the practice of reading a column is a decision and a man does not get to make it twice in a season.

The counting clerk at the second counter came out into the corridor with her coat on.

"Go on," she said.

"I am not going to go in."

"You have been standing in that doorway for four minutes," said the counting clerk, "and I have been standing in this corridor for four minutes, and we are two women and a man in a doorway on a Friday afternoon and one of us is going to have to say something about it, so say it."

"I may not examine the schedule," said Owen Rusk. "An examiner may examine arithmetic, procedure and the currency of any paper a reconciliation rests upon, and the schedule is none of those three, and the schedule of draws is where the schedule comes from, and if I open that book and it is what I think it is, then I will be holding a document about the schedule, and the only thing I am allowed to do with a document about the schedule is nothing."

"That is the first correct sentence anybody has said in this corridor about that book since I came here," said the counting clerk. "Do you want to know what the other thing is?"

"Yes."

"**It is a public document and about forty people have read it and every one of them went straight to a figure they already knew,**" she said. "Four hundred people came into this room in nine years and not one of them has ever read down a column. They come and they find their own line and they go away, and the reason they find their own line is that they know their own figure, and the reason they know their own figure is that it is on a board in a field. That is not a secret. There is no rule about it. Nobody has ever hidden anything from anybody in that room and the room is the reason the practice is still there, and it will still be there in nine years, and the day somebody works out how to use it is the day this office is a different office."

She went back inside. Owen Rusk stood in the doorway for another minute and then went up the stairs and out into the street and down Fetter Lane.

---

On the Sunday morning of the fourth of the month he did the sum again at the table in two rooms in Fetter Lane, with the lamp out, because the lamp made him think he was working.

He had it on one sheet. The eleven off the board: **nine thousand four hundred.** The district: **nine thousand four hundred.** And beside them, because he could not stop himself, the third column of the printed board added up as well, and it came to **seventy-seven**, and seventy-seven was the number of doors in eleven places and it had nothing whatever to do with the water, and he had read that column nine times in nine days and had talked about it in a field in front of thirty people, and it was the wrong column.

**The board has been telling the truth about itself since the ninth of this month and nobody in this county has been reading the column that lies.** The eleven were not chosen because they had eleven doors or nine doors or two doors. They were chosen because of what they drew, and the thing that chose them is printed on a board at Cutter's Fields in three columns, and one of the three is a column of doors, and the doors are innocent.

He sat with that for about an hour and then wrote out the three things it does and does not let him do, in the order he could bear to look at them.

**It does not let him examine the schedule. His card says so in the first line and it has said so since the fourth of the month.**

**It does not let him put a word in the reason column. He asked for that column and got it in pencil and it has been standing in a field for three weeks full of the same figures twice, and today he has worked out why it is full of figures twice, and that is not a reason to put a reason in it, and if he goes back to that board now and asks for a fifth thing he is a man who asks.**

**And it does not let him carry a sum.** A sum made by a man with a pencil on the back of his own hand, in a field, in about forty minutes, out of a public stand that anybody may read, is a conversation. He could put it on the fourth column of a public board tomorrow and the woman who maintains it would wire it up in an afternoon and Cobb Rennie would stand in front of it and be told, and a warden would be asked about it, and in about nine days a man with a folder would say in a room that a schedule printed in the last week of a year is not invalid because its figures are large.

There is a document. It exists. It is a return made on the twenty-eighth of October in a room with a pigeonhole wall and a man of about fifty-five on it, and it has the eleven on it and their authorised draws and the year of the last reconciliation and the premises of record, and it is a document of an office of this county, and any person of the district may apply for a copy, and the copy is made and delivered within fourteen days.

He applied on the Friday. **It comes on the sixteenth.**

The season is certified on the ninth. The count of the Concord Year 488 for Docketford district is done being taken on the ninth, and the day-book stops, and the schedule for the season of 489 is printed on the twenty-eighth of the next month, and Warden Kade will not read a letter from him until the season is done and has said so twice.

So the sum is a conversation and the document is a week late.

He tore the sheet in half, which he has done about nine times in eleven months, and put the halves in different pockets, and then he did not do the tray, which he has not done on a Sunday in about four months, and instead wrote a letter to nobody in a drawer and put it in the drawer under the cord, which is a different drawer and a cord of a different colour and has nothing to do with anything in this season.

Then he went down to the field at about half past four because a board is a thing a man can look at instead of a person, and the day-book on the trestle table had eight lines on it and the quota of eleven at the head of it, and the fourth row was the row with a strip of paper under it saying four words and a stop, and the granary had five days in it, and on the third of the month a man of fifty-eight in a room in the city had stamped a form and said fourteen days, and Owen Rusk stood in a field behind the tanneries with his hands in his coat and worked out that he had seven days in a season and a document arriving on the first day of the next one.
