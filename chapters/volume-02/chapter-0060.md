# Chapter 60 — The Reason

There is no form in this county for asking for a figure to be printed in public, and Owen Rusk did not have to find one, because there is not a form in this county for most of the things he has ended up doing and the reason is that an office supplies forms for the things it expects to be asked about.

There is a form for a request by an examiner. It is one sheet, printed one side, and it is item fourteen of the schedule, and it is headed **REQUEST BY AN EXAMINER, AS TO THE PUBLICATION OF A REASON**, and the whole of it is:

*State the settlement. State the instrument. State the publication applied for. State the authority for it.*

Four lines and a signature and the day.

He filled it in on the Monday morning of the seventh day of the Concord Year 488, at the end of the second desk, in about six minutes, and the fourth line took him the longest, because the authority is the only interesting part of a form in this county and there are two answers to it and he wrote both.

*The correction form provides for the reason and nowhere for its publication. The schedule of settlements to be reconciled is published for notice at item nine and the day-book of the count is published at item ten, and the publication of a reason is not a matter of either. I have no authority for it. I am asking as the officer who has examined this correction, on the ground that a reason published in a public column is examinable by any person who can read a column, and that a reason not published is examinable by nobody but the office that made it.*

Then he signed it, and it went up the stair, and it came back on the Monday afternoon at about twenty past four with a word on the corner of it in pencil in a hand that is not any clerk's hand in this building, and the word is **granted**, and there is no second word, and there is no condition, and there is no note, and Owen Rusk stood at a counter on the ground floor of the River Desk and looked at a pencil for about a minute and a half.

---

The woman who maintains the board has been doing it for nine years and she does it on a Wednesday morning with a ladder and a bucket of nails and a piece of chalk, and her name is not on the board and she has asked twice in other districts to have it off and got it off twice, and Owen Rusk has never once asked her what it is and did not ask her on the Tuesday of the eighth day either, and he had spent a week and a half learning that there are questions you do not ask because the answer is not yours.

She did not want to know why. That was the thing he had not expected. She looked at the four lines of his request, and at the four columns he had drawn out for her on the back of a sheet, and she said:

"**Four columns is a lot for a board that is nine feet.**" And then: "I will do it. It is my board. I have been putting what the count gives me on it for nine years and nobody has ever asked me for a fourth column and I have wondered about it every season and I have never said so, because it is not a thing a person says out loud to a counting ground."

"It is a thing somebody can say out loud to a counting ground," said Owen Rusk.

"It is on Wednesday," she said. "And I am not going to hear it, and here is the fourth column, and do not tell the warden I let you have it, because she did not ask for it and she will be here on Friday and she is going to be very pleased and I am not going to have her looking at me."

---

The board had four columns on the Wednesday morning of the ninth day and it took about forty minutes to wire up, and it is worth setting out what it looked like, because the whole of the argument of the season is in it and it is a sheet of paper nailed to a frame.

**SCHEDULE OF SETTLEMENTS TO BE RECONCILED. DOCKETFORD DISTRICT. Season of the Concord Year 488. The week of the season in which each settlement falls to be reconciled is given in the fifth column and is given in advance. The day is entered on the day-book when the line is earned.**

*Week 1. Nether Sill. 486. Premises of record, nine. **Reason: four.***
*Week 1. Wexford Landing. 479. Four. Four.*
*Week 2. Gallants Reach. 479. Six. Six.*
*Week 3. Lower Lock. 486. **Eleven. Reason: eleven.***
*Week 3. Marl Dykes. 481. Two. Two.*
*Week 4. Quill Bank. 486. Nine. Nine.*
*Week 4. Rush Halt. 486. Seven. Seven.*
*Week 5. Witherow. 479. Three. Three.*
*Week 5. Ossery. 486. Eleven. Eleven.*
*Week 6. Otter Stair. 486. Nine. Nine.*
*Week 6. Ashlocks. 486. Six. Six.*

And then, under the Nether Sill row, on a strip of paper the size of a hand wired to the frame with a loop of tarred cord, the notice of the correction itself, which is what an examiner reads out:

*Corrected the twelfth day of the month of the Concord Year 488. Reason (a figure): 4. Premises of record recorded: 9. Premises of record produced: 4. Struck: the sluice house; the mill house; the shop; the barn. The water not drawn is returned to the district's main and is not reserved to the settlement. Appeal lies to the Glass Stair. — W. O. Kade, Warden, attested.*

And the strip had not been wired up on the Thursday. It was wired up on the Saturday at about a quarter past three, ten minutes before the reading, by the man of sixty, who said one sentence while he was doing it and did not say it to Owen Rusk.

**Ten of the eleven reasons are the premises column written twice.**

Owen Rusk read the board from the left at about half past eleven on the Wednesday morning with a cup of nothing in his hand and worked it out, and it took him about a minute and a half, and it is the whole of what he has for the whole of the Concord Year 488 and it is not a discovery, it is an absence, and there is no noun in this county for a discovery that consists of finding out that a column has nothing in it.

Eleven rows. Two columns of figures. In ten rows the figures are the same figure.

And the reason for that is not a secret and it is not even a policy. It is that **there is no reason yet.** The ten have not been reconciled. Their fourteen days are running or have not started. Their corrections are not made. And a reason is made at the end of the fourteen by a warden in a box the size of her hand, and until then the office has nothing to put in a published column for them, and what it has put there instead is the figure it already had, which is the figure of premises of record, and the figure of premises of record is a figure about doors.

So a person standing in a field behind the tanneries on the ninth day of the Concord Year 488 could read that the reason Lower Lock is to be reconciled is that it has eleven premises of record, and that it has eleven premises of record, and that the two halves of that sentence were printed in different columns by two different offices on two different days and neither of them knows the other one is there.

**And the row against Lower Lock is a figure that Owen Rusk could not explain, and he is the officer of the record of the district returns.**

The ward returned eleven premises of record on the seventy-sixth day of the month before. It is the correct number. Two of the ward's thirteen household references are in one building and have been for years, and the county has never been to check that, and the count does not need it to, because the count wants a door and there is a door.

The twelfth line of the ward's first column was left empty on purpose, in capitals, in a ward's hand, and it is the pitch at the foot of Fetter Lane, and the pitch is not premises of record and cannot be made one by a district writing it on a form, and the household on it is a woman of seventy-one who has had the pitch for forty-one years and whose name is in a steward's book and not in a column in this county.

And the fourth column of the ward's return has six names in it and the six names are entered as **none**, in capitals, on a line that may not be left blank, because there is a line on it that may not be left blank and six people in that ward cannot be asked and the ward is performing a care act for all six of them at four in the morning and there is nobody to name.

**Eleven is a correct figure of the doors of Lower Lock and it is a figure of nothing else, and it is standing in a public column on a board behind the tanneries as the reason this ward is to be looked at, and Owen Rusk read it four times on the Wednesday morning of the ninth day and could not make it mean one single thing.**

There is no second figure. He looked. He read the foot of the sheet and the margin and the printing shop's line about enquiries, and there is no second column and no note and no footnote, and the eleven is the eleven, and the number standing in the reason column for ten settlements out of eleven is a copy of the number in the column beside it that is a fact about doors, and the eleventh carries a number about four doors produced of nine recorded, which is also correct, and which is also, entirely and only, a fact about doors.

He wrote one line in his own register on the Wednesday and it is the whole of what he wrote:

*Ten reasons on this board are the premises column twice. The eleventh is a figure of four of nine. There is no column in this county in which a place is given a reason in words, and the reason box on the correction form is a figure because the form is for a machine, and the machine reconciles four hundred districts, and the machine does not need to know why a place has four doors. Warden Kade has wanted this printed for nineteen years. I have printed it in an afternoon and it has cost the county nothing and it is not a remedy and I do not know what it is.*

---

He told the ward on the Wednesday evening, in the hall, with the stove on, because it is the second week of a season and the hall has a stove in it for business.

He read out the fourth column. He read out the row with Lower Lock in it, and the eleven twice, and he read out what an eleven is a figure of, in plain words, standing at the end of the long table, and about fourteen people were in the room and nobody interrupted him and two of them stopped taking notes.

Then he told them the other thing, which was the receipt, and he told them it plainly and in the wrong order, which is the order a thing like that has to be told in.

**The receipt of the district and of its witnesses, promised in the answer of the nineteenth day of the month, on a day to be named at an hour to be named, and asked for again on the twentieth, was disposed of on the ninety-first and last day of that month under item nine of the Schedule of Instruments in Force, on the ground that the certification it was a receipt of had run its term and that no instrument in the matter was in force. It was on a spike on the ground floor and then it was on a tray of mine, and the rule does not care which. There is no person in this city who was required to be told. It is the fifth of five lines. It is still owed, and there is now nothing on any desk in this city that says so, and the rule that did it is item nine and item nine is four hundred years old and has never been amended and is correct in every word of it, and nobody has done anything wrong, and I have come up here to say it out loud in a hall because I am the only person in this city who was ever told.**

Nobody said anything for about twenty seconds. There is a stove in that hall and it ticked.

"I took it off the spike," said Owen Rusk. "On the seventy-second day. I carried it up a stair and put it under a weight on my own tray, and I wrote one line about it in a book that leaves the building, and it made no difference whatever, and I did the arithmetic wrong at the time and I have found that out this week doing the only arithmetic anybody has done about that receipt since the twentieth day of the month."

Marguerite Bray ruled it. She ruled the day and the hour and she ruled the name of the rule and she ruled, under it, in her own hand, which is the fourth time in thirteen weeks that she has ruled a sentence nobody asked her to rule:

**Rule it also that no person in this hall is to be blamed for the receipt, and that includes the office, and that item nine is correct, and that the fifth line is the fifth line, and that the fifth line is now the only one of the five that cannot be paid at all, and that this is a thing a ward has been let have.**

"That's the end of the fifth of the five," said Ada Rusk, from the wall, without turning round. "So there are four left and none of them has moved and one of them cannot be moved, and I would like it noted that we have spent a week on a season and the fifth line went out of the world while we were doing it, and nobody did anything to it. That is the count. That is what it is."

"It is," said Owen Rusk. "And the second column of the board is the year of the last reconciliation and the third is premises of record and the fourth is now a reason, and it took an afternoon, and it is on a public board, and Lower Lock is fourth on it with eleven against it twice."

"Where is the granary at," said Cobb Rennie, from the back, and Ada Rusk said, "A fortnight," and Cobb Rennie said, "It was a fortnight and a bit in the month of rain," and Ada Rusk said, "It was a fortnight and a bit in the month of rain and it was empty on the fourth day of this month and the factor has put four tons in against a paper and the paper is not money and nobody in this hall thinks it is money, and it is a fortnight, and I would like it noted that it is a fortnight and not a fortnight and a bit, because that is the first time since the flood week that it has gone down and not up."

"And the sixteen?" said Marguerite Bray.

"The sixteen are sixteen," said Owen Rusk. "Stability Order 114 is in public appeal at the Glass Stair, it has not been withdrawn, and it is not affected by any of this. **None of it touches any of that.**"

"Then it is two lists," said Cobb Rennie.

"It is two lists made by two offices on two days and neither of them can move a line on the other," said Owen Rusk, "and in about nine years somebody is going to say they are the same list, and on that day we will all be able to point at this hall."

"Read me the eleven again," said Marguerite Bray, and he read it again, and she said, "That is the figure Ada filled in on the Friday in a slate hand," and Ada Rusk said, "It is," and the steward said, "Then it is right," and Ada Rusk said, "It is right, and it is the figure of our doors, and the count is going to come here in the third week and count our doors, and I am going to write the answer in the book tonight, in this hand, and I am going to put it in the bag myself, and I am not going to ask any of you to sign it."

She did it on the Wednesday night and it is in the delivery book in her own hand and it is the ninth line of the ward's answer to the Count and it is the only one of the nine that is not a schedule of what the ward has done:

**This district was entered on the published schedule of settlements to be reconciled on the seventy-seventh day of the month before, posted for notice in advance of the season, and a fourth column was added to that schedule on the seventh day of the Concord Year 488 by a clerk of this district who had no authority to add one, and the figure given against this district in the reason column is eleven. This district returned eleven premises of record on the seventy-sixth day of the month before and the return was correct. This district is not suspended and no person in this district has been asked anything by the count and no water has been taken from this district's main. This district has performed every act in its return every week for twelve weeks and the roster is read aloud on Thursdays at ten past six and anybody may come. The Count wants premises. The paper of this district says four words. The four words are not premises. This district performs and is not counted and says so itself, in this hand, and does not intend to stop.**

---

**The reconciliation for Lower Lock is on the day-book for the third week of the season.** That is a figure and a position, and the day-book is public, and Ada Rusk read it off the board on the Friday morning with the mill's factor standing behind her and said one word, which was "Right," and then went to prime a pump.

And on the Friday afternoon a man of fifty-eight came down off the hill on his own two feet to stand in front of a board behind the tanneries, because a board behind the tanneries is a thing a man can stand in front of without anybody arranging it.

Cobb Rennie is not on the schedule. Cobb Rennie is not in the figure of eleven. Cobb Rennie is on a standpipe rota in a ward hall in Fetter Lane with four households on it, and two of those four households are a household of two people, one of whom is twelve.

He stood in front of the board for about four minutes with his hands behind his back and read the fourth column, all eleven of them, twice, and Owen Rusk stood about six feet off and let him.

Then Cobb Rennie said:

"Eleven."

"Yes."

"And the eleven is the eleven that Ada wrote in a slate hand on the Friday."

"Yes."

"Then it is not a figure of me," said Cobb Rennie.

"No," said Owen Rusk. "It is not. I want to be exact with you about what it is not, because you have asked me twice this season to be exact and you have never once got a sentence that was about you.

"It is a figure of eleven doors, of which two are in one building. It does not contain the pitch at the foot of the lane, because a pitch is not premises. It does not contain your two rooms, because your two rooms have not been a premises of record since before you moved into them. It does not contain the person at the end of the fourth column of that return, who is a girl of twelve and who is on a standpipe at four in the morning, and she is on no column anywhere in this county, and there is a line in my own instrument from Friday that says her name and a line in your ward's book that says the word *none*. **The eleven does not contain her and does not contain you. It is a correct figure of the doors of this ward, and it is a reason for looking at this ward.**

"And I have read it four times and I cannot tell you what a figure of eleven is a reason for, and I am not going to stand in a field and tell you that anybody has done anything to you, because nobody has."

Cobb Rennie looked at the board for a while.

"You have written me down twice in a month," he said. "Once in a return of premises, once in a book at the hall. Two years and one month, and that is the first time anybody has stood in a field and told me what a number is not a number of." He took his hands from behind his back. "It is worth less than money," he said, "and more than nothing, and I am not going to say thank you, and I am going to walk up the hill and stand at that board again tomorrow and the day after that, and if you are going to keep putting figures up there I am going to be the first man in this city who has read all of them."

---

**The day-book of the count had four lines on it on the Saturday morning of the twelfth, and the season's notice runs to the eleventh, and the quota is eleven, and the man of sixty said the reason out loud at about ten o'clock because Owen Rusk asked him for it twice.**

*A settlement is not a row, it is its people.* Fourteen days is per person, and a person gets a notice, and a notice wants an answer, and the answer wants somebody at home, and a warden does the second place on a settlement and not the first. Wexford Landing had nine households. Quill Bank has eleven. Rush Halt has fourteen. Witherow has eleven. The season has been open twelve days and has four lines on it, and the quota is eleven.

And the eleventh day of the month is the last day of the notice, and there is nothing on any form in this county that says so. It is Owen Rusk's own arithmetic, made in a field on the Monday of the last month of the last year, out of a quota of eleven and an opening date and nothing else, and he has never been asked to produce it and has never offered it, and it is the only figure standing in this season that no office is responsible for.

So Docketford district is reported short by seven. Kade said that in a field in the last month of the last year — a district reported short is a district whose count is qualified, a qualified count goes up to the province, and this district was reported short in the Concord Year 484 and again in 486 — and Owen Rusk wrote it down in a register that leaves the building and has read it about nine times this week.

"Then the Guard has failed," said Owen Rusk.

"Then the district has been reported short," said the man of sixty, "which is a line on a form in Concord Hall about a district and not about a person. I have been in this work thirty-one years and it is the same as the two years before. You are the first person in this field to ask me whether the Guard has failed, and it is not a good question, and I am going to answer it anyway. No. **It is late. It is late because you cannot serve thirty-one notices in a day, and it is not late because anybody is wrong. A district that runs a season behind is not a district where a warden was careless. It is a district where the quota and the notice are the same number and the notices are fourteen days long per person, and that arithmetic was printed in the schedule before any of us came up the river.**"

---

**The two-lists question was asked at about half past three on the Saturday afternoon, at the board, by a man Owen Rusk did not know.** He answered it in a sentence and a half, out loud, in a field, and has thought about it every day since.

*No. That is a schedule of settlements to be reconciled, made by the office that holds the returns. That is an order of a Deputy Registrar on sixteen named returns, made in the last month of last year, public, appealable to the Glass Stair, still in appeal, not withdrawn. They are two instruments, two offices, two dates, two lists, and neither can move a line on the other. The sixteen are sixteen whichever list you read them in. The eleven are eleven. A district officer and a census warden each think their list is the list, and neither of them is wrong, and the difference between them is a column.*

---

**The reading was at about four in the afternoon on the twelfth, at the board, and no count correction has ever been read out in this district before, and it took eleven minutes.**

It is not a ceremony and it was not announced, and there is a line in the counting rules that says the reconciliation of a settlement may be read out by the officer of the record of the district returns, and there has never been a reason for anybody to use it. The man who maintains the board put a stool out at about ten past three. The woman with the slate wired the strip on and went and stood at the tent door. About thirty people came, which is a great many, and fourteen of them were from Lower Lock, because Ada Rusk had told the hall on the Wednesday night that the reading was on the board, and had said, in the ordinary voice, that anybody who wanted to come and hear a number read out could come and hear a number read out.

There was nobody from Nether Sill. Two hours upriver, and the season's notice carries a line about enquiries at the count room at the Glass Stair, and it is not a notice that a person is to be told something, and telling them would be a service, and the form does not provide for a service. Owen Rusk looked at the thirty people at about five past four and worked out that the eleven households on a silted bend had not been told that the thing done to them on the ninetieth day of the month before was going to be read out in a field this afternoon, and that the apparatus for telling them was a boat and a girl of seventeen who takes people up for money, and that there was no form in which he could put *tell them*.

He read the reading out. He said the settlement. He said the year of the last reconciliation. He said the premises of record recorded, which was nine, and the premises of record produced, which was four, and the four that were struck, by name, from the strip of paper, which was the sluice house, the mill house, the shop and the barn. He said the reason, which was a figure, and he said it twice, because a reason is a figure and a figure said once in a field is a number and a figure said twice is a reason, and that is the entire difference and that is the whole of what this building has managed in twelve days.

Then he said the date, and the attestation, and that the water not drawn is returned to the district's main and is not reserved to the settlement, and that the appeal lies to the Glass Stair and will take a season.

Then he read the fourth column, all eleven of it, from the top, with the settlement's name against each, and he read the Lower Lock row twice, and he said, in the ordinary voice, in a field, in front of about thirty people:

**"Ten of these eleven reasons are the number of doors written twice. The eleventh is a figure of four doors of nine, which is correct, and which is also only that. And the fourth row is eleven, and the eleven is a correct figure of the doors of a ward of two hundred and forty people, and I am the officer of the record of the district returns of this district and I cannot tell you what a figure of eleven is a reason for. It is on a public board. It is the first time in this county that a reason has been printed against a settlement's name. It took an afternoon and nothing required it. It is not a remedy and I do not know what it is."**

Warden Kade was at the west gate with the case shut behind her and the stone in it and she had come out of the tent at about ten past four and had not come closer than the gate, and she listened to the whole eleven minutes from about forty feet off with her hands behind her back in the way of a person who is on duty and is not going to be photographed, and when it was finished she came about six paces in and said:

"That is the first true thing that has been said in front of my count in nineteen seasons and I am not going to be able to use any of it." She looked at the fourth column on the frame. "I asked for it in a tent at eleven o'clock on the last night of the year and I did not know whether it would be refused and I did not sleep. And it is granted in pencil with no condition on it, and I have put a figure in that box four times for this district and I have seven more to put in before the notice is out, and every one of the four is correct, and now there is one public column in this county where a person can stand and see that they are correct, and **I do not know what to do with that and I am not going to pretend to.**"

"The reason column is a figure because the form is for a machine," said Owen Rusk. "And I asked for it as the examiner. And the form does not provide for it and neither does item nine or item ten, and there is no authority for it, and I wrote that in the fourth line of the request in my own hand and it was granted anyway."

"Then somebody granted it," said Warden Kade, "and it was not me. And there is a list in my coat of the settlements I was right about, and there is no form in this county on which a sentence like that goes, and I have never wanted one and I am not going to start now, and I would like to go and do a settlement." She looked at the board. "You will not get the other seven out of me this season and you know it, and I am not going to pretend I have not worked out what you are going to write to me about, and I said on the last night of the year that I would not read it until the season is done, and I have not moved on that, and I have had four letters this week and two of them were from men who want me to say that the schedule is a mistake."

"Are you going to say that."

"I am going to say what the form says, which is that a schedule of settlements to be reconciled is made by the office that holds the returns, and the Warden reconciles the settlements named in it, and a Warden who is not satisfied with a schedule may return it and may not amend it. **I have said it four times this season and it is the true answer and it is not an answer at all, and I have been the one standing in the field for nineteen years, and I would like somebody in this city to be angry about it, and to be angry at me about it, and to say so where I can hear it.**" She put her hand flat on the frame for a second, on the strip of paper, and took it off again. "Also, about the fourth column, before I go: the reasons were not set by the Guard. The Guard does not make reasons. **The reasons in this column are set by the office that supplies the count, and the count of premises exists to count draws, and the office that supplies the count is the Water Office of this district, which is two floors up in another building and is not this office and has never been this office. A man from it came out in the last month of the year and stood where you are standing and read the third column for the best part of an hour and did not touch it, and one of the figures he asked about was not a figure of doors at all. It was a draw, and it was the draw for a landing on the reach above the mill, and the landing is called Coal Hill.**"

"I have never been up the Coal Hill landing," said Owen Rusk.

"No," said Warden Kade. "And there is a woman at Coal Hill who has kept a tide table for nineteen years and a woman at Wexford Landing who keeps one as well, and they have not spoken in two years, and neither of them has ever blamed the other out loud, and that is not a matter for a counting ground and I am not going to make it one. And if you go up there I will find out and I will not be glad about it, and if you do not go up there then you are the only officer of the Registry in this county who has worked that out on his own, and I have watched you for three weeks and I do not think you will go."

---

**Ada Rusk said the sentence at the west gate at about ten past five, to about eleven people who were still standing there, and in the ordinary voice, and she did not say it to him.**

"Visibility is not remedy," said Ada Rusk.

Nobody said anything, because about nine of them had heard her say a version of it in a hall and it is not a new sentence in this district.

"I want it ruled that he asked for it anyway," she said, and she was not looking at Owen Rusk, she was looking at the fourth column on the frame. "I want it ruled in the book in Fetter Lane in my hand on Monday night, and I want it to say that on the seventh day of the Concord Year 488 my brother put a fourth column on a public board in a field, that nothing required it, that he told us it was not a remedy before he asked for it, that he asked for it anyway, and that the eleven is against us and is correct. And I want it ruled that nobody in that hall is to go up to that board this week and stand in front of it looking pleased."

"Sully says you'll want looking pleased about," said a man at the back.

"I want somebody at the pump at four in the morning," said Ada Rusk, "which is Wil, and which is me, and which is a girl of twelve, and which is what the eleven does not have in it, and which is the second line on a list of five that a man in Docketford read out to us in a hall on the Tuesday of the last month and I have not stopped saying since."

She went up the hill. Owen Rusk stayed in the field with about nine people and the light going and the tent coming down at the west end, and Nell Arbour was at the back of the crowd and had not come up and he had not known she was there.

She said about eleven words to him and none of them were about any of it.

"The woman at Ossery in the third week of a suspension was struck on the ninth," she said. "I asked for the figure and they gave me the figure, and it is the figure I had since the spring: a lime kiln, eleven buildings, and a man of seventy-nine who has never once been asked and would not know the form if it came up the lane." She looked at the fourth column. "So that is a column and not a door, and you have been in this city thirty-two years."

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"Then somebody decided on a figure, and the figure is a department's, and the field does not make the list," said Nell Arbour, "and you have said it out loud in front of thirty people and you cannot put it back, and I said it in a kitchen last season and I have carried it about ever since, and the difference between us is that you have a card that lets you say it in public and I have a kitchen, and I would like somebody to explain the arithmetic of that to me one day in a form I can read."

Then she went, and there are ten settlements on that board that have not been corrected, and the fourth name on it is four streets from where Owen Rusk sleeps.

---

The board stayed up.

It is a frame of three planks and about forty feet of printed sheet and a strip of paper the size of a hand wired to it with a loop of tarred cord, and it is the most-read object in Docketford district, and there is no queue in front of it, because a published list does not need one.

Underneath the schedule, at the foot of the frame, in a narrower type and a smaller frame, is a standing extract that the count room keeps and renews every season and has kept every season for a hundred and forty years. It is headed:

**PLACES STRUCK FROM THE COUNT OF THIS DISTRICT. Place. Year of the correction. Premises struck. Reason.**

It is as long as the frame above it and it has four hundred and six rows in it and it is the oldest public document in Docketford district and it is not a list of anything: it is a record of every place in this district that has been taken out of the count, with the year, and the number of doors, and a reason, in the fourth column, in figures, because that is the only kind of reason there has ever been.

Owen Rusk stood in front of it on the Saturday evening of the twelfth, at about ten past five, with the light going and the man of sixty taking the case in out of the wet at the tent door, and he read the heading, and he read one row, and then he stopped.

The year **four hundred and sixty-one** is in the third row from the bottom. It is a year. The place in the first column of that row is the name of a ward that was officially closed in that year, and it is not a settlement, and it has no premises, and it is not on the schedule of settlements to be reconciled, and it is not on any list of eleven, and it is in the same column, in the same book, in the same ordinary hand, in the same ink, struck out of the count, with a figure against it, and it has been on that board every season for a hundred and forty years, and nobody has ever put anything else there.

He did not read the rest of the column. He stood in front of it for about a minute and a half and he did not read it, and the reason was not a mystery and he did not make one of it. The reason was that the drawer under the black cord on the ground floor of the River Desk was still shut, and that a fourth column on a board in a field and a drawer in a building are the same decision in two places, and that he had made that decision at the top of a stair in the last month of the last year and a man does not get to make it twice in a season, and that he was not going to make it again in a field in the second week of a season because the light was going and because it would have made a good story, and that there is no form anywhere in this city on which a person is allowed to have a good story at his own expense.

Eleven settlements and eleven reasons on a board in a field behind the tanneries, and ten of the eleven reasons are the premises column written twice, and the eleventh is a figure of four doors of nine, and the year 461 is in a column he has not read, and Owen Rusk had a card in his pocket with three holds on it and three named claimants in it, one of whom is a girl of twelve who is not on any column of anything.
