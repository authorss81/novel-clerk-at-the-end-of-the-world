# Chapter 29 — What Was Refused, and to Whom

A sub-table is not a district office. That is the first thing and it is the whole thing, and Owen Rusk had known it since the third week of the month and had spent three days not being able to do anything about it, which for him was the difference between being unwell and being afraid.

The three refused requests are the **Fetter Bend sub-table's**. Tal Sarr walked two miles upriver four times in the autumn of Concord Year 486 and put a form on a counter three times and was refused three times in a sentence about a place, and the register of those refusals is a book that belongs to a county intake sub-table two miles from here, and it is not a district return, and it is not a petition, and it is not a public document, and there is no person in Docketford who may go to the county and say *produce it*.

Except that there is, and it is on a shelf in a room two floors below the counters, and it is two hundred and nine words long.

The Registry's own precedent says it may not refuse a return without publishing a schedule of its refusals. The schedule in that precedent is a **schedule of the office's refusals** — the office's, not a sub-table's — and the honest reading, the reading a careful clerk at a Glass Stair counter would give it in about four seconds, is that a sub-table is not an office of the Registry and its refusals are county matters and the precedent has nothing to say about them.

Owen Rusk had it written out in that form on the Wednesday morning of the thirty-seventh day of the month, in a request register, and he had looked at it, and it was thin, and he had gone on writing it anyway, because a thin argument that is on the page is a different object from a suspicion.

*That the intake sub-tables of this county are appointed and paid by the Registry of Continuant Works and exercise its receiving function; that their refusals are refusals of this office within the meaning of the precedent; and that the office having produced a refusal column in extract in the matter of petition 487-GS-19, it cannot be that its sub-tables' refusals are of a kind it does not publish.*

That is the argument. It is four sentences and the weakest sentence is the third and everybody in the room would know it.

---

He argued it at the Glass Stair counter at ten past nine on the Wednesday morning, as a co-petitioner, on a point of law, in a matter with a docket, in the ordinary form, and the clerk on duty was the young man with the good memory, who read it twice and then read the third sentence a third time.

"That is thin," said the clerk.

"Yes."

"It is thinner than the one about the answer being on a template, and that one held, and I have told three people that one held."

"I know. I have read it four times since."

"You have read this once, in this room, thirty seconds ago." The clerk put it down. "I am not going to sustain it. I want to be very clear that I am not going to sustain it, and that I am going to do something else, which is that I am going to read you the standing answer to a point of law on a sub-table, which is one paragraph, and which is *that a sub-table is not a district office and a county register is not an office record and the sub-table is not subject to the precedent*."

"Then I have no route."

"You have a counter," said the clerk. "That is not a route and I am not going to call it one, and if you print anything on a table about what I have just told you I shall consider it an abuse of a public room. But the point of law is on the docket, and when a point of law is on a docket the office is obliged to *answer* it, and the answer will be a paragraph, and a paragraph is a thing that gets written by a person, and a person who has to write a paragraph saying that a county register is not an office record is a person who has to think about why the office's own refusal column was produced in your matter four days ago."

He looked at Owen for a second.

"I am not promising you anything. I am telling you where the pressure is, and the pressure is not on me, because I do not have to decide anything. Sit down. It takes about four minutes."

---

The person in the room was Nell Arbour, and she was there in a capacity of her own and it was not one Owen had arranged.

She had come down from Mill Ward at eight in the morning on her own account, and had stood at the end of the counter in the queue at twenty to ten, and had not said anything to him, and when he had seen her he had almost asked her to leave, because he had spent four weeks learning that a woman standing next to him in a Registry building is a thing that gets put in somebody's file.

She was there because she is a person who may be affected by the answer, and that is a standing that exists, and nobody granted it to her and nobody could have refused it to her. Six years ago she had stood at a counter at a county intake sub-table and put a form down and had it receipted, and the woman at the counter had read it and written a number on it and given it back, and that had been the whole of it, and she has never known what the number was for, and she has never asked.

She said so, once, at about eleven, in about nine words, without looking at Owen, and it was the only thing she said in the room for an hour and ten minutes.

"Six years ago at Stonecross. I put a form on a counter and a woman wrote a number on it and gave it back to me. I have never been told what the number was for."

The clerk at the counter wrote it down as a note. It is the only thing in the file of petition 487-GS-19 that is a statement by a person who is not a party to it, and it is four lines long, and it is in there because a co-petitioner did not object to it and a docket clerk did not have a form for it.

---

They came back at a quarter to one with an answer, and the answer is a paragraph, and it is this:

*That the sub-tables of this county are appointed and paid by the Registry of Continuant Works and exercise its receiving function in the county; that a county intake register is not an office record of the Registry; that the refusals of a sub-table are not refusals of this office within the meaning of any precedent of this office; that the office notes the submission of a co-petitioner and of a person not a party; and that the office has nothing further to say upon the matter.*

Six sentences. It is correct. Owen read it three times and found nothing to hold up, and had not expected to find something, because the clerk had told him at ten past nine where the pressure was and the pressure had not moved a word of it.

But it had done the other thing, which is that a paragraph had to be written, and the paragraph contained the words *the refusals of a sub-table are not refusals of this office within the meaning of any precedent of this office*, and that sentence is now a thing that has been said out loud in writing by this office, in a docket, in a matter that Lower Lock is in, and it is a sentence about the *meaning of a precedent*, and it is the kind of sentence that is very difficult to write twice.

And then, in the corridor, the clerk said the thing that was actually useful, and said it as information and not as a favour.

"A register is a register, and you are not getting it, and I could not get it if you asked me in a month. But a register is not a document and it is not disclosed, and what is disclosed at a counter is a *number*. If your ward wants to know how many things a sub-table refused against a named place in a year, that is a count, and a count is not a record of a person, and if somebody with a standing asks a county intake table for a count, a county intake table will give it to them, because a county intake table will do almost anything that does not require it to open a book."

"A standing."

"A person who may be affected by it," said the clerk, "or a person acting for a district. Neither of those things is you. You are a clerk in the receiving office of this district. You are, in fact, about the worst standing anybody in that building has this morning, and I have watched you not notice that for four weeks, and I am mentioning it as a matter of professional interest."

---

The first thing he did was go and ask the only person in the building who was better at it than he was, which was a piece of discipline he had learned from her and had never once wanted.

"You have a piece of paper in your coat and you have been carrying it since Monday," said Junia Pell, "and I have watched you carry it for three days the way people carry a thing they are about to be told off for. So I am going to say it before you go, and then you are not to do it twice. **You are not the injured party.**"

"That is four words and it is the whole of it, is it not."

"It is four words and it is the whole of it, and I have been waiting three days to see whether you would get there on your own, and you have got there on your own, and I would like that noticed in the sense that I noticed." She did not look up from the tray. "Nine things were refused for that named place in that year. One of them was surrendered by the man who surrendered it. You did not surrender the other eight. The other eight were refused from other people by other people, in a form, at a counter, and every one of those people is a person who may be affected, and every one of them is not you, and not the ward, and not this Office, and the only person on earth who may go and ask is them."

"There is no instrument that compels anybody to—"

"There is no instrument that compels anything at all," said Pell. "That is the whole of the practice and you have had it for a month. A person asks. A person may be affected. A person asks. Eight people have to ask. That is the price of the finding out and it is charged to them and not to you, and the only thing you can do with a piece of paper in your pocket is to put it where they can reach it. And I am telling you that before you go, because if you go without knowing it you will come back with it under your arm and put it in a drawer of your own, and I have watched you do that twice."

---

He went and got it on the Wednesday afternoon from the Fetter Bend sub-table, in person, on the walk up, because it took twenty minutes to ask and an afternoon to get an answer by a boy, and because he had been told not to go, and he had a piece of paper in his coat and a form on it that said *a person who may be affected by an entry may ask for it* and he is not one and he had never been one.

Nine.

**Nine refusals** entered against the named place *Lower Lock* in the sub-table's register for the intake year. Nine numbers, nine dates, nine forms, nine dispositions, nine household references — eight of them given, one not — and the dispositions are all the same sentence, and Owen read all nine of them standing at a counter in a room that smelled of wet wool and had a stove in it, and did not get a single name, and did not expect to.

The ninth is dated the ninth day of the month of the intake year and it is a form 12-B and it is the one he surrendered on the sixth day of this month in a warm room, and it is the ninth of nine because eight people in his ward had been turned away at the same counter, in the same form, in the same year, before Tal Sarr of this ward walked up to a counter with a folded sheet for the fourth time.

The household references are household references. *Household 14-B, Fetter Lane.* *Household 22, Fetter Lane.* Four of them are Fetter Lane. One is a court off Bell Row. One is a house on the water side that Owen knows the name of and has never been inside.

Not one name. A sub-table does not take a name against a refusal. A sub-table takes a reference, in the way a sub-table takes a reference for everything, and a reference is a way of saying *a person was here* without a person being anything at all.

Owen Rusk walked back down the hill from Fetter Bend in the rain with a piece of paper in his inside pocket on the left that had nine lines on it and no names, and at the bottom of the hill he sat down on the wall where he had sat on the sixth day of the month, and stayed there for about four minutes, and then he went up to the hall, because the hall closes its business at eight and there were two things he had to do about a piece of paper and only one of them was his.

---

He went up the hill and he did the second thing, and the second thing took four minutes.

He put the sheet on the long table in the ward hall with the nine lines on it and the register number at the top and the household references down the left and the dates down the right, and he did not give it to anybody.

"This is not mine," he said. "I have no standing on this and I have been told so this afternoon by a person who is better at it than I am, and I am not going to argue. There are eight things in this column that I have never seen refused and cannot see refused and have no business asking about, and there is a woman in this ward who has kept a roll by hand for thirty-six years and who could put a name to four of those references before supper."

Marguerite Bray looked at the sheet for a while.

"Household 22, Fetter Lane," she said. "That is the Anstruthers' second house. No — it is not. It is the house that was the Anstruthers' second house, and it has been the Reddy house since the winter, and I know the Reddys because their boy walks down with Ivy on a Tuesday when she carries my notices to the factor's." She put her finger on the third line and did not say anything for a moment, and then she said, "And that one is at four Lantern Walk."

Owen Rusk stood in a ward hall and did not move.

"Four Lantern Walk," he said.

"It has been the Peregrines' for as long as I have been keeping this," said Marguerite Bray, "and before them it was nobody, and there is a letter box and a gate and nobody has lived at it in this ward since I was a young woman, and if there is a person at it then they are not of this ward and it is not mine to write about." She looked up. "Why."

"Two returned notices," said Owen. "In the tray, since the start of the month. A quarterly works notice, twice, not known at the address."

"Then it can wait, and it will wait a long time, and that is not your business either." She squared the sheet. "Seven, then. Not the one at four Lantern Walk."

She got the pencil out.

"How do you want it done."

"I would like it done the way you do everything," said Owen Rusk, "with the day and the hour, and read back, and the page ruled, and I would like you to do it in the hall and not at four doors, so that anybody who has been in this room can say what was asked."

Marguerite Bray wrote one line at the top of the delivery book, in pencil, in her ruled hand, and it said:

*Seven questions, from the county sub-table's register, intake year 486, against this ward, being the eight references in this ward of which one is not ours. Asked by me, in this hall, one at a time, to any person who chooses to come.*

And then she wrote seven numbers under it, and the seven numbers were seven of the eight household references, and she did not write a name against any of them, and she read the whole thing back to the room, and Owen Rusk did not write a single word of it down.

He had it in the sheet, which the ward now had. He had the count, which was nine. He had the register number, which was a number. He had nothing else, and he was not going to get anything else, and the Office had not explained the three and was never going to explain the three, and the thing that Tal Sarr's fourth visit had become, eleven months on, was not a correction and was not a precedent and was not a vindication, and the charge on the docket still said **scope**, and the charge Owen had withdrawn on the sixth day of the month was not reinstated and could not be by him and would not be by anybody.

And the reason he wrote none of it down was that at about eight o'clock that night, sitting at his own table with his mother's good plate on it, he took the register out and started to write down how he felt about giving it away, in four lines, in the flat unhurried hand, and got about nine words in and stopped, and read what he had written, and understood that he was not writing down how he felt about giving it away.

He was writing down that he had given it away, so that on a bad day in three months he could point at the entry and say that he had done it.

He left the four lines blank and closed the book.

And then, on the way out, Ada Rusk read the line in the book and read it again and asked him one question.

"The third term," she said. "Any person not of this ward may read the roster on any day, without notice, and no person of the ward may ask them why. And the woman at Wexford Landing has given us one day, and she has said the ward may name a further one. Has the ward named one."

"Not yet."

"Then the ward is going to name one," said Ada, "and it is not going to be the Registry's man who names it, and it is not going to be me, and it is going to be a piece of the ward's own paper with a day on it and my name at the bottom of it, and I am going to write it tonight." She put her hand flat on the book. "Go home, Mr Rusk. You have been useful for three days running and I do not trust it."
