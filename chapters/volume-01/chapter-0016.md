# Chapter 16 — Separability

The challenge came down at nine in the morning on the twenty-second day of the month, on the countersignature desk, in the correct form, and it was correct, and Owen Rusk read it twice standing up in the corridor outside the seal stair and then went and sat down on the bottom step with it on his knees for a quarter of an hour.

It was four paragraphs and it had no name on it, and it had a docket number, and the docket number was the file number of the ward's return, which meant it had been raised *against* the instrument rather than in ignorance of it, by somebody who had read all nine pages and the two leaves and the countersignature and had then gone to the countersignature desk and written four paragraphs in the correct form on the correct day.

*In the matter of District Return 487-LL. It is submitted that the second observer named in this return is an officer of the Registry of Continuant Works; that this Office is the district office before which the return is filed; that the Office is the office which received the transfer of the maintenance of the named work to the Mill Ward district return, of the eleventh day of C.Y. 486, being intake 461/F-B/9; and that the Office's interest in the filing succeeding is therefore not an interest separable from the outcome. It is further submitted that a separation of interest made by an officer of an interested office is a statement by that office, and cannot cure that interest. It is further submitted that the return should stand or fall upon the second observer alone. It is further submitted that no statement of the first observer's interest has been contradicted, and that nothing in this return answers the objection.*

There were eleven returns in a rack behind the Front Desk counter and a bundle in it that a man had handed in on a Thursday, and this was the twentieth day of the month and Owen had had four hundred and eleven words read to him in a hall, and now this was on the step.

It was correct.

He had known it was correct by the third line, and he had gone on reading to the end anyway, because a man who stops reading a challenge at the line he likes is a man who will be told later that he did not read it. And at the end of the fourth paragraph there was nothing at all to hold up, and he sat on a step for a quarter of an hour and could not think of one thing to put his hand on, and the reason he could not think of one thing was that the challenge was not attacking the return. It was attacking him — or rather, it was attacking a fact about the return, which was that one of the two people standing behind it works in the building the return is filed in.

He was aware, sitting on the step, that he had wanted, for about four days, for something to attack the ward. And that this was not that, and that he could feel the difference between the two at about the age of nine, and that being able to feel it had not stopped him from feeling the other thing first.

---

Pell was on the second desk with the tray in front of her and no case in it, and Owen put the challenge down on the corner of the board.

"Read that first," he said, "before I say anything."

She read it. It took her about a minute and a half, which was four times as long as it needed, and she did not say anything, and she read it a second time, and then she put her hand flat on it and left it there.

"Right," she said. "Now you are going to ask me who filed it, and I am going to tell you not to find out, and I am going to give you a reason that is not moral, because you will not take a moral one from me and you are entitled not to."

"I'm listening."

"A challenge that names a filer can be answered. That is what a filer is for. You go and say *the fourth paragraph is not good because this man has wanted Lower Lock closed since the autumn*, and I go and find out who it is, and I find out who it is, and I bring you a name." She took her hand off the paper. "If it is a name that is in the district office, then you have answered the challenge and you have also started a fight about a man's character, and a fight about a man's character cannot be won in a review room by a man whose name is the filer's, because the whole room will spend the day being kind to you. And if it is a name that is not in the district office, then you have spent your morning and told a counter-clerk in Mill Ward that you are looking, and the next document that comes down this week will be written by somebody who knows."

"So we do nothing about who it is."

"We do everything about whether it is right," said Pell, "and nothing whatever about who wrote it, and I want you to understand that those are not the same size and I have thought about it for an hour."

Then she turned the sheet round and squared it, and sat down properly, and told him the second thing, and he understood afterwards that she had got herself settled before she said it, which she had never done about anything in a month.

"The challenge is good," she said.

"I know."

"I want to hear whether you have got anything, because you have had four days and I have had one night, and if you have got something I would rather have it now than on Friday." She put both hands flat on the board. "I have read it twice. If I were a reviewing officer I would sustain it. I am going to sustain it myself this afternoon unless you can produce something better by four o'clock, and I want you to understand that the person sustaining it is me, and that it will be in my own hand, and that it will be on the file of the instrument I witnessed."

Owen sat down on the end of the tray, which he was not supposed to do.

"Pell—"

"Here is the part that is actually difficult and I am going to say it once." She did not raise her voice, and that was the terrible thing about it, and the hall went on copying around them. "You wrote a separation of interest in the margin on the seventh day, and you did not write it, and I did, and I put it in the wrong order on purpose so that nobody could take the last two words and leave out the first two. And it is four sentences long, and it is honest, and it is worth exactly nothing, and it was worth nothing the moment I signed it and it is worth nothing now, and everything it says is true and none of it cures the thing it is about."

"Because—"

"Because a separation is a statement." She looked at him directly. "And I am an officer of this Office. And when I make a statement, the statement is made by the Office. It is not that I am lying about my interest. Everybody in that review room will accept that I am not lying about my interest. It is that a Registry officer of the Registry of Continuant Works saying *my interest is my office's and my office has an interest in this succeeding* is a piece of information about the Office, and the Office has an interest in this succeeding, and so the statement is a statement by an interested party, and it does not become less interested by being accurate."

"I know the argument. I argued it in the intake alcove on—"

"You argued the transfer." She stopped. He had not meant to say it and she had heard him mean to. "You argued it in the interview alcove in the first week of the month, and you were right, and I wrote it down, and the sentence you said was that a smaller thing validly received has been used past the edge of what it could carry, and that the Office took it and stretched it to a ward." She put her finger on the challenge. "That is the whole of this. That is this document. A smaller thing validly received — one officer's statement, in a margin, honestly given — has been used past the edge of what it can carry, and the person who used it is us, and the person who handed it over is the ward, because the ward asked for two observers and there are two kinds of observer in this city and the ward could have one of each and asked for one of each and did not know there was a choice."

The hall made the noise it made. A door somewhere. The press.

"You handed it over," said Owen.

"I know what I did," said Pell. "It is my margin and my hand and I put the sentence in in the order I wanted it in, and I would put it in again, and I am not going to be able to tell you for about a year whether I would have found a way to do it that would not have been handed over."

---

He had until four o'clock and he used the first hour of it correctly, which was to go and look at what the form required, and the form required nothing at all, which was the discovery that undid the morning.

A return is entered on two observers with separable interests. The challenge was right that the second observer's interest was the Office's. The challenge was also right that the first observer's interest was his district's, and had been written in his own words, on the seventh day, in a margin, uncontradicted. The form did not say *and they may be separated by their own statements*. The form said *separable interests*, and it said it in the only language it had, and a reviewing officer reading the form in the morning of the twenty-second day of the month had read *separable* and understood *capable of being set apart*, and had found a document in which one of the two had set himself apart in his own hand and had decided that a self-setting-apart by an interested officer is not a setting-apart.

Which meant the return was not, at that moment, two observers. It was one and a half, and the half was a woman of twenty-two who had been in the building three months and had already refused a short-form attestation, dated a statement she had not witnessed, and put her own office against herself in a margin in the wrong order on purpose.

He had one hour and there was no document in the world that fixed it, and at twenty past eleven he stopped looking for one and went and found the only other person on the return.

Wend Voss was behind the Mill Ward annex counter with a queue of four and a tray of counterfoils in front of him, and Owen did not ask him to leave the counter, because a man who has been asked to leave a counter cannot be asked properly about anything.

He stood at the end of the queue for twenty minutes, and when Voss came round the end of it with the last man's paper, Owen said, very quietly, "Mr Voss. I am going to ask you for a thing and you are going to be able to say no to it inside a sentence, and I am going to tell you what it is before I ask, because I have been told not to negotiate it in a room."

Voss put the counterfoil down.

"I have got a challenge on the return," said Owen. "Against Pell. It says her office has an interest in the filing succeeding and that her separation of interest is a statement by the Office and cannot cure it, and that the return should stand or fall on you alone. It is correct. I have had it since nine this morning and I have not been able to find one word of it to hold up, and I am not going to try, and I am not going to go and find out who wrote it."

Voss waited about two seconds.

"Then what do you want."

"I want to know whether you will stand on a document that a Registry officer has just been challenged on, having told me exactly what I am asking and exactly what it costs, which is this." Owen put his hands flat on the counter, and did not look at the queue. "If you say yes, the return goes to a review with two observers on it, and one of them has been publicly found contestable in the correct form by somebody in the district office, and the other one is a counter clerk in the annex that received the transfer. And then a second challenge comes. It will come, and it will not be as careful as the first one, because the first one did not have to be more careful than the standard. The second one will come at the second review, or the third, and it will come at you, and it will say that your district received the duty and has never performed it and that a district which has been carrying a cost it did not ask for has a separable interest, and that will also be correct, and you will be the only person left in this instrument whose name is a thing the Office can use."

"And if I say no?"

"Then there is one observer, and the return comes back, and the ward is frozen on Friday the twenty-eighth, and I will have to go up that hill and tell nine people that we got one of them as far as a second day and then could not keep it." Owen did not soften it. "That is the whole of it. I am asking you as a favour and not as an instruction, because you are not in this return as a favour, you are in it as the second observer of a document you read before you signed it, and if you would rather answer a reviewing officer than answer me then you may do that instead and I will put it in the file in the same words."

Voss looked at him for a long moment. Behind him, the queue was three people long and a woman at the end of it had a parcel she was holding wrong.

"I have been carrying that crossing for eleven months," he said. "Not the bridge. The cost of it. A line in a district works schedule that reads *crossing, Ninth Weir, no crew available*, which has meant for eleven months that when the district's clerk asks me what a district return cannot do, I say *the weir*, and everybody in that room stops talking, and then somebody says *we know*, and then we all carry on." He squared his tray. "That is a cost my district has had and has not used. I wrote that on the seventh day in a margin, in my own words, and I would like to say one more thing about it, in my own words, on the same page, and then I will take whatever comes."

"Then say it."

"The Office's interest is not separable," said Wend Voss. "I have never pretended it was. My district is not a neutral party to this document; my district is a party to the thing that made it necessary. If I stand here and somebody asks me whether I am independent, the true answer is no, and I have put that in ink twice and I will put it in ink a third time. The only reason any of it is tolerable is that it is written down in my own hand where a reviewer can find it, and a man whose interest is written on the document cannot be quietly holding it up, and a man whose interest is in his head can." He picked the counterfoil back up. "Send me the page and I will put it in. And tell your clerk I am not doing it for her and I am not doing it for the ward. I am doing it because if that document goes down I will be a man at a counter who read a ward perform a duty for eleven months and then watched it be taken off them, and I will be asked about it every year for the rest of my service, and I would rather be asked about a thing I put my name next to."

---

Owen wrote it down. He wrote down almost nothing else, and the almost nothing was this: that in eleven days he had gone up a hill and asked a man for a favour and got it, and that in the same eleven days he had gone to a woman on a copying floor and asked her to be examined and she had, and that neither of those things was in his power, and that the whole of what he had done that month was arrange for other people to be asked.

Pell sustained it at half past three.

She did it on her own paper, in her own hand, and she filed it at the countersignature desk herself at ten to four, in person, and she did not put Owen's name on it and she did not tell him she was going to do it, and she did not go to the ward and she did not go to Voss. He found out at twenty past four from the docket stub, which is how he found out, and which she had clearly arranged.

It ran to a page. He read it in the corridor with his back to the wall.

*In the matter of District Return 487-LL. I have read the objection of this date against the second observer. I sustain it. I am an officer of the Registry of Continuant Works; the Registry is the district office before which this return is filed; the Registry received the transfer of the maintenance of the named work on the eleventh day of C.Y. 486; the Registry has an interest in the filing of this return succeeding; and no statement I have made or can make on this instrument is capable of separating that interest from the outcome of it. A separation of interest is a statement. A statement made by an officer of an interested office is a statement by the interested office, whatever the officer's private accuracy. I record that I put a separation of my interest in the margin of this return on the seventh day, that it was four sentences long, that it was in my own words, and that I do not believe it is worth what it appears to be worth. I remain an observer of this return. I do not withdraw, and I understand that the ground of my remaining is that this return is reviewed and not bound, and that it is reviewable by a body which may hold my separability open for as long as it likes. I ask the reviewing officer to do exactly that.*

Owen read the last sentence four times.

She had not defended herself. She had not argued that the challenge was wrong, and she had not withdrawn, and she had not asked Owen to find her a replacement; she had taken the whole of it onto herself, in her own hand, on the day, against herself, and she had converted the return's one weakness into the thing the Office would have to hold open, and she had done it by giving the Office exactly the excuse it wanted and putting it in writing where a reviewer could not lose it.

And she had done it first. Before Voss, before Owen, before the ward, before the counter at the annex. First, on the day, in her own hand.

He went up and found her at the tray with the lamp not lit yet and the hall almost empty, and he stood at the end of the board and said:

"That was the correct thing to do."

"Yes."

"It was also the only thing."

"Yes." She put the pen down. "Are you going to say something about the other thing?"

"I don't know what the other thing is."

"You've been at it all day like a man with a stone in his shoe and you have not once looked at your own notebook, and you have had it in your coat all day, and you have checked the clasp four times, and I know what is in it because I was on a wet step in the first week of the month and you told me what was in it out loud, and I have never told anybody and I am not going to, and there is no reason in the world for me to be standing near you this afternoon except that I know what is in a notebook and you are the only person in this building who keeps one."

The hall was very quiet, and it was about four o'clock on a Tuesday in the third week of the rain season, and there were nineteen clerks in it, and Owen Rusk stood at the end of a trestle table and looked at a woman of twenty-two.

"Thank you," he said.

"Go and find out about the review."

"I found it out at ten to five from the stub. It's adjourned to the twenty-fifth, because the observers are in question, and the twenty-fifth is the next day a reviewing officer is in, and I have three days, and in three days Pike has a Friday."

"Yes," said Pell. "That is what I would have told you at five o'clock."

He got as far as the stair and then he went back, because there was a thing he had not said and had been carrying since about four o'clock the previous afternoon and had not admitted he was carrying, and because he was, at the end of a month in which he had been right about a great many things, going to say one out loud for the first time in his life to the person it was about.

"I have been keeping a notebook the way this Office keeps registers," he said.

Pell looked at him.

"Yes," she said. "You have."

"And I did not know that until about a minute ago, and I want to say that it is not a comfort, because it is not a comfort, and I have been frightened of it for a fortnight, and it has taken a woman in a different office to point out that the honest description of a register is *a register*, and it is at least honest about what it is, and I have been carrying a thing that was honest about what it was and I did not know I was carrying it."

"Which is what everybody finds out," said Pell. "About four months after they have been frightened. Most people find it out from a lawyer." She picked her pen up. "Go home, Rusk. You are going to be no use to anybody for the next three days and neither am I, and the only thing either of us has to do is be ordinary."

He went home upriver in the rain. On the ground floor of the Registry building, on a table in a long hall with a cold press, District Return 487-LL was open in a rack with a challenge against it in four paragraphs, and a page in a junior clerk's hand sustaining the challenge in her own words, and a margin in a counter clerk's hand that said twice that the interest was not separable and once that the only reason it was tolerable was that it was in ink where a reviewer could find it.

The review was adjourned to the twenty-fifth. The instrument was still an instrument. And Owen understood, walking upriver with his hand in a glove, that it had not survived the morning because he had done anything to it at all.

It had survived because two people he had never once been able to pay had put the worst fact about it in their own handwriting, in public, on the same afternoon, and had then gone back to work.
