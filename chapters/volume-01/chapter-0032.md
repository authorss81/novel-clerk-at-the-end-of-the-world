# Chapter 32 — Why He Signed the First One

He wrote the question on the Sunday morning at his own table, with the good plate on it and the lamp on and the rain going at the window, and it took him until about eleven because the hard part was not the question.

The hard part was the room.

He had learned in six weeks that the Registry of Continuant Works will answer a question in a corridor and will not answer it in a hall, and that the difference between the two is not courage. It is that a corridor has no clerk in it. A thing said in a corridor is a thing two men know happened, and either of them may at a later date be asked about it, and a careful man with nineteen years of Fridays behind him has a very good answer ready for that question, which is that he does not recall it.

So the question had to be asked where somebody was writing, and the somebody could not be Owen Rusk.

He went through the possibilities at the table with a pencil and did not write any of them down, because a list of the ways in which a Registry clerk might get a Deputy Registrar to answer a question on the record is a list of things a Registry clerk has thought about, and he had learned in the fourth week what his own handwriting in a private book does to that book.

The right answer was the oldest thing in the building and it was not clever at all. In an office, a thing is on the record when a person with a duty to write it has written it. A runner from the Deputy Registrar's office carries a note-book for the district office's file, and a runner who has been handed a sheet of paper with a question on it, a date on it, and a matter number on it, is a runner who has to write down what is said in answer to it, because that is what the sheet is for, and because a runner who does not do it has a conversation with his own office about it afterwards.

The other half of it was harder and it was the half that mattered.

He could not put a question *in the docket*. He knew that. He was a co-petitioner, and a co-petitioner is a petitioner and is not an advocate, and there was no application in front of anybody, and if he filed a question into a docket that had no hearing in it he would be doing in public on Tuesday the thing he had spent a fortnight explaining to a man in a hall about a notice with a pointer in it.

So he was going to stand in a corridor and ask a man a question in front of a person with a pencil, and there was going to be a sheet, and the sheet was going to have the day and the hour and the matter number on it, and the runner was going to have to write the answer down, and none of it was going to be a filing, and all of it was going to end up in a drawer.

And then he sat at the table for a while and worked out, honestly, that the reason he wanted this was not entirely procedural.

He wanted the man to have to say it in front of somebody.

---

Pike came out of his office at ten past nine on the Monday morning of the forty-second day of the month with his hat under his arm and the door open behind him, and stopped, because the corridor outside the Deputy Registrar's office is ten feet wide and is the only place in that building where a man can stand still without looking idle, and Owen Rusk was in it with nothing in his hands at all.

He had thought about the hands. He had thought about it for a day and a half.

"Rusk."

"Sir."

"You are standing in my corridor with empty hands and you have been standing there long enough to have planned it, and I am going to be a great deal more interested in this than I am going to show." Pike did not go. "What is it."

"I am going to put a question to you at eleven o'clock this morning, on the second floor, in front of a runner from your office, who is going to write the answer down on a sheet with the day and the hour and the matter number on it. And the sheet is going into the district office's file, and I am going to have a copy of it, and anybody in the ward may inspect it, because it is in a file and a file may be inspected."

"You have not asked it yet."

"No."

"Then I am going to say the first thing and you are going to hear it, and then I am going to say the second thing." Pike put the hat down on the window ledge, which was the most informal thing Owen had ever seen him do. "The first thing is that I am not going to be caught. I want you to have that in front of you before you say one word, because if you have spent a day building a manoeuvre and the manoeuvre is the only thing you have, then you have nothing. I have been the Deputy Registrar of this district for long enough that a good part of the business I do is deciding what I am not going to be asked, and I have got very good at it, and you are not better than me at it this morning."

"That is the first thing."

"The second thing is that it does not matter." Pike took the hat back up. "Because a man who can be put in a hall and made to answer has not been caught, he has been *recorded*, and recording is worse, and you know that it is worse, and you have arranged the whole of your morning so that you cannot ask me this in a corridor, which tells me you have worked out that a corridor is worth a great deal to me and I am going to lose it today, and I am going to lose it because you are right that a memorandum would be worse, and I would like you to understand that a memorandum is a thing I write alone, in my own words, with no question on it and no date on it, and in five years somebody in that building is going to read it as the Deputy Registrar's account of a conversation that nobody saw."

He picked up his hat.

"Eleven o'clock," he said. "Do not be early. If you ask me at a quarter to, the runner has not got his book open and he will write it on the back of a hand and I will stand here and tell you it was taken in a corridor."

---

The runner was a girl of about nineteen with a hard-covered note-book and a bad clasp, and Owen had seen her in and out of that corridor all quarter without once having spoken to her, and she came at a quarter to eleven and stood about four feet away with the book shut, which was the correct procedure and which she did without being asked.

Owen gave her the sheet.

It was a single clean page. At the top, in his own square hand: the day, the hour, the matter, *petition 487-GS-19, and the return filed therein, District Return 487-LL*. Then a line, and under the line the question, and under the question a space ruled for the answer and a space ruled for the officer, and at the foot of the page a line that said *this sheet is not a filing and is not an application and is to be entered in the district office's file for the matter named above.*

He had written that last line on the Sunday night, and it was the only sentence on the page that he was proud of, and he was not proud of it for the reason he would have wanted to be.

"Thank you," said the runner.

"Read it."

She read it. It took her about half a minute and she read it all of it, including the last line, and then she said: "I have to read it out loud before you ask it, and I have to write the day and the hour myself, and then I have to keep the book open on the page for the whole of the answer."

"Yes."

"And nobody puts a mark on it, sir. Not you and not him. If you put a mark on it then it is a document and I have to carry it to a desk and give it a number, and if I give it a number then it is a filing and a filing has to be answered in writing by somebody, and that is not what either of you wants."

"That is correct."

"Then say the question."

There were about forty people on the second floor and the copying floor is one long hall, so the four of them — Pike, Owen, the runner, and Junia Pell at the second desk who had come back from the tray and had not sat down — were in the middle of a working room at eleven o'clock on a Monday morning, and the four words *a claim to a status* went through Owen's head at about half past ten the night before and came back now, and he held it, because a claim to a status is not a claim to a duty, and the whole of the six weeks had been that sentence, and it was on the record in a reviewing officer's own hand and it was not going to be in his mouth in this hall.

He asked the question.

"Mr Pike. In the flood year of the Concord Year 474, this district withdrew the return of Lower Lock. Why was that instrument signed."

The runner's pencil was moving. He watched it move for about a second and a half and then he looked up at Pike and stopped watching, because a man who watches a pencil is a man who is afraid of the answer.

Pike did not answer for some time. He put his hat on the table, and turned it once, and did the thing that Owen had seen him do in the corridor outside the seal stair in the first week of the month, which was to put a flat hand on a surface, and then took it off again, which he had never done.

"There is a bank at the bottom of Fetter Lane," he said, "four hundred yards of it, and it is not a bank, it is a spoil of forty years of somebody else's spoil, and a district engineer put a wire across it and marked it and wrote a finding on it on the fourth day of the second month of that year that said the whole of it would go in a flood and that it would go in a flood that year, and he was right and he was right early, which is the worst combination there is."

"How many people were on it?"

"One hundred and eighty-one. That is not my figure. That is the ward's own count, taken by the ward, at the time, because I sent a clerk to take it and the clerk came back with a number and could not tell me the name of the street it was on."

"How long."

"Nine days. The finding was on the fourth and the water was in it on the thirteenth and there were one hundred and eighty-one people on four hundred yards of it nine days after the wire went across, and the ground was clear."

Pike stopped, and looked at the runner's book, and Owen understood that the man was checking that the sentence was going down in the right words, and understood that this was the most frightening thing he had ever watched a Deputy Registrar do.

"I signed a withdrawal of this district's return on the ninth, on the engineer's finding, under my own hand, and it cleared the ground, and it worked, and it was the right instrument, and I have never for one second doubted that it was the right instrument, and if you ask me in a room in five years whether I would sign it again I will say yes, and I would like that said in a room with somebody in it, because it is going to be the sentence that decides what happens to me and I am telling you now that it is true."

The hall went on copying. Somewhere behind them a press went.

"And the cost," said Owen.

"The cost is twenty-two," said Pike.

He said it flatly, at ordinary volume, in a hall of forty people, and the runner wrote it down, and Owen found that he had to put his hand on the edge of the second desk.

"Twenty-two dead," Pike said. "In Mill Ward and on the Havelock bank, in the same water, on the seventh day, and not one of them on the ground I had cleared. All of them on ground that was not cleared, because the ground behind them belonged to districts that had not come into this office with anything for two quarters and had a return that was failing and a district office that could not compel a single man to move a single chair, because a district office cannot compel anybody of anything, that is the entire difference between a district office and a river."

"Why is that not in the instrument?"

"Because the instrument is about a place."

He said it the way a man says a thing he has said to himself a great many times and never to anybody.

"Your return, Mr Rusk, claims a duty. That is a different universe from what I did. A duty is somebody's own act and it can be performed again on a Tuesday by anybody who signed it, and it can be withdrawn by the person who signed it, and it costs the office nothing and it asks nothing of anybody who is not in it, and it is the only kind of claim I have ever been able to stand behind without lying. What I signed in the second month of the Concord Year 474 was a withdrawal of a place. And a withdrawal of a place is a statement about where a thing is and who is standing on it, and the instrument records what was done to the place, and what was done to the place was that four hundred yards of it was cleared and one hundred and eighty-one people were moved off it and the bank was not there any more. And the twenty-two are not a finding about the instrument. The twenty-two are the river. The twenty-two are what a river does. And if you put the twenty-two in the instrument then every man who opens that file for the next hundred years is going to read them as the fault of the instrument, and they will not be, and the reason they are not is that the instrument is the only thing anybody had, and that is not a defence of the instrument, it is an account of what the district was in the second month of that year."

Owen stood at the end of the second desk and had a sentence in his mouth that he had brought up the hill and it would have been the best sentence of his life and he did not say it, because it was *and you hid them inside a favourable reading*, and it was true, and it was the whole of Saturday's paragraph at the Glass Stair restated in a different room, and if he said it in this hall with a runner's pencil going then the next thing that happened would be a fight about whether the word *hid* was fair, and he would win that fight in about ninety seconds and he would have spent the only thing in the building that could not be taken back.

He said instead: "The file records the hundred and eighty-one and not the twenty-two."

"The file records the hundred and eighty-one and not the twenty-two," said Pike, "because I wrote the file, and I wrote the file in the way this office writes every file, which is by what is in it. And what was in it was a wire, a finding, a clearance and a number. There is no line in it that anybody could have written twenty-two into, because there is no column for it, and because a number of dead in a district that has no return is not a finding of anybody's instrument, it is a fact about a river, and this Office does not keep a column of facts about rivers." He stopped. "I have read that sentence back to myself a great many times and I have never once been able to make it sound like what it is, and what it is, is that I read my own file the way this office reads every file, and the reading was favourable, and I signed it. I did not lie. I want that in the sheet. Put it in."

The runner looked up.

"Both parts?"

"Both parts," said Pike. "Write that he asked me and I answered, and write that I did not lie, and write that I would sign it again, and write the number and write the number, and do not put it in one place and not the other, because a sheet with the hundred and eighty-one on one line and the twenty-two on another is how a document becomes two documents."

Pell had not moved. Owen turned his head about a quarter of an inch and saw her hands flat on the board in front of her and the pen in her right hand and the pen not moving at all, and he understood that she had not written one word of it, and understood afterwards, on the walk down the hill, that she would not, and that there was a reason for it that he was not going to think about on a Monday.

---

Afterwards, in the corridor, Pike put his hat on and said one more thing, and he said it to the wall, because he was a man who said things to walls.

"You have taken the corridor out of this building."

"I have."

"I want you to understand what that is worth to me and I am not going to tell you, because you already know, and a man who does not know what he has lost has not lost it." He turned round. "And I want it written somewhere that you did not catch me at anything. You made me choose a form and I chose the one with a date on it. You put a piece of paper in a girl's hand and you made a room full of clerks hear me say a number I have never said out loud, and I could have refused, and I did not refuse, and if anybody in that building tells you tomorrow that you took a Deputy Registrar by surprise in a corridor, they will be lying and I will say so in writing."

"The runner's sheet will say it."

"The runner's sheet will say what I said," said Pike. "It will not say that I could have refused. That is in this, and this is me, and you will want to write it down and I would think twice."

---

The ward hall at half past six on the Monday evening had a stove going and about nineteen people in it, and Owen Rusk was at the long table with a sheet of paper in his own square hand which was the first draft of the ward's hearing statement, and which he had read four times on the way up and had not once managed to make shorter.

Marguerite Bray was at the table with the delivery book open. Ada Rusk was at the end of it with her sleeves rolled. Ilse Mern was by the stove with her back to the wall the way she stood.

He read the paragraph.

"There is a finding of the district engineer of the fourth day of the second month of the Concord Year 474, and a withdrawal of this district's return signed by the Deputy Registrar on the ninth of that month, on which four hundred yards of the bank at the bottom of Fetter Lane were cleared, and one hundred and eighty-one persons of this ward were moved off it before the flood of the thirteenth, and the ground was clear. Twenty-two persons were drowned in the same flood elsewhere in this district on ground which was not cleared, and were not within the scope of the instrument, and the instrument does not record them, and the office's own record of the withdrawal does not record them. The district notes this and does not claim that the withdrawal was wrong."

He had got to the end of it and he was, in the way he had become used to and did not enjoy, briefly and privately elated. It was the best sentence he had written in a month. It was true, it was short, it had two numbers in it, and it was about the Office and not about a bridge.

Ada Rusk read it twice. She read it the way she read everything, which was slowly, and once more than was needed, and with her hand not on the page.

"Strike it," she said.

"Mrs Bray—"

"No, Mr Rusk, in my own words. Strike it out." Ada did not raise her voice. "You have written a paragraph about a flood that happened thirteen years ago in a ward that was not this ward, because the ward that was this ward had been taken out of the record before that and put back in after, and there is not one person in this hall who was standing on that bank. I will read you the paragraph I have got."

She put her own sheet on the table. It was in her capitals, and it was four lines, and there was no number in it at all.

"**This ward was asked to lose its standing once, in a flood year, so that a bank could be cleared, and the bank was cleared. It is being asked a second time, in a form, by an office, and it is going to be asked a third time in a week by a man who has never been in this ward. Every time it is asked, the answer is the same, and the answer is always that somebody else clears the ground. This ward has stopped clearing ground. The four houses on Fetter Lane are on the standpipe at four in the morning and the crew's wage is four months in arrears and the crossing is shut to loads and the repair is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and unfunded, and that is what this ward is doing while the office asks it to do the fourth thing.**"

Nobody said anything for a moment, and the stove ticked.

"That is worse than yours," said Ada. "Yours has two numbers in it and mine has none, and yours is about a bank, and mine is about this week, and I am the one who has to stand on the top path at seven forty in the rain, and I have read your paragraph four times and every single time it has been about thirteen years ago."

"It is evidence—"

"It is a fact you were given on Monday and you are going to give it back to them on Friday in a room, and when you give it back they are going to hear a Deputy Registrar explain why he was right in a flood year, and they are going to be quiet, and about a third of them are going to think *well, he was right in a flood year*, and the two thirds who do not think that are going to think *and he read his own file the way the office reads every file*, and both of those thoughts are going to happen in the same three seconds, and I am not going to be standing in that room." She turned the sheet over and put it face down, which is a thing she had learned from Marguerite Bray and it is the only thing she has learned from her. "And I am not saying do not give it to them. Give it to them. Give all of it, the hundred and eighty-one and the twenty-two, and let them have it. But do not let it be the argument. The argument is the standpipe."

Marguerite Bray looked at the two sheets on the table for a while.

"The engineer's number is worth having," she said. "I have been in this ward thirty-six years and I have never once been told what the four hundred yards was, and now I know, and it goes in the book with the day on it." She put her pencil down. "And the hearing statement is the ward's, and the ward's is mine to rule, and I am going to rule both, in both hands, in the same page, and rule the day, and rule the hour I am ruling it now, which is the hour after the hour you asked it in."

---

Owen Rusk walked down the hill at ten past seven on the Monday evening of the forty-second day of the month in the rain, and he was carrying a sheet with his own paragraph on it and a page with Ada Rusk's four lines on it, and the second one was going into the ward's book and the first one was going nowhere at all.

And the thing he found on the way down, in about two hundred yards, standing in a lane above a river with a rolled umbrella in his hand like a man waiting for a bus, was not a feeling and was not an argument. It was a piece of arithmetic he had been putting off since the second hour of the Monday afternoon, and he did it standing in the lane and it took about twenty seconds.

The return the ward holds did not exist before the flood. The return had been withdrawn in the Concord Year 474 and the ward had gone most of a decade without one, and it had been filed again, and it had been paid — a maintenance grant against the named work, in writing, on a published schedule, which is a schedule Owen has read in a record room two floors below these counters — for three years, and then the money stopped and the returns lapsed and the transfer came. The instrument Owen Rusk had spent six weeks defending as a piece of civic plumbing was, in its origin, a piece of civic damage, put back by an office that was ashamed of what it had done in a flood year and had wanted one thing on a page that said it had been right.

Which meant that the best thing in Lower Lock's file was also a consequence, and that the office that had produced the precedent, and the schedule, and the hundred and forty pounds, had produced them because of twenty-two people dead on a bank in Mill Ward.

He stood in the lane and understood that this did not make the precedent wrong. It made the precedent a thing a frightened office had built for itself out of its own dead, and he was going to have to live in a building where that was true, and he was going to have to argue there about a refusal column in a form somebody's grief had made shorter, and he was not going to be able to say the second half out loud in a hall, because Ada Rusk had just told him the whole of what a hall can hold in three seconds, and she was twenty-four, and she had been in this ward her whole life, and he had been arguing about a bridge since the first day of the month.

He got to the bottom of the lane and turned round and looked back up it, at the wet roofs of the ward, and at the hill, and then went in to his dinner, because there were two hundred and eleven sheets of comparison waiting on the second floor and the hall empties at six.

The order was still on a docket with no hearing date on it. And the return was due on Friday.
