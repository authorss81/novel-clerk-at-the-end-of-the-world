# Chapter 27 — The Standard Was Issued

On the Sunday the building was cold and empty and he was the only man in it, and he spent the day on a rule.

He had spent a month being governed by a sentence nobody could name. It had a shape — a second officer's countersignature before a filing is acted on — and it had no number, and it had no issuing office, and he had asked in the first week for both and been given the same answer twice in a form so polite that it was a wall, and he had gone on living under it the way you live under a load you cannot see.

What he had learned about it in a month was that it had ruined his month. Not unfairly. Correctly. It was a sound rule, applied in the correct form, by careful people, and every single thing that had gone wrong in four weeks had gone wrong because of it: because a return needed a second officer, and the second officer's interest was the Office's, and the Office's interest was in the filing succeeding, and therefore a ward which had done everything right was standing in a hall on a Friday waiting for a document that could not be written.

Owen Rusk sat in an empty hall on the Sunday of the fourth week of the rain season and asked himself a question he had been carrying since the third day of the month.

*A standard is issued by somebody. And where it is issued, it is recorded. Nothing that binds three hundred people is issued into a void. There is a book somewhere with the thing in it and the date on it and the reasons on it, and if I cannot find the book then the book is not being consulted, and if the book is not being consulted then nine years of clerks in this building have all agreed, without ever saying so, that a rule does not need to be readable in order to be obeyed.*

He got to the answer at about two in the afternoon and he got it the way he has got every answer in a month, which is by being made to wait in a room.

---

The district office keeps a **Schedule of Instruments in Force**. It is not a public document. It is not on any counter. It is a working list, kept in the district office, in one hand, revised as instruments are issued and withdrawn, and it exists because an office cannot apply a rule it cannot find and cannot tell a member of the public what binds them.

It is also, Owen worked out on the ferry, the one thing in this building that is *required* to answer his question, and the reason is not legal subtlety. It is that a Council instrument must be discoverable by anyone it binds, or the binding is void; that is not a doctrine, it is a condition; and the way a Council makes itself discoverable to a hundred and eighty thousand people it has never met is to be findable. The Council does not send anybody round. The Council writes itself into a list and keeps the list, and the reason it keeps the list is that a person who is affected by a thing is entitled to be able to find the thing.

Which means: if the countersignature review standard is in force in Docketford, there is a line in a book with its issue reference on it.

He went and asked for it on the Monday morning of the thirty-fifth day of the month, and the clerk at the district office's counter, who was a different one again, looked at him for a moment and then went and got the schedule without arguing, and Owen noticed that she had not argued and thought about that for a quarter of an hour afterwards.

**Item 14. Second examination of community instruments.** *Issued by the Provisional Council. Issued in the Concord Year 44. In force in all district offices. Answering to no local office. Superseding: nothing. Superseded: nothing. Amended: never.*

Four hundred and forty-three years old. Never amended. Owen read that four times.

And then, under the line, in the same hand, in smaller writing, a heading he had to turn the page sideways to read, and which was not part of the schedule at all and had been added in a different ink in a different decade:

*Recitals, as recorded at issue. An instrument of the Council binding district offices is discoverable with its reasons. Grounds:*

And then a paragraph, and it is the paragraph that took his week, and it is worth setting out entire because it is the sentence that explains four weeks of Owen Rusk's life and is nine lines long.

*That returns of a public work made by districts were, in the years of the flood closures, produced in numbers and in haste which local offices could not examine within the quarter in which they were made; that such returns were commonly made by a person with an interest in the work to be supported and commonly witnessed by persons of the same district; that the office could not distinguish, upon the face of the instrument, a duty honestly performed from a status merely asserted; and that it was therefore necessary that every such instrument be examined a second time by an officer having no district, before it be acted upon.*

Owen Rusk read that at a counter on the second floor of a Registry building on the Monday morning of the thirty-fifth day of the month and had to put his hand on the counter.

It was **right**. That was the thing that went through him like cold water, and it went through him because he had spent four weeks with the other version in his hands — *somebody didn't lie, and that is worse* — and he had had the whole of his month built on the proposition that the rule was a thing the Office had invented to be difficult with.

It was not. It was a body of people in Concord Year 44 looking at a genuine, documented, repeated failure — returns asserted rather than performed, witnesses of the same district, offices that could not tell the difference on the face of the paper — and writing down the smallest thing that would fix it. Not a rule about who is right. A rule about who looks twice.

*That the office could not distinguish, upon the face of the instrument, a duty honestly performed from a status merely asserted.*

That is Owen's case. That is the entire case, in a sentence, written down by careful people four hundred and forty-three years before he arrived, and the sentence is a complaint about *instrumentation*, and the whole of the last four weeks of his life has been an attempt to make the office able to tell the difference — and it has worked, and the only reason it is still stuck is that the second pair of eyes who is supposed to be able to tell the difference works in the building that has the interest.

He read the recitals out loud, twice, in a corridor, to nobody.

---

The next part took the rest of the morning and the whole of the afternoon and it is the part that explains him.

A standard that requires a second officer requires there to be a supply of second officers. The supply is called the **roll of countersignature**, and it is not kept by the Council and it is not kept by the district office. It is kept by the River Desk, on the second floor, in a drawer with a printed card on it, and Owen had walked past that drawer every working day for nine years and had thought of it as the drawer that junior clerks are put in when they are new.

He went and got it. He was permitted to see it, on the ground that the roll is a list of names and a list of names is not a record of a person, which is the same ground on which the Office had given him the schedule of a redaction batch on the Monday, and which he noted, with a small cold feeling of recognition, as the second time in a month that the cheapest thing in the building had turned out to be the right door.

Nineteen names.

Nineteen officers in the whole of Docketford on the roll of countersignature, and eight of them were in other districts, and the roll is a *district-exclusion* roll: an officer may countersign for a district they do not live in, and may not countersign for a district they do, and the appointment is made by the district office holding the return, out of the nineteen, by name, in writing, and is a **continuing appointment** that does not go with the posting and is not ended by moving house and has to be withdrawn in writing by the officer's own hand.

He read the nineteen names and the appointments and the dates, and the dates went back twenty-six years, and on the list, fourth, with an appointment dated three years and four months ago, in a hand he knew from a margin on the seventh day of the month:

**PELL, J. — second desk, River Desk. Appointed by district office, Mill Ward annex and River Desk, in the matter of a return from Havelock Reach. Continuing.**

Owen stood in the copying hall and read his own life off a card.

Nine years at a desk. The first ten weeks of a clean run, broken by a return that could not be acted on because two people who lived in it were named in it. Four weeks of a ward he lives in. A man at a counter who could not be hurried. A stability order that a careful man would not sign quietly. A district that has the interest. A woman of twenty-two who reads a challenge against herself and sustains it and asks a reviewer to hold her separability open *for as long as it likes*, and the phrase that did that work is the phrase of a rule that requires a second officer from outside the district, and the rule is four hundred and forty-three years old and has never been amended, and it was written by people who were right.

Not one thing in his month was anybody's villainy. Not one. The transfer was validly made by a tired man. The refusal was a correct sentence about a place. The batch was a backlog cleared in an afternoon. The order was a queue of sixteen, on a word. The rule that has cost Lower Lock everything is a sound response to a documented failure, issued in a year when the flood closures were worse than this one, and it has been applied every single day since by careful people in the correct form, and it is the reason a ward cannot be examined by anybody who lives in the ward.

He sat down on the end of the second desk, which he was not supposed to do.

---

He asked her at eleven in the morning on the Monday, in the copying hall, with about sixty people in it, and he asked it in the open on purpose, because it was a question about an instrument and instruments are public documents and a question asked quietly about a document is a question about a person.

Pell was at the tray. She had nothing in it.

"Junia. Is it all right if I ask you a question in front of everybody. It is about a document and not about you, and I want it asked where it can be repeated."

"Ask it."

"You are fourth on the roll of countersignature," said Owen Rusk. "Your appointment is dated three years and four months ago, it is by the district office, in the matter of a return from Havelock Reach, and it says *continuing*. I want to know three things and I want them answered in this hall. Were you put on it, or did you ask to be put on it. And who put you on it. And can you get off it."

The hall did not stop. About sixty people carry on copying when two clerks at the second desk have a conversation that is not about copying, and what happened was that the conversation happened inside the ordinary noise, which is the correct condition for a public question and which he had chosen deliberately and which she recognised immediately.

Junia Pell put the pen down.

"Three years and four months ago I was nineteen," she said. "I was two floors down from here, I had been in the Registry two years, and I was the fastest typist anybody had seen and I was extremely pleased about it. On a Tuesday a clerk from the Mill Ward annex came down with a return from Havelock Reach and a sheet of paper with a name on it and said the district office needed a second examination and had run through the whole roll and there was nobody free except a nineteen-year-old two floors down, and would I come and do it."

"And you said yes," said Owen.

"I said yes, because I was nineteen and it was an errand and it took an afternoon, and somebody in that room wrote my name down and dated it, and nobody asked me anything."

She picked the pen up and put it down again.

"You were not put on it by a villain and you were not the beneficiary of a scheme. You were the only person in the building who was free on a Tuesday afternoon. That is the whole of how you got there and there is nobody in this city you can be angry at about it, including me, and I would have said yes again."

"And can you get off it."

"That is the part I have thought about for two years," said Pell. "You cannot take yourself off a district-exclusion roll by writing to the district office, because the district office appointed you for a matter and the matter is closed and the appointment is continuing and there is no form for a continuing thing ending except a form the officer signs herself and hands in. So yes. In principle I can write one line and hand it in and I would be relieved of it."

"And then?"

"And then there would be eighteen, and the district office would have to find one, and the eighteenth is in Havelock and the seventeenth is at Coal Hill and one of those two is suspended, and if the next instrument that arrives at this desk needs a second examination and there is no second examiner, the instrument does not go up, and it sits, and it does not sit for long — it sits until a quarter has gone by and somebody writes *lateness* in a column and somebody signs a thing with sixteen names on it." She looked at him. "And if the next instrument is District Return 487-LL, then it is your ward, and it is you, and it will not be a second examination, it will be a file with a gap in it, and the gap will be the reason."

The hall copied. Somewhere behind them a press went.

"So no," said Pell. "I have not written the line. And I want to say the last part in this hall because you have asked it in this hall and I will not answer it in a corridor. I did not choose this. I have never chosen it and I cannot leave it and I did not know it existed until a man asked me about it at eleven o'clock this morning, and everything I have done since — the margin on the seventh, the challenge on the twenty-second, the page I filed against myself — I did all of it *inside* this, and I would have had to do all of it anyway, and the only thing this changes is that I now know what the room is and I have to live in it."

Nobody said anything.

"And one more thing," said Junia Pell, "and then I am going back to the tray, because I have a tray."

"Go on."

"You have been waiting for a month for somebody to come into that hall and say something indefensible," she said. "Somebody who signed the thing, or who made the rule, or who was in the room. And there is not one, and there is going to be one at some point in the next eight weeks if you keep printing things on tables, and I want you to have heard me say, in advance, on a Monday, in a hall, that when it comes it will be a policy with a rationale and a date and a paper behind it, and it will be a *good* policy, and you will not be able to do anything with it, and it will be four hundred and forty-three years old and it will have been right."

Owen Rusk stood in a copying hall and did not answer that, because she was right and there was nothing to say to a person who has just told you a thing about yourself in front of sixty witnesses and then gone back to work.

---

He went to the tray at a quarter to eight that evening because the tray had seven things in it and one of them had to be routed, and he had a bad hour, and then a worse one, because it was a Monday and the copying floor emptied at six and there were four people left in the building and he was doing the day's work out of the drawer the way he had done it every day for a month.

The unrouteable correspondence is the only part of the Registry's daily work that arrives already finished. It has been to a counter. It has been returned. It is going nowhere. It has no schedule. It is the reason a man posted to a tray is in the building at a quarter to eight and it is the one job in this building in which Owen Rusk is the only person who can possibly be right, because nobody else is doing it.

There were nine items on the Monday night, which was two more than usual, and he took the first one off the top of the stack and turned it round to read the address on the face, which is what you do, and he got about half way down the line of the name.

And it did not come.

Not a hesitation. Not a blur. The name on the envelope was right there in the front of him in the office's own small print, and the name — a name he had copied, on an average, four hundred times a quarter, for nine years, in his own hand, on a routing slip — was not there.

Owen Rusk put the envelope down on the board, squared it with two fingers, and looked at the wall, and did not open the notebook, and said, out loud, in an empty hall, in the flat voice:

"Well."

And then he picked it up again and looked at it, and it came back inside a second, and he wrote the routing slip, and it was right.

He put the slip in the tray and went home upriver in the rain, and he thought about it for about two hundred yards and then he stopped thinking about it, because there were two possible explanations and one of them was a man of thirty-two who had been awake since four, and the other one was a thing he could not put in a notebook, and he had promised himself on the sixth day of the month in a warm room that he would not do this to himself again.

He was at the copying floor at ten to seven on the Tuesday morning, and the tray was on the board, and the first envelope was on the top of the stack, and Owen Rusk put his hand on it.
