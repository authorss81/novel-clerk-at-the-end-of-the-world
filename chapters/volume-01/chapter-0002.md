# Chapter 2 — What the Ward Knows

The ward hall smelled of wet wool and the paraffin the stove gave off when it was worked hard, and the first thing Owen learned in it was that he was not going to be allowed to speak.

He had come in through the side door with his coat still streaming because he had walked the last two hundred yards at something close to a run, and he had a piece of paper in his inside pocket that he had been thinking about for three hours, which was the second leaf of an annex he had been told not to need, and he had a plan, and the plan lasted about five minutes.

The meeting was standing room only and it was not about him. It was a crew wage meeting. That was the agenda, chalked on the board in Holm Ferris's big slow letters, because the bridge crew had not been paid on the last two granary loads and Ferris kept the board for anything the hall had to remember. Ada had called it. Ada was in the middle of the room with her back to the fire and a folded drawing under her arm, and the drawing was the thing on everybody's mind, so that Owen understood at once that he had walked into the middle of somebody else's whole afternoon.

"Somebody's in," said a voice near the door. Then somebody laughed, and the voice said, "That's Rusk. That's Owen Rusk, in his good coat."

"Sit down," said Marguerite Bray.

She was at the long table on the left with the ledger open in front of her and a flat brass weight holding the pages, and she had said it without emphasis, the way a woman says sit down to a man who has come into her hall expecting to be asked. Owen sat down. There was a bench behind him and he went backwards onto it and the bench was wet and he did not get up to move.

Marguerite Bray was sixty-one and kept Lower Lock's roll by hand because, she had told the hall twice a year for as long as he could remember, the Office's roll was not fit to write a grocery list on. Her paper roll was nine inches thick and ruled in her own hand and it was, Owen understood sitting on the wet bench, the only record of this ward that had never once been ambiguous.

"Right," she said. "You're here, so you'll hear it. Not because you asked."

The letter had come eight days ago. It was on the table face up under the brass weight, and it was a good letter, that was the trouble with it. It was warm in the way the Office manages to be warm. It noted that the Registry understood that households in marginal wards were often placed at a disadvantage by the cost of maintaining public works, and that in the interests of "better protection" residents were invited to *consolidate claims at the Mill Ward annex*, where a single counter would serve several wards more efficiently than scattered parish arrangements, and where assistance with roofing and water grants could be applied for under one return.

It did not mention a crossing.

Owen read it twice with his hand flat on the table, and the hall watched him read it, and he understood that he was being given a chance to notice something and that nobody in the room believed he would.

Some of them were glad. That was the part he had not been ready for. Ilse Mern, laundress, forty-four, had two children and a roof of tarred board going soft at the ridge, and she wanted the slate. She was not a fool about it. She sat with her hands in her apron and said, out loud, that if the crossing had to belong to somebody with money to keep it, then the crossing had already stopped being hers, and she would rather have a roof over two children than a vote about a bridge. There was a silence after that which Owen understood he would remember for a long time.

Nessa Kray, seventy-one, the seed stall, said the same thing harder. The seed stall was not in the letter. The seed stall was not in the transfer either, so far as anyone in the hall knew, and Kray had noticed that, and had asked about it, and had been told by a counter clerk in Mill Ward that the stall was not a maintenance act and therefore not a matter for consolidation. Kray said she had been selling seed in Lower Lock for forty-one years and had never once needed a Registry to agree that she was a woman who sold things.

Bram Tillet, ferry cook, forty-six, wanted to know what the crossing was for, if nobody in the hall was maintaining it. Who crossed it. Where they went. He asked it seriously, as a man asking about a route, and Ada told him: two hundred and forty people, the Mill Ward allotments, the hospital at Fetter Bend for anybody who could not walk the long way, the school at Coal Hill, and a hundred and nine barrow-loads of stone a year that came over it because it was the only flat way out of Lower Lock before the second lock. And the abutment, she said, had a crack in it that she could put a thumbnail into, and the last estimate for the repair was four thousand one hundred and sixty, and the ward had had that estimate since March.

"Who's paying it?" Tillet asked.

"Nobody," Ada said. "That's the estimate for the repair. Nobody's paying it."

She said it the way she said everything, which was as though the sentence had already been load-tested and had held.

Owen stood up.

He had the piece of paper in his hand. He had rehearsed the thing on the walk upriver — the receipt, the two days, the register, three refusals in the correct form — and it was a good piece of paper, and the room had gone quiet in the way rooms do, and he got as far as the first sentence of it before Ada Rusk turned round.

"No," she said.

"Ada—"

"Brother." She said it the way you say a word you have been using your whole life. "Not in this room."

"I don't need the room. I need you to understand what the Office has done—"

"I understand what the Office did in the autumn." She set the drawing down. "I understand what the Office did, and I understand it's eight days old and it's the reason I'm standing here tonight with nine men who haven't been paid, because if the crossing's a Mill Ward matter then the crossing markers are a Lower Lock matter and the markers are unpaid, and that's the meeting. That's what's on the board. Do you see the board?"

He saw the board.

"You're a clerk," Ada said. "You've got a piece of paper. And I'm the one who has to walk into that annex in the rain and ask a stranger for our wage. So you'll sit there and you won't turn this into something where you get to be the reason I know things."

The hall did not rescue him. That was the part he would think about later, and think about for years, in fact, in other rooms, in other weather. Twenty-odd people looked at a wet bench and a man in a good coat and not one of them said a word. The fire moved. Ferris's chalk letters stood up off the board.

Owen sat back down.

They did the meeting. He stayed for all of it, which was not asked of him and which cost him something he could not have written down, and he learned the ward's actual economy in about an hour, which no entry in any file had ever given him and which he had spent fifteen years believing he already knew.

Two hundred and forty people in thirteen households of record, and the rest of the two hundred and forty — the ones nobody could count, the ones who slept in the sheds behind the granary, the ones who came and went and paid Kray for seed on credit — lived here and would not appear in a district return this century unless somebody wrote them in by hand. The granary held nine weeks in a good year and four in a bad one, and the ward had been having a bad one, and the pump had been hand-primed nine times since the twelfth of last month, and the crossing markers were four months in arrears, and the crew of nine had been working a fifth day on the strength of a letter from the mill about stone that had not arrived.

And then a man at the back stood up, awkwardly, with his hat in his hands.

That was Cobb Rennie, fifty-eight, who lived in the same two rooms he had lived in for nineteen years, on the Fetter Lane side, and who was known to every person in the hall and to the milkman and to the man who came for scrap, and who could not draw his pension, because the pension wanted a record placing him at that address, and there was no record placing him at that address, and there had not been for two years.

Two years. Not eleven months. An earlier quarter, a different review, a different piece of paper with a different number on it, gone out from under him while he was in the ward infirmary with the cough and had come back to find the ward had carried on without him in the only way it knew how, which was to keep his tin cup on the shelf above the pump.

He was not bitter about it. That was the unbearable part. He said he had a room and a widow's pension on the far side that he did not draw, and that he was not asking anybody for anything, and that he was only standing up because the clerk was here and had a piece of paper, and if it was not too much trouble he would like to know whether the same thing was going to happen again in the autumn. And then he sat down.

Bray wrote the name down. That was all. She wrote it in the roll, in pencil, in her own hand, with the date, and she did not ask him to wait, and she did not explain.

Then the meeting broke, and the hall emptied into the rain, and Owen did not go home.

---

He sat at the table until midnight with Bray's roll open in front of him and the good lamp pulled close, and she let him, which is the only way anybody has ever got anything out of her.

He copied.

Not the Office's version. Hers. Every name she had, every act each household actually performed, and the date of the performance, and then the witnesses — two per entry where the ward could supply two, and where it could only supply one he wrote *one* and left the second blank rather than invent it, which is a thing he has never done in an Office book and did in his own that night.

It took until the lamp wanted trimming. It took longer than that. He wrote out thirteen households and then the ones the roll only partly knew, and then the crossings of the same name from three households into one, because a name in two places is one name and an entry that appears twice is two half-facts that will not hold anybody up.

At about two he came to the thing he had been avoiding, which was the entry under Fetter Lane.

Cobb Rennie. Age fifty-eight. Nineteen years at that address, before the infirmary. Present residence, twenty-one years, the same two rooms. Performed act: none recorded. Witnessed act: none. There was no act because there had been nothing to do and nothing to fail at; he had simply been a man in a ward for nineteen years and then, for two of them longer, a man in a ward that the roll could not locate.

Owen looked at the entry for a while, and then he wrote under it, in pencil, in Bray's book and not in his own: *witnessed by H. Ferris, night watch, and by B. Tillet, ferry cook, at ward meeting, both to personal knowledge.* He looked at Bray. She gave one short nod, which from her is a decoration.

That was a good entry. Two living men with separable interests had said to his face that they knew where Cobb Rennie slept, and it was worth nothing whatever, because a ward roll is a thing a ward knows and the pension wants a Registry. Different book. Different year. Different quarter. He could put the whole of it into the proper book on Monday and it would arrive two reviews too late for the man it was about.

"Don't do it twice, though," Cobb Rennie said from the doorway. He had come back for his hat and he had plainly been listening, and he was not unkind about it. "I like being written in the one time. It makes the shelf look honest."

By three he had the whole thing copied into his own notebook, and he was not proud of the notebook, which was new and cheap and had a bad clasp, and he was aware of a thing he would not have admitted at the Fourth Desk: that the Office's version and this version were not the same shape. The Office could tell you a ward's returns, its grants, its rates, its public works, its dates. It could not tell you who carried the pump house key. It could not tell you that a man could go missing for two years and be missed by every single person in the room.

The rain had not stopped. It had changed into the sort of steady that gets into paper and makes the whole building smell like a cellar.

Bray put her hand on the ledger and closed it, and said: "You'll take that back in the morning and they'll ask you what you want the Office to do."

"I know."

"You don't." She stood up, which ended the evening. "You'll go back and you'll want them to undo a thing. And they'll be very glad to hear you, and then next year there'll be a date on a leaf that says it was all a compositor, and we'll be exactly where we are, and you'll have spent a year." She put the brass weight on her ledger. "Nothing in this ward is a compositor. Something came downriver and took three things. You want to know what it was, I'll help you. You'll find out."

---

He walked back upriver through the rain at half past four with the notebook inside his coat against the wet, and at home he took out the second leaf of the annex, because he had promised himself in the copying hall that he would read it, and he had not read it, and a man who makes lists does not get to skip the second item on them.

The no-local-record schedule was smaller than the first leaf and much older in the hand, and it did not carry dates at all. It carried a place and a list.

The place was one line at the top.

*No local record at the named place.*

And the named place was the crossing. The Ninth Weir. Not the ward, not the district, not the granary, not the pump. A structure, a piece of river masonry with a name, and beneath it thirteen lines in a column, and beside each line the same four words.

Ivy Sarr, twelve.
A child of nine, surname Anstruther.
A child of six, whose entry gave a grandmother's name in the place of a mother.
Three people whose column said *unable to answer*, and beside one of them an address instead of a name, the shed behind the granary that Owen knew.

That was six. And then seven adults, and the first of the seven was a name Owen had seen on a crew sheet.

Dory Anstruther, nineteen, apprentice, bridge crew, Lower Lock.

Owen read the sheet twice and then a third time with his finger on the last column, because the phrase was the thing, and the phrase was not *missing*. Nobody had written *missing*. Nobody had written *lost*, or *untraced*, or *presumed*. Thirteen people had been positively and specifically recorded as having **no local record** at a bridge, and every one of the thirteen had been given a name and an age and, in two cases, a home, and the assertion had been made about a place, and the place was a place, and you cannot say *we are here* to a document that is not asking whether you are.

He got his coat off the peg and stood in the cold of his own front room for a while with the sheet in his hand, and then he put it back inside the oiled paper with the other leaf, because a clerk who carries a document loose in the rain is a clerk who has stopped thinking.

He slept two hours. In the morning he would go downriver and ask, in the interview alcove, of the man who had to answer: who had stood behind it.
