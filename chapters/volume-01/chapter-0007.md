# Chapter 7 — Performing It

On the ninth day Owen found that nobody in the ward would agree to witness anything, and that this was not cowardice, and that it took him most of a day and half the night to learn the difference.

The first refusal came from Ilse Mern, at the pump, at seven in the morning, with her sleeves rolled.

"I'll say what I saw," she said. "I'll say it here, to your face, to Marguerite, to the man from the mill. I saw the pump start on the fourth and the pump start on the ninth and I've primed it myself with my own hands and I'd put my name to that in front of the Council." She wrung a rag out. "And the ward signed a paper on the seventh that gave the crossing to Mill Ward, and if I put my name under a return as a witness of the ward's crossing, then somewhere in a building full of drawers there'll be a sheet with my name on it saying the ward performed a duty this week, and the same building will have a sheet saying the ward gave the duty away on the seventh, and one of those two is a lie and I know which one I'd rather be able to say out loud in a room."

"That's not the question I'm asking."

"It's exactly the question you're asking." She hung the rag on the post. "You want somebody to hold the ward's coat."

The ninth day went like that. Owen went round Lower Lock with a sheet and a pencil and asked everybody who had put a hand to one of the four things to witness a duty their own household was part of, and every one of them found a way to be kind and to say no, and the reasons were not all the same, and the reasons were the most useful thing he learned that day.

Bram Tillet would not, because he had voted against the transfer, and a man's refusal of one thing is a man's refusal, and he would not put his name under a return the four of them had voted against. Holm Ferris would not, because the watch is paid out of the ward and a man cannot hold a lamp for both sides. Nessa Kray, seventy-one, would not, because the seed stall is in neither leaf of that annex, and she has kept a thing outside the Registry for forty-one years and is not joining one at the end of them. Wenna Sarr would not, and did not need to be asked twice, and gave a reason Owen wrote down word for word because it was the sixth of the six: *I am not a witness. I am the only grown person that child has got.* Ada was not asked, and did not need to be; she was one of the four who had said no, and a woman who has said no once in a room does not say it twice in a kitchen.

He came back to the hall at four with the sheet almost empty and sat down, and Marguerite Bray read it upside down and put it face down and said, in the voice she used for the delivery book, "Well. Now you know the shape of the job."

He came back to the hall at four with the sheet almost empty and sat down, and Marguerite Bray read it upside down and put it face down and said, in the voice she used for the delivery book, "Well. Now you know the shape of the job."

---

So he wrote to two people, which took him the whole of the evening, and both of the answers came back the same day, and one of them came back in person.

Pell's came first. He had filed his inquiry in his own name and it was on a visible docket, and the docket number was on the stub, and he wrote four lines on a slip and gave it to the girl on the second desk rather than carry it to her table, because he had watched her for three weeks and understood that a thing carried to a table is a claim and a thing left on a corner is an offer.

*Lower Lock proposes a district return naming the maintenance of one public crossing. The work is being performed and is being performed now. Two living witnesses with separable interests are required, and the ward cannot supply them. Will you witness acts only, without reference to the drafting, and separate your interest in writing?*

She read it standing up. She read it twice.

"You drafted that."

"I wrote it."

"You know the difference." She did not smile, and Owen understood that she was checking rather than making a point. "And what you're asking me to sign is a ward's duty instrument. In the district office's language, that is an officer of this Office on the outside of a community return."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know it gets attacked at the *witness* line, not the *act* line, and that when somebody in a review room asks me whether I was independent, the true answer is that I saw four acts on a riverbank and a lamp in a pump house and did not know what they were for. That is true, and it will not be enough for four seconds, and then he will ask whether I was told the answer first."

"Were you?"

"No," said Junia Pell. "And I have thought about it for a day and a half, which is longer than it has taken me to decide anything in three weeks on this floor. I am not doing it for you and I am not doing it for the ward. I am doing it because it is the only piece of work anyone has handed me since I got here that is not already decided." She picked up her pen. "I will separate my interest myself, in my own words, and you will not write it, and if you try I will not sign."

"That is entirely fair."

"It is not fair at all. Don't do that." She went back to her tray. "Bring me the acts. I'll witness what's done, not what's claimed."

Voss's came back on a written note the same afternoon, brought by a boy from the annex counter, and it was four lines and no greeting.

*W. Voss, counter clerk, Mill Ward annex. I will witness acts I personally see, at the crossing and the pump, on my own time and not office time, and I will put my reservation in the margin in my own words. My district received the maintenance of that crossing eleven months ago and has not performed it. That is a cost my district has carried and not used, and I will not pretend the interest is not there. I am also not prepared to sit at a counter and read again that a bridge I can walk to in ten minutes has no district behind it. If the ward performs, I will say so. If the ward stops performing, I will say that too.*

*Send the page.*

Owen read it four times and had to put it down and walk to the window.

---

The tenth day began at four in the morning, because the pump prime was at four, and the pump was the act the ward had chosen to lead with, and the reason was practical: the pump had failed on the sixth and been hand-primed on the seventh, the eighth and the ninth, and if the ward was going to file anything it was going to file about the thing that had to be done at four in the morning by two people who turned out whatever the weather did.

So Owen was standing on a pump house step at four in the morning in the rain with a lamp when Ada Rusk came up the lane with a wrench over her shoulder and two of the crew behind her, and one of them was nineteen years old and had a name on a schedule in a drawer in Docketford.

"Name," said Owen.

"What?"

"Your name. To the act. Not to the return. What's your name?"

The boy looked at him as though the question were in a foreign language he was making a generous effort with. "Dory Anstruther."

"Spelled the same on the crew sheet?"

"There's only one of me."

"Dory. Two things and then you may hate me. One, I am not asking you to sign anything tonight and you will not be asked by me. Two, I have written a thing about you in a private book, and if it ever comes up it will come from me and not from a leaf, and I would rather you heard it now than in a review room."

"Say it."

"Your name is the first of the seven adults on a schedule that says there is no local record of you at that crossing." Owen said it the way you read a figure. "It is dated last autumn, it was signed by two officers of this Office, it is false, and I have a docket open saying so, in my own name, on the public book. I have not proved it yet. I am working on it. But it is out in the world, and I would rather you knew a man in a good coat had read it than that you found it in a year."

The boy was seventeen-looking in that light with rain in his hair, and he thought about it for three seconds, which was longer than Owen expected and better than he had any right to.

"That's the Dory in Ada's crew," he said. "There's only one of that as well." Then he went past him, got the priming bar out of its socket, and worked harder for the next four hours than anybody else on the pump.

Which was the first act. After it there was a marker to go up on the weir path, chalk and paint, because the water was up and the marker had been down since the flood and the crew had been waiting three weeks for a day they could set it without a man standing in the river. Then the granary accounts, because the season's second weighing had been done on the seventh with nobody from the office there, and the counts were in Mern's hand and Tillet's and Bray's, and the double count matched, and the hundred and nine barrow-loads of stone a year did not, because there had been no stone in the quarter and the line had been carried forward by somebody in an office with a pen.

Owen wrote that down too, and when Ada read it she said, "That's a fourth thing, and nobody has claimed it since the flood."

"Three and the seed stall."

"Three and the seed stall. Three's not a ward, three's a list, and if we're filing a return for a list then the day something's missing off it we're a short return and we're back where we started. The thing that holds a ward together isn't the things it keeps." She looked up the lane. "So the fourth thing. If we're doing this we do it properly or not at all, and properly means every person in this hall who did one of these things this week goes down as a separate person with his own act and his own page, and each of them can look at his own page and say yes or no, and if one says no he is out and the rest are still in. That's how a household is. That's how anything with more than one person in it is."

---

The return was drafted branch by branch. It took the rest of that day and most of the night and it was four times the work Owen had allowed himself, and it went the way Nell had said it would go: three of the nine wanted their own page.

They wanted it in terms Owen had never heard in nine years of copying. Not *private* — that was a word from the Mill Ward, and the three of them had heard it and disliked it. What Ilse Mern said was: "I'll not have my name on a page with anybody else's on it. There's nine of us and four who said no, and the minute a page's got a yes on it and a no on it and the two are the same sheet, in ten years there's a clerk reads that page and says the ward did it and the ward didn't. I want my own page. I want to be able to hand it in on its own."

They were right, and it took him a moment to see why, and it was a moment he wanted back afterwards: a page with a yes and a no on it is not a record of two people, it is a record of an argument, and an argument is the one thing a return can never be built out of.

So nine sheets, one adult to a sheet, no shared line anywhere, the three who had asked got their pages and the other six got theirs, and nobody was asked to sign for anybody. There was no proxy clause on any of the nine and no witness line a householder could fill, and at the foot of the return, in Owen's hand and Marguerite's, a note:

*A branch may be withdrawn by the person in it. Withdrawal removes that branch. It does not remove the return.*

He wrote that in the margin before he wrote anything else, because a man who has been handed a tool by a woman in a kitchen deserves to use it correctly the first time.

The scope took the rest of the night and the better part of the morning, and it was the part of the whole business that mattered most.

They fought about the word *crossing* for two hours. Owen wanted it in the first line, in the title, in the subject box, in the act, in the effect, and he wanted *the Ninth Weir is maintained by this ward* in a form that could be copied into a claim by anybody who ever needed it. He wanted it because he had learned, since the first of the month, that a thing which is not named cannot be held by anybody, and because a document that says *public works* can be made to mean whatever the next clerk needs it to mean.

Ada beat him with a sheet of paper and a wet marker boot.

"Try it," she said. "Read me the version where it says the crossing is maintained."

He read it. He had written it twice by then, and the second version was good, and it said: *The Ninth Weir public crossing is maintained by this district. It is load-restricted to vehicles above four tons and closed to loaded traffic pending structural assessment. Its maintenance is the duty of this district.*

"Last line," Ada said.

"That last line is true. The maintenance is ours, if we file it."

"The maintenance is ours." She sat down on the step. "Say the other one. Go on, read me the first line again."

"Maintenance of the Ninth Weir public crossing, a named work at the named place, is performed by the household branches of this district—"

"There it is." She stood up. "Say *is*. Go on."

"—is performed by the household branches of this district, at the river path, on the days recorded, by the persons recorded, before witnesses recorded; the crossing is closed to loaded traffic; the repair of the abutment is not performed by this district and is not claimed by it; and this return claims nothing as to the safety, condition, or use of the crossing, which no witness in this return is competent to speak to."

He stopped.

"There," Ada said, with satisfaction. "Now the second half of that does the work and you will not like it. *This return claims nothing as to the safety, condition, or use of the crossing.* You write that and every fool in this ward is going to read it and say you've disowned the bridge, and a fool is right, and it is the only sentence in the document that keeps nine of us out of a review room in a year's time. We are not saying the bridge is safe. We haven't got a single person here who could say that and be honest. We're saying we are the ones who will keep doing the thing we do to it, and here's when we did it, and here are the two people who watched us, and if the thing we do is not good enough then the district that can do it better is welcome to come and do it, and we will hand them the marker and the wrench and the key to the pump house and we will not fight them for it."

Owen wrote it out and it took a quarter of an hour and it was, he understood as he copied it, the most precisely correct sentence he had ever had to put into a document, and every word of it was a loss.

"That's Ada's," he said. "I'll put her name on the drafting line, not mine, and I'll put mine on the copy, because I'm the one who'll be asked to explain it and I'm the one who can afford to."

"You'll get the questions anyway."

"I know. That's the arrangement."

---

Wend Voss came out at half past three on the tenth day with a lantern and a folded coat under his arm, and stood at the top of the weir path for ten minutes without saying one word while a nineteen-year-old set a six-foot marker in the wet and two of the crew held it, and then he wrote his observation in the margin himself, in his own words, and added a second line for a pump start on the ninth that he had walked down from the annex on his own time to see and had mentioned to nobody.

*Witnessed by W. Voss, counter clerk, Mill Ward annex: the marker set at the weir path, and a pump start at the hour stated. I witnessed these acts. I witnessed no drafting and I have no view on the wording. My district received the maintenance of this crossing and did not perform it. I record that I consider its performance a cost to my district and not a gain, and that I have signed because a cost I have carried for eleven months with no district behind it is a cost I would rather see named.*

Pell came at four, signed for the marker and for nothing else, and wrote her own separation in the margin, four sentences, of which the third was: *I am an officer of the Registry of Continuant Works and my interest in the outcome of this filing is the interest of my office, which has an interest in the filing succeeding. I record that against myself.* Owen read it twice and understood that she had put the sentence in the wrong order on purpose, so that no one could take the last two words and leave out the first two.

Owen took the return out to Marguerite, who read every page of it, and who made him read the recorded dissent aloud twice.

"Four names," she said, "and the page says the meeting resolved nine to four and that the four are named and none of them signed and none of them objected to the meeting being held. Is that true?"

"None of them objected to the meeting being held."

"Then it is true and it is the best thing in the book." She put the weight on it. "The nine are going to be told they did the right thing for a generation. The four are going to be told, every day for the rest of their lives, that they lost. If you keep only the nine, in twenty years this ward will be a ward that agreed, and that is a thing that has never once in the history of this city been true, and if it is in the record then whoever is watching the record will believe it. Keep the four in. That is my whole advice and I have given it to you and I should like to go to bed."

And then Owen did the thing he had been carrying since the sixth day, which was that he wanted to see what the ward's own copy of the transfer would tell him.

It was in the drawer, and he had known it was there since the sixth, and he took it out and put it on the table beside his notebook and the ward's return, and he had a document in front of him and an act performed in front of him, and there is a thing a copy clerk has been able to do since the beginning of this Office, which is to set a real record beside a real thing and let the page tell him what the record can currently support.

The page said nothing.

Not nothing, exactly. That was the part he had to sit with. There was no small cold place behind his sternum, no pressure, no line of print in the margin, no date, no name, no next act required. He put his palm flat on the ward's copy and read it and looked at the pier behind it and then picked up the six other documents on the table and read all of them in the same way, one after the other, and got the same nothing from every one, and it took him four attempts to understand what had happened.

He could see the *district works inspection roll* the moment Pell named it. He could see a thin file. He could see a file with a contradiction in it, and he had seen one on the first day of the month, sitting at the Fourth Desk, comparing two dates and a stamp, and the margin had told him the date was early in a single line and left him to do the rest of the work himself.

This file was not thin.

It was closed. The review had sat on the fifth day — he had learned that on the sixth, in a bundle of returned papers on the wrong desk, and had not thought about it since — and a review that has sat closes the file, and a closed file does not answer. The district works inspection roll was a public document kept in an open drawer and a thin error file is a document somebody still has a use for, and this was neither of those things. It was a file that had been finished, on purpose, by men who had done what they were required to do and had gone home.

Owen Rusk sat in a ward hall in the rain with his hand on a dead file and understood the first true thing anyone had ever taught him that he did not already know: that the difference between a document that answers and a document that has been closed is not a matter of degree, or of cleverness, or of how well you read, and that a thin file having once spoken to him was not a promise that this one would.

He had no instrument for that. There was nothing in nine years that told him what a silence was. He wrote it down anyway, in his own book, in the plainest words he could manage, and wrote down the word *closed* and the date of the review, because he was a man who wrote things down and because on the day somebody wanted to know what the Office knew and when it stopped telling, the date would be the only thing he had.

*Nothing came. The file was closed at the review on the fifth. A closed file is not a thin file. A thin file answers and a closed file does not, and I cannot tell them apart except by the stamp, and the stamp is a thing they put on.*

He put it back in the drawer and closed the drawer, and then he did the second thing, which was the thing that made the tenth day worth having.

He wrote the scope in the ward's own words: the actual words, in the order they had been said, on the pump step and at the weir path and in the hall, and where a ward member's phrase was better than his he kept the ward's, and where two of them disagreed he wrote both and noted the disagreement, because the difference between *we closed it* and *the district closed it* is the whole difference between a ward that keeps a thing and a ward that was done to.

He was still writing when the light came up over the water, and Ada came in out of it with her sleeves full of river and told him that the District Deputy Registrar's office had sent a thing up to the seal stair at noon, that the Fourth Desk was to receive it in the afternoon, and that it was not a courtesy.

And Owen put the nine pages on the desk and squared them, and looked at the clock, and understood that the return was finished — signed, witnessed, narrowed, disputed, and complete on its face — and that there was not one qualified man in the Registry of Continuant Works who could enter a single word of it.
