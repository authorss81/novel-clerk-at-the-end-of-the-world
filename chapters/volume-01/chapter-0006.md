# Chapter 6 — Notice

The instruction sheet came up from the Mill Ward annex on the seventh day with a clerk who would not come past the second desk and put it on the rail and went away again, and the deadline was already printed on it, in a box, in the annex's own hand: *this evening*.

Owen read it standing up. It was four lines of instruction and a facsimile of a ward notice, and the four lines were reasonable, and that was the part he could not get past for the whole of the following two days.

*The receiving return requires the ratification of the affected ward to be posted before the transferred duties attach.*
*State the scope. State the benefit. State the deadline.*
*Ward notices do not carry public works crossing language. Do not add it.*

The last line was in the same hand as the other three and had no more weight in it than the other three, and beneath it somebody had drawn a short line and written, in a different and more hurried hand: *per W. desk.* He looked at that for a while. A man at a counter had written a rule about his ward's notice on a piece of scrap and sent it up to the Fourth River Desk to be obeyed by a clerk who had never asked.

He took the notice to his desk and filled it in.

The form has three ruled boxes and a space at the foot for the hall warden's seal. He wrote the scope first, because scope comes before everything and because a notice that gets its scope wrong is a notice that gets returned.

*Scope: the maintenance of public works at present maintained by this ward, as entered in the transfer registered at the Mill Ward annex and available for inspection at that annex.*

He read that four times. It was true. Every word of it was true, and being true was the difficulty, because a true notice of that kind is a notice that says: there is a thing, it is over there, and you may go and look at it. Ten of the thirteen households on the roll had not been to the annex. Three had, in the last three days, and were sitting in a kitchen downriver being told what it meant.

*Benefit: access to the consolidated water grant and to roofing assistance, applied for at the Mill Ward annex under a single return.*

Also true. Ilse Mern's roof was going soft at the ridge. The pump grant was real money, three years of it, and half the ward's people wanted it more than they wanted the crossing, and wanting it was not a mistake.

*Deadline: the ward meeting at the hour named, this seventh day.*

Then he came to the foot of the form, where the form gives the preparing clerk two lines and asks for his initials against them, and where a third box sits empty and says that the notice is accurate and complete and contains no matter not germane, to be ticked by the receiving office.

The first line said *prepared by* and the second said *scope filled as instructed*. He put his initials against both, because the notice was his work, and because the scope had been filled as instructed, and both of those were true, and the box that said *accurate and complete* was not his and would be ticked in an office he had never been in, by a man who would not read the box, on the strength of the two lines he had just initialled.

He had been copying for nine years. He had never once put a false word into a document. He was extremely good at it, and there was no prize for it, and he got up and carried the notice down to the Glass Stair antechamber himself and watched the porter take it and staple it to the board with the other ward notices, and the porter said *that's the lot, then* and went back to his stool, and that was the whole of the act.

The moment the sheet is on the board, the document exists, and everything that is not in it does not exist, and no man can put anything into a notice afterwards, because a notice that changes after it is posted is a second notice, and a second notice needs a second posting, and a second posting is a second deadline, and the receiving return cannot wait for a second deadline.

He went back up and finished the batch. He was on the third sheet when he realised that he had a sheet in his coat pocket that nobody had given him and that he did not want to look at, and he put his hand on it, and he left it there.

---

The ward hall at half past six on a wet evening is a warm room with about thirty people in it and one stove with a crack in its flue, and the notice was on the table face up with a salt weight on it, exactly as a notice is.

Ada got there at ten past seven.

She picked it up. She read it standing, which nobody else did; the others sat, and read, and looked up. Ada read it twice, all the way through, including the two lines at the top with the Office's reference, and then she put it down and picked it up again and read the scope box a third time, and Owen watched her do it and understood exactly which six words she was on.

"Which person," she said, "does this letter treat as disposable?"

He had been asked a version of that question before and had prepared for it and the preparation was useless. "Ada—"

"No, I want the actual answer. I want you to say a name." She turned the sheet so the whole room could see the scope box, and she said it louder, because she knew exactly who she was talking to and what a person does in a room of thirty when a clerk says nothing. "It says public works. Public works is pump and granary and the seed stall, and everybody's sitting here thinking public works is pump and granary, and the crossing is not in it, and the crossing is *over there*, in a document none of us has read, at an annex. So which person does this letter treat as disposable?"

"I don't think of it as a person—"

"That's not the question I asked." She was not shouting. That was worse. "I asked which person. There's four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds of estimate and nobody paying any of it, and a load notice on the bridge since the spring, and the ward's being asked to sign a paper that says the crossing goes to a district that has four men on its works list and two of them are over sixty. So when you filled in that box, who did you see? Was there anybody there? Did you see a person in it at all, or did you see a *scope*?"

The room had gone completely quiet, and Owen understood in a way that was almost physical that twenty-nine people were waiting for him to say something true, and that there was nothing true available.

"None of us has seen the annex document," he said. "I have. It was given to the ward by a resident in the autumn and it was never put to a meeting, and—"

"Then say the rest of it."

"The crossing's maintenance duty is in it," Owen said. "Along with the pump and the granary. The scope box points at the annex instead of naming the three, and the form doesn't let me name the crossing, and the instruction I was given says not to."

He got it out. It took him a long time and it was the worst minute of his working life so far, and it changed nothing at all, because the form was already on the board with his initials under the clerk's line.

"Add it," Ada said. "Add the crossing. Get the clerk to add the crossing and we'll sign it with the crossing in it."

He tried. He went back to the hall and asked the hall warden — a silent man with a seal and a queue of his own — to withdraw and repost it with an amended scope box, and was told it could not be done, and asked why, and was told that a posted notice could not be amended, and asked for the rule, and was given it in four words: *posting is the act.*

He went the next morning to the Mill Ward annex counter and asked the same question of the man behind it, and the man did not even put his pen down.

"It was posted," he said. "It's on a board. If you want a different notice you lodge a fresh one and it goes out for the next period, and the next period is the quarter, and the quarter is closed, and I could not tell you in front of this counter what happens to your duties in the meantime." He looked at Owen properly for the first time. "Are you the clerk that filled it?"

"Yes."

"Then it was filled correctly," the man said, "and I have read it, and if you want to know what I think about it I will tell you that I think the form is the form." He went back to his tray. "Next."

---

Nell Arbour was in the hall, at the back, on a chair she had brought.

She had been invited. That was the part Owen could not get past afterwards: the annex had written to her, a week before, in a courteous paragraph, to say that advocates were welcome to observe at ward ratifications, and that they might not speak. She had come anyway, and she had not spoken, and she had not spoken once in two hours while a clerk from the Registry sat in a ward hall and did not defend the document he had written, and Owen understood that she was there to watch a thing happen and that she had chosen to watch it in the room rather than hear about it later.

He found her in the doorway at nine.

"You were going to let it go," she said.

"Yes."

"You wrote it."

"Yes."

"You initialled the clerk's line."

"Yes."

"You know that document is going to be read in a year by a man who wants a reason, and every box in it is true, and there is not one thing in it he can hold up and say *here, this is what they did to us*?" She said it without heat. "Rusk. You write like a man who's already decided who the sacrifice is."

"I don't know who's—"

"Yes, you do. You knew when you filled in the scope box. You knew and you did it well and you did it correctly and that is the entire indictment." She looked past him into the room. "The ward that signs that tonight will be signing for a roof and a pump, and every one of them will be right about the roof and the pump, and in six weeks one of them is going to say *you told us about the roof* to a person who lost something, and that person is going to be right too, and there is no document in this city that can hold both of those truths at once. That's not a flaw in the form. That's what it's for."

"Nell—"

"I'm not speaking. They said I couldn't speak." She picked up her chair. "I'd have liked you to have been a coward about it, honestly. It would have been easier to clean up."

---

He stood up at twenty past nine.

He was a Registry clerk and he said so, and he said that he had drafted the notice on the annex's instruction, and that it was accurate in every particular, and that he wished to record in the room that the scope box referred to a document rather than naming three works, one of which was the Ninth Weir crossing, and that the register of the ward was not in the hands of the people in the room, and that he considered that a material omission even though the form did not require one.

The hall warden came down the aisle inside a minute — a heavy man with a seal and no opinions at all — and told him that a Registry officer was not a party to a ward meeting, and that if he wished to make an objection he could make it to the district office in writing, and he stood at Owen's shoulder with his hand out and Owen went.

The door of the hall was twenty feet from the stove and it was the coldest thing in Docketford that night, and outside it he could hear the river.

Ada Rusk finished the meeting without him. She did it badly, by the standards of somebody who had watched him do it well for four days, and she did it anyway, and she put the question in plain words and she did not put any words around it, and she said the ward's business was the ward's business and the Registry's clerk had said what he had to say and could go home.

The vote was nine to four.

He knows the four. Ada Rusk, who had told him to his face that he was not to be her reason for anything. Bram Tillet, who had asked in the first meeting who crossed the bridge if nobody was keeping it and had not liked the answer. Holm Ferris, who had written the board and who said at his table that the notice was for the ward and the ward was for the notice. And Wenna Sarr, who keeps a stall two doors from Marguerite Bray's and who has a niece of twelve whose name is on the first line of a schedule in a drawer in the Fourth River Desk, and who stood up last, and said one sentence, and sat down: *My girl is on that other paper. Nobody asked me and nobody has ever read it out to me, and I am not signing anything until somebody does.*

Nine in favour, witnessed, sealed, at a quarter to midnight, in a hall with a crack in the flue, by a warden who did it because it was the form.

Owen was not in the room. He was on the step, and he heard the seal, because you can hear a seal.

---

He came in on the eighth day with the batch and did the batch. The run had ended on the first, in a memorandum, and two clean batches since then had not put it back, and the mark in the supervisor's book beside his name was now a question with an answer attached to it, and there was nothing at all to be done on the eighth day.

At noon he walked upriver with a sheet of paper on which he had written a list, and by the time he got to the hall he had written a second one underneath, and the second one was the same list twice.

Ada was doing the marker, out of the wet, in waders, with two of the crew.

She came over and looked at the list upside down, the way people do, and read two thirds of it, and gave it back.

"Copyist," she said.

"Yes."

"Copyist who stayed." She went back to the marker. "Come and hold this end. I've got the ninth post and I need somebody who won't tell me a story."

He held the end of a six-foot painted board in the rain for four hours and did not tell her a story once, and afterwards he walked back down with a chalk stain on his hands that would not come out in three days, and stopped at the top of the weir path, where the water was up a foot on the lower stair and moving, to work out what he had actually done this week.

The crossing was transferred. The transfer was not an error; it was correct, and it had been correct since a tired man gave a folded sheet to a warden in the rain, and it was still correct, and the ward had just signed its own name to it in front of thirty witnesses.

The transfer would attach when the receiving return performed the duty, and the receiving return was not going to perform the duty, because the receiving return had four men on its works list, and so on the next weekly continuance roll — seven days off, the fourteenth, and not a hearing date, and not anything anybody had petitioned for, because nobody had petitioned for anything yet — the crossing's status would be entered with no district behind it at all.

He had a week. He had a ward that had just voted, in front of a stove, with the truth on the table, to take the crossing off itself.

And the only thing he had left to give them was a form that said *we perform the duty*, and which nobody in that hall could fill in, because every person in the ward who could stand up and say what the ward had done was also a person the ward's return would be about, and there was not one other person in the world in a position to say otherwise.

Owen stood on a stair in the rain with a chalk stain on his hands and understood, entirely and without any comfort at all, that he was going to have to go and find somebody outside this ward to be a witness, and that he had no idea at all who that would be.
