# Chapter 88 — The One They Offered Her

The strip came down on the Thursday morning of the twenty-seventh day of the third month of the Concord Year 488 at about ten minutes to ten, at the counter of the count room at the Glass Stair, and it is unsigned, because a note is a thing a building says about itself, and it is four lines, and the counting clerk at the second counter gave it to Owen Rusk face up and then kept her hand on it for about a second longer than the hand-off required, which he noticed and did not comment on.

**COUNT OF THE SEASON OF 489, DOCKETFORD DISTRICT. Note of the twenty-seventh. The count of a season is certified by the Warden of the Census Guard. Where a district has settlements named in the schedule of settlements to be reconciled for that season which are not reconciled in it, and which are unresolved at the certification, a schedule of them is to be printed and attached to the certification. The district is asked to nominate a person to attest that schedule. The person nominated is not required to be an examiner. Note, and not a rule.**

Owen Rusk read it twice standing at the counter.

"The count is certified by the Warden," he said.

"It is certified by the Warden."

"And a schedule of places is to be printed and attached to it, and the district is asked to nominate a person to attest that the places are not reconciled."

"Attest that they are not reconciled," said the counting clerk. "That is what it says and that is a different thing and I want you to read the third line again before you say anything at all about it."

He read the third line again.

"The person nominated is not required to be an examiner," he said.

"The person nominated is not required to be an examiner," said the counting clerk, "**and that is correct, and it is in the note on purpose, and there is a reason for it and the reason is not a kindness to the person, and you will work the reason out in about nine seconds and then you are going to have to go and ask a person, and I want you to have worked it out first so that you know what you are carrying down the hill.**"

"The reason is that a person who is not an examiner cannot examine a schedule, and therefore cannot be challenged for examining one."

"And cannot be challenged for anything, near enough, and the Office has never had any view on whether that is proper and does not intend to have one," said the counting clerk, "and I have been at this counter for nine years and that is the first time in nine years this office has put a person's unexaminedness in a note as though it were a qualification, and I have not been asked to have a view and I am telling you what I noticed and nothing else."

---

Owen Rusk could not sign it and it took him about four seconds to establish and he had known it before he had finished the first line and had read the whole thing anyway.

"A schedule of places is a schedule," he said, to the counter, to the clerk, to the room, which had four people in it. "**I may not examine the schedule. I may not examine a choice of places. And a schedule of the settlements not reconciled in this district in this season is a list of places, and there is nothing about the ten on it that is not a choice of places, and the only thing I am permitted to do with a document about the schedule is nothing, and if I sign it I have made a document about the schedule with my name at the foot of it and there is no form in this county on which a person may take that back.**"

"Then it goes to the roll," said the counting clerk.

---

The roll of district exclusions is nineteen names and it has had no vacancy since the spring and the fourth name on it is Junia Pell, who is twenty-two years old and has been in the building three months and a fortnight and is a clerk and not an examiner, and Owen Rusk went down the stairs and found her at the second desk with the assisted tray.

"I am going to ask you to do something," he said, "and I am going to give you the whole of it before you answer, and you are going to tell me the answer before I have finished, and I have known that since the first month and I have brought it up the hill anyway."

She put the correspondence down.

"The district has been asked to nominate a person to attest a schedule of the settlements named in the printed schedule for this season which are not reconciled in it and which are unresolved at the certification. It goes up tomorrow. The count is certified by the Warden and the schedule is attached to her certification. **You are the fourth name on a roll of nineteen and there is no vacancy and there is no officer of this district who may sign it, so the nomination is yours by arithmetic, and the office has said in a note that the person nominated is not required to be an examiner.**"

"Which means it is because I have examined nothing," said Junia Pell.

"Yes."

"And that is correct," she said, "**and it is an insult, and I would like it noticed that both of those are the same word and I am not being theatrical, and that a note has come down a building's own stair to say in writing that the reason a person is fit to sign a list of places nobody went to is that she has never looked at a place, and that is a qualification, and it is correct, and it is the first time in this building that anybody has been put forward on those grounds and I have been on a roll for three months and a fortnight and I have not asked for anything.**"

She did not say anything else for about four seconds, and then she said the thing that made it a refusal and not a complaint, and she said it in the ordinary voice, at the second desk, at about ten past ten on a Thursday morning, and it is the reason and it is not the reason anybody expected.

"No. And the reason is not the note and it is not the insult and it is not that I have examined nothing."

"All right."

"**A schedule of the settlements that were not reconciled is the only document this county will ever print that is true about a place and about the people in it at the same time. Every other instrument in this season is a column that has been given a meaning by a person who chose it. This one says, in the ordinary form's voice, that a place exists, is named, has premises of record against it, was named for reconciliation, and was not looked at. That is a fact about ten places. It is the first fact about ten places that will ever be public.**"

"**And if my name is at the foot of it, then in about four years the document is read as evidence that a person agreed with it, and there is no column on that schedule that says she did not go, and I will have spent the three months of this building and about nine years of the other thing being a name on a document about ten places I have never seen.**"

She squared the tray.

"And I have worked out the rest of it and I am going to say it because you are standing there. **You did not choose me. The Office did not choose me. You offered me the one thing in this season that nobody in this building can be given, and you offered it to the one person in the building who has said a true thing out loud in a room about a round of five settlements of which four were looked at and one was not. And I said it out loud because it was true and not because I was free that week, and you are asking me to put my name under a list because I am the one here who has already been loud.**"

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"Well. I am not going to be loud on a schedule." She picked the correspondence back up. "**You may tell the office that the reason was the note. It is the shorter reason and it is true and it will do. Do not tell them the other one, because the other one is mine and I have not given it away, and it is not going into a building's record in the season that it happened, because a person who was proud does not want it in a drawer with a date on it.**"

"You are not being offered anything," said Owen Rusk. "There is nothing in it. It is a signature on a list of ten places and it does not move a figure and it does not open a crossing and it does not pay anybody and if it is refused the count is certified tomorrow without a schedule, which is a thing that has never happened in this city."

"Then it is certified without a schedule," said Junia Pell, "and I have lost nothing whatever, and you have lost a thing that was never worth having, and I would rather you worked that out today than in four years."

She turned back to the assisted tray and got about nine lines into it, and then she stopped, and she did not look up, and she said the last of it in the voice she uses for a counter.

"And there is one more thing and I am going to say it once and twice is not on the table, and I am saying it because you will spend tonight deciding whether I was right and I would rather you spent the evening on something else."

"All right."

"**There is a piece of paper in a building in this city that two living people can swear to and that I have not raised this season and am not raising tonight, and I have not referred to it, and I have not hinted at it, and I have not used it, and if the schedule had been something about a person instead of a place I would still not have used it, and you know that, and I am not thanked for it and I am not going to be, because thanking me for not using it is the same as pricing it.**"

"I have not asked you for it," said Owen Rusk.

"No," said Junia Pell. "**You have not. That is the whole of my standing and I have never said so to anybody in this building and I am not saying it to you either. Go up the hill. There is going to be a hall tonight and you are going to stand at the back of it, and I have worked out by about half past four this afternoon that you are going to stand at the back of it whatever happens this morning, and you will not be asked a single question about that, and I have already asked you two today and one of them was not fair.**"

---

Nobody at the district office signs it. Owen Rusk wrote that out on the back of a sheet from a tide table in about ninety seconds and it is a fact and not an opinion and he wrote it in the standing order:

**I may not sign. A Continuance Examiner may not examine a schedule and may not examine a choice of places, and a schedule of the settlements of this district not reconciled in this season is a list of places. The officer of the record of the district returns is the person in this building most likely to be asked to sign it and is the person least entitled.**

**The district office of Docketford nominates no person.**

He read it back and did not send it, because the district's answer is not required, and the Office is not waiting for it, and a note went down a stair at ten minutes to ten and the count is certified at about ten tomorrow and the schedule goes with it or it does not.

**And it goes with it, because the only person left in this county who may attach a schedule to a count is the Warden of the Census Guard, and a certification is the Warden's act, and there is not one other hand in this city that may do it. Nobody at the district office signs it. The Guard must. And a person can be wrong in a form: the Warden of Docketford district has been in none of the ten settlements named in the schedule printed on the twenty-eighth of the second month, and she has said in a field in front of about thirty people that she has returned one schedule in nineteen seasons and it came back with the same names on it four days later, and she is going to be asked to certify a list of ten places she has not been to, and the form will not say so, and the form does not have a column for it.**

He did not write to Kade. She had told him in a field in front of nine people on the twenty-ninth of the second month not to write to her for a season, and had said that doing it there was deliberate and worse than a refusal, and he was twenty-six days into that season. He did not write to her and it did not occur to him to, and the reason it did not occur to him is that there is no letter in this county that may carry a schedule to a Warden, and the reason there is no such letter is that she shut that door, and it was shut well and on purpose and he has not knocked on it since.

He put the tray on at a quarter past six that evening and there was nothing on it, and then he went down the hill to the ward hall, because it was a Thursday and the roster was read at ten past six and he is in the hall on Thursdays now, and about thirty people were in it and the stove was on and the sheet with the eight things and the three things was lying face up on the long table in Marguerite Bray's hand with the day and the hour under it, because a sheet that has been ruled into a delivery book is a thing that lives on a table.

---

Ada Rusk read the second of the four terms out again, and she read it the same way she had read it in the hall on the twentieth, and there was not one person in that hall who had not heard it in a hall in two years.

"That the figure which goes in the reason box goes on the public board with this district's name against it, whatever it is, and before we see it." She put the sheet down. "**It is not performed. It is not withdrawn. It is in the eight. I am reading it out for the thirteenth time and I am going to say why, and the reason is that tomorrow morning at about ten o'clock at the west gate of Cutter's Fields a count of this season for this district is going to be certified, and it is going to be certified with a list of ten places on it that nobody went to look at, and there is going to be no figure about this ward anywhere on it, and no figure about this ward in the reason box, because there is going to be no reason box, and no figure about this ward on the board. And I am the only person in this hall who has ever written a sentence in this county that wanted that, and I wanted it in the first month of the year.**"

"**And I am not going to say the words visibility is not remedy. I said them in the first month. I said them at the gate on the seventh. I have said them twice and I have watched about six people in this county say them this season and every single one of them was pleased with themselves. I am not going to give it to anybody a third time, and if any person in this hall wants to say it they may stand up and say it and I will sit down while they do it.**"

Nobody stood up.

Marguerite Bray read it back twice and ruled it in the delivery book in her own hand with the day and the hour, and read the ruling out.

**Ruled the twenty-seventh day of the third month, in the hall, with about thirty persons present. That the second of the four terms of this district is again stated, and is not performed, and is not withdrawn, and that on the twenty-eighth day of this month the count of the season of 489 for Docketford district will be certified with a schedule of the settlements not reconciled in that district in that season printed and attached, and that no figure concerning this district will appear upon it in any column, and that this is the thirteenth time the term has been stated in a hall of this ward, and that the person who wrote it is on a file about it and that no person in this hall is to be told anything about that.**

And then, because it was ten past seven and the stove was going, Cobb Rennie read the eight and the three off the table out loud in about four minutes, the way he reads a price, and he got them exact, and he read the third one twice, and the fifth one he read in the voice he uses for a price and in no other voice at all.

"The pump grant restored in exchange for a name beside the six, and the name is not going to be asked of us."

He stopped there and said, "**I have been on that list for two years and I am not going to say anything about it, and I have said that in this hall before and it is not going to be said again, and Ruth is on that list and Bram is on that list and Ada is on that list and I do not know how many more and I have stopped counting because the count is not the point of it and the hours are the point of it.**"

Ruth Mapp said, "**Then don't say it at all,**" which is the whole of what she has ever said in a ward hall.

And then she said a second thing, which she has said once before in about nine years, and it was at Ada Rusk and not at Owen Rusk, and Ada Rusk turned round and listened to it.

"You have not read us the three things," said Ruth Mapp. "**You have read the eight twice and the three once and you have read the second of the four terms thirteen times, and nobody in this hall has heard the three read out properly, and one of the three is that we are to be left alone on the rest of it, and about nine people in this room would like to be left alone on the rest of it and have never once been asked.**"

Ada Rusk stood still for about two seconds and then she read the three out again, all three, in the same voice, and the third one is this.

"Being left alone on the rest of it."

And a woman of about sixty at the back said, "**Say that one on Friday as well,**" and Ada Rusk said, "**I am not promising anything on a Friday,**" and Marguerite Bray ruled nothing and wrote nothing and closed the book about a quarter past eight, which is the earliest she has ever closed it in nine years.

"Before I close it," said Marguerite Bray, "I am going to say a thing I have said three times in two years and I am saying it for the fourth, and then I am not going to say it again this season."

About thirty people stopped what they were doing.

"**I have asked a question in this ward, in my own name, on this ward's own paper, about eleven times in about two years. The answer to eight of them was four words. I have written the numbers down in this book and not the answers, because an answer is not a fact about a ward and a number is, and there are eight numbers in this book now and there is a ninth, and I have got them all in my own hand and dated and I have never once written down what the answer was.**" She put her hand flat on the closed book. "**I am sixty-one years old. I am not going to be thanked for that, and I would like it noticed that a steward who writes down a number instead of an answer is doing the only thing in this county that anybody can do, and that somebody in a building is going to read nine numbers in a book in Fetter Lane one day and there is not one column in this county that says what they are for.**"

Nobody thanked anybody, and there is a great deal in this county that nobody is thanked for, and the reason is in the book: a thank you is a receipt, and there is nothing here to receipt.

Owen Rusk was at the back, and he was not asked for anything, and about thirty people in that hall had spent the evening reading a list of eight things a ward will not accept and three things it will, and one of the three was *being left alone on the rest of it*, and nobody in that hall said a word about that either, and about nine people in that hall thought about it.

He got up the hill at about a quarter to nine and did the tray, and it was on, and the last thing on it that day had been a ward's own paper with eight refusals and three acceptances on it addressed to an office, and the tray had been that kind of day about four times in nine years.
