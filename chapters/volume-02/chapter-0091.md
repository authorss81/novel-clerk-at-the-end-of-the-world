# Chapter 91 — A Line Against Which a Quantity Is Authorised

The ledger in the Water Office across the cut is four columns and it is not the return, and the man behind the counter with the bad knee put the copy on the counter himself and did not correct him about the year, and it was on a Monday morning of the fourth month of the Concord Year 488, and Owen Rusk had come in with a question and had left with a head.

The head is on the third column, in a hand that is not the hand of about twenty-nine other hands on that page, and it is eleven years old. It reads **SLACKWATER TANK, HEAD OPENED 478/19**, and against it there is a quantity of one thousand and ninety a day, authorised, and there is no street on the line and there is no settlement of record on the line and there is nothing whatever at the foot of it that says where the water is, because there has never been a column in this county for where the water is.

He copied the head out onto the back of a sheet from a tide table. He did not copy the quantity. He has the quantity in his head and it is not a difficult number.

---

**Slackwater is up a track off the Fetter Bend road and it is four miles and about a mile of that is up.**

There is a field at the bottom of it with nine flat acres in it and a hedge on two sides, which is how he knew where it was, because Cutter's Fields is a flat piece of ground behind the tanneries and somebody a long time ago measured one and then measured another one about a mile away and put a hedge round it. There is a stile and then a grass track and then a cart track and the cart track climbs for about half a mile through two fields and a line of nine elms, and it is the worst half mile of walking in this county and he did it at about half past nine in the morning with his coat buttoned to the throat.

Nobody sent him. Nobody asked him. He is the officer of the record of the district returns of Docketford district and there is going to be a return made in that building for the season of 490 and the fifth column in it is going to be completable, and one line of it is a head opened in the Concord Year 478 against which a quantity is authorised, and there is no place on the line, and a return is a return of places.

It was not a summons and it was not a case and there is no form in this county on which a person goes to a place to see whether it is a place, and he went.

He has thought since about the four mornings he spent on that head and the one that decided it, and the one that decided it was not a good one. It was that if he filled in that cell with a season, the quantity on the third column would belong to a place he had not been to, and he would have done it, and it would have gone up a stair, and about four years afterwards there would be a form in Concord Hall with a year in it against Slackwater and nobody in this county would be able to say where the year came from. He has done that once this year, to ten places, in his own hand, and it is in the back cover of a book on a shelf, and he decided about that one in about four seconds and wrote the reason down and sent it up the stairs anyway.

This one he decided about on a grass track with a bad knee coming on in the cold, and it took him two miles, and the reason he gave himself at the top of the climb was that the head was opened in 478 by a man who was not asked anything and has been dead a long time, and that a line against which a quantity is authorised is a line, and the Office does not examine the basis of a quantity, and that is information and is not a filing, and the Office will not answer it as one in any other quarter.

He has used that sentence eleven times since the second month. He used it twice on the climb and he got a hundred yards out of the second one.

---

The track comes out at a walled yard with a tank in it, a low stone tank about nine feet across with a wooden lid on two thirds of it, a standpipe at the corner with a tap and a chain, and a low building with a slate roof and one window, and about eleven people standing in the yard at ten in the morning on a Monday doing nothing in particular in the way of a place where nothing has been asked of it.

The eleventh person turned round when he came through the gate, and she was sixty-one or near it and she had a book under her arm, and she said, before he had got four paces in, "**You are the man who put a column on a board.**"

"My name is Owen Rusk."

"**That is not what I asked and you have not answered it,**" she said. "I asked who you are. I can see what office you are from about half a mile back, because the coat." She did not put the book down. "**There is nobody in this county that comes up this track. There has been nobody up this track that I can remember, and I have been at that tank nineteen years and I was forty-two when I started. What do you want.**"

"I want to look at the tank."

"You may look at the tank," she said. "**Nobody has ever asked me for anything about it and I have said no to a great many fewer things than I should have.**"

Her name was Prud Naylor and the book under her arm was a household's book, a diary of the tank going back to the Concord Year 469 in five hands, and it is not an instrument and it is not on a form and it has never been read by anybody out of this yard, and she showed him about four pages of it and then shut it, which was the correct thing to do and which she did on purpose.

---

**He was in that yard until about two in the afternoon and about half of what he was told he was told by a woman with a book under her arm, and she was the only person there who told him anything at all twice.**

The second thing she told him was about a list nailed inside the door of the low building, and she made him go and read it rather than telling him what was on it. It is a rotation for the sluice wheel, in the hand of about four different people going back a long way, and it is a list of **households** — *Naylor, Ragg, Harker, Caws, Skell, and so on for twenty-two* — and against each household there is a day, and the days are the days of a week and the week is a fortnight because the wheel is worked twice a day and the list is not a roster of persons.

"There are twenty-two names on that door," said Owen Rusk, "and there are nine doors in the district's book."

"**There are twenty-two names on that door because there are twenty-two of us and because I put the last four up myself in the spring, when the Brydes went down the road,**" said Prud Naylor. "**And I have known for about eleven years that there are nine of us in a book somewhere and I have never once found out which nine, and I want you to understand the shape of that before you go away. It is not that nine of us have been counted. It is that nine of us have been counted and the other thirteen of us have not, and there is no way on this earth to stand in this yard and work out which we are, and every one of the twenty-two of us has done it.**"

Somebody said, from the standpipe, "**You have not, Mrs Naylor.**"

"**I did it in the winter of 479 and I did it again in 483 and I got the same answer twice and that is the whole of what I have ever known about it,**" she said, "**and if it is a fortnight's list then it is a fortnight's list, and there is no column in this county that says a person who cannot be asked is somebody you can count the water for, and if there is I should like to see it.**"

---

**Here is the shape of the place and Owen Rusk did not know any of it before the middle of that morning and most of what he was told he was told by a woman with a book under her arm.**

Twenty-two households. Not eleven and not nine and not a number out of a book. Twenty-two households in about forty rooms and nine outbuildings and one chapel, and the chapel has a stove in it and a bench down each wall and it closes on the Sunday, and it has closed on the Sunday for as long as the woman with the book can remember, and the closure is not the building's. The building is sound. There is no money in it and there has not been a subscription for it in six years and the last one was collected at a harvest supper by a man who has since gone down the road.

There is a tank. There is a standpipe. There is a sluice up at the head of the beck above the yard, and the sluice is a timber frame with an iron screw and a cast wheel about four feet across, and it is worked by hand twice a day by whoever is in the yard at seven and at six, and it has been worked by hand twice a day for nineteen years by the same household in rotation on a list that is nailed inside the door of the low building and is a list of names and not of premises.

The draw is a thousand and ninety gallons a day in the district's ledger and about nine hundred in the tank on a wet week, and the difference is about a hundred and ninety gallons a day and nobody has ever been able to account for the hundred and ninety and it has never once been anybody's business.

**And the place is not on anything.** It is not on the schedule of settlements to be reconciled for the season of 489, and it was not on it for 488, and it is not on the schedule of the settlements that were not reconciled attached to the certification of the twenty-eighth of the third month, which is the first schedule in this city's record that prints a list of places nobody went to look at, and there is no row on it, and there is no reason for the row being absent, and there is no form in this county on which anybody may ask.

"I have been in this office nine years," said Owen Rusk, "and I have the book of premises of record for this district in a room two miles down the hill, and there are nine premises against your settlement in it."

The woman with the book looked at him for about four seconds.

"**Then that is nine of us and I have never been able to find out which nine, and I have asked twice, and both times I was told the book was not a place to ask.**"

---

The man at the sluice came down about half past eleven and he was forty-six and he had been at Slackwater nine years and his name was Jos Harker and he said the sluice had been going since the ninth of October and had needed a wedge twice.

There is a letter about it. A competent man said in the winter of 486 that the frame wanted looking at in the spring, and the spring came, and in July a man from eleven miles up the road put a new frame in on the Otter Stair drain for four hundred pounds and sent a letter about it, and the letter is in the low building in a tin, and nobody has answered it, and there is nobody to answer it, because the settlement has no premises of record and therefore no works maintained against it and therefore nobody in this county whose duty it is to read a letter about a sluice.

"That is a work," said Owen Rusk.

"That is a timber frame with a wheel on it," said Jos Harker. "**I have been putting my shoulder against that wheel twice a day for nine years and if you want to know what the county thinks of it, the county does not think of it, and you cannot think very hard of a thing nobody has written down.**"

---

**There is a girl of nine in the yard and there is a woman of about eighty who does not come out, and Owen Rusk asked about both of them because he is an officer of the count and because the count does not count people, and he asked them in the wrong order and he knew it while he was doing it.**

He asked the woman of eighty first, through Prud Naylor, and he asked it as a question about premises, and Prud Naylor told him that the woman of eighty was eighty-four and had come to Slackwater in a cart in the Concord Year 459 and had not been out of the yard since the wet of 481 and that her name is on the fortnight's list against the Tuesday and the Friday and that if you want to know why it is the Tuesday and the Friday it is because that is when the cart goes.

"That is a care act," he said, before he had thought about it, and then he heard himself say it, in a yard, out loud, in front of about six people, and it was a phrase he has used on paper perhaps forty times this year.

Prud Naylor put her book against her hip.

"**It is a cart on a Tuesday,**" she said. "**And I would like you to write down the order in which you asked me that, because I want it kept that you asked about the water before you asked about her.**"

He wrote it down. He wrote it in the back of the tide-table sheet after the head, in his own hand, and the order of it, and the day.

The girl of nine was Tobin Ragg's daughter and she was in the yard because there was nowhere else for her to be, and Owen Rusk did not ask her anything at all, which was correct, and which cost him something he did not have a place to put.

He asked Tobin Ragg in the end, at about one, at the gate, on his way out, and Tobin Ragg was thirty-four and had been at Slackwater eleven years and said the thing that was going to be the argument in a hall four days later, without knowing it, in about nine words, and then went to work.

"**The sluice went on the eighth and it is not going to be mended by anybody, and I have a girl of nine, and I would like somebody to come up this track and put a roof on it before the winter and not to ask me a single question about how many rooms we are in.**"

---

**Nobody in that yard asked him a single question about himself all morning, and two people told him things about the tank that are not in any book in this county, and neither of those two things is on this page because a page is not a place and this is not a page.**

What he came back with, at about twenty past two on the Monday, walking down the half mile of elms, was this.

The nine premises of record in the district's book had been struck out of it in the Concord Year 471 with a single clerk's stroke and nothing else, in the fourth column of that book, which is headed *struck, or not struck*, and a stroke is a decision somebody made, and there is no column in that book for why and there never has been one. He knew that from the book and it took him eleven minutes in a room with a bulb on a flex to find the season. He did not find who did it. There is no column for that either.

The third column of that book said 471, and the third column is the season an entry first belongs to, and the fourth column said struck, and there was no fifth column, and there is no fifth column in that book because a book of premises of record in this county has never needed one.

And the water was authorised in 478 by a man who did not go to any of the nine, in an office across the cut, on a form with four columns and a habit, and it has been authorised every season since, and the head on the ledger has never been married to a place, and the place has never been named in anything.

**And the only office in this county that could have sent anybody to look at Slackwater is a building that has been unable to find it in its own books since the Concord Year 471, and so it has never sent anybody, and so nothing in this county's arithmetic has ever been able to reach it, and the only reason that has been a safety is that a book forgot.**

He came down off the track at half past two and stood at the stile in the cold and worked out the rest of it, standing up, the way he does it, and it took him about nine minutes and nobody watched him do it.

The arithmetic is committed. A quantity is authorised against a head that nobody can marry to a place, and it has been authorised for eleven years, and it goes into Docketford district's allocation, and the district office is the office that computes the district's allocation, and the district office produced no figures of premises of record for the season of 489 and was certified short in a field behind a tannery on the twenty-eighth of the third month.

**So there is a quantity in this county that is authorised and unspent and there is no place it is spent at, and the only instrument in this world that can touch it is a count correction, and a count correction has a reason box, and the reason is a figure.**

---

On the Tuesday morning of the second day, at the count room at the Glass Stair, a woman of about fifty with a slate and no coat was not there, and a strip of paper the size of a receipt was there, and it was not from her.

**COUNT ROOM, PROVINCIAL COUNTING OFFICE. Docketford district. Schedule of premises struck from the district book of premises of record in the Concord Year 471 and not reinstated. The schedule of premises attached. Closed. The Office has struck the line. Unsigned, because a strip is a thing a building says about itself.**

The counting clerk at the second counter read it to him standing up, because she has read four or five of these out in nine years and has never once read one for anybody who did not ask.

"It came down at ten past eight," she said. "**It is not signed and it is not hers, and I have not asked, and I would like it noticed that I have not asked and that is the whole of what I have done with it.**"

"Does it say anything about the water."

"No," she said. "**It says a line is closed. There is no column on it for what the water does. There has never been a column on it for what the water does, and I have been at this counter nine years and I would like that written down somewhere that is not this.**"

"Then what is it for."

"**It is for a return,**" she said. "**The Office has to make one for the season of 490 and one line in this district's arithmetic will not close, and a line that will not close is not a place and it is not a quantity, it is a hole, and the Office does not carry holes.**"

"And the nine."

"The nine go on a schedule of premises attached to a correction, and there will be a date on the correction and there will be a reason in a box, and the reason will be a figure, and it will be correct, and I have not been told what the figure is and I have not asked and I am not going to ask, because a clerk who asks what is in the reason box this week is a clerk who is asked to sign it in about six months." She put the strip down and squared it to the edge of the counter with two fingers. "**And I will tell you the thing I will say to nobody else, because you will not do anything with it, and then I am going back to my counter. If the Office strikes the line, the quantity goes back to the district's main. If the Office reinstates the premises, the quantity comes back here. It cannot do both and it has until the middle of next week to decide and it is not going to take a week.**"

---

**Owen Rusk went back up the track on the Wednesday morning of the third day and walked the last half mile again, and about two hundred yards from the stile he met the Guard coming down.**

Two of them and a woman of about thirty with a slate, and a handcart, and no tent, and the woman said, without stopping, that they had been at the top of the beck on Sunday looking at the sluice and had been told by a man with a shoulder against a wheel that the frame had split in the October and wanted a wedge, and that she had written both of those in her round sheet in the ordinary way, and that there was no settlement here and there never had been and that her round for the season was finished on the twenty-eighth of the third month.

"Then what are you doing here," said Owen Rusk.

"**I am walking down a road,**" she said, "**and there is no form in this county that says a clerk of the Census Guard may not walk down a road, and I have put the date in my sheet because the date is a fact and the fact was told to me at a gate by a man, and I am not going to pretend that I did not hear it.**"

And they went on down the track past him, and the handcart had a stone on it in a wooden case under a tarpaulin, and he did not say anything about the stone, and neither did she.
