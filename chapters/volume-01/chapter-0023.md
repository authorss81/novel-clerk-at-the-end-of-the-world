# Chapter 23 — What the Registry Did Once

The point of law had to be written down before ten in the morning, and writing it down took him until a quarter to ten, and the reason it took him until a quarter to ten was that he had it four different ways and every one of the four was a lie dressed as a question.

*Whether the Registry may refuse a community return without publishing a schedule of its refusals.* That was the one. He wrote it at the top of a clean sheet and looked at it for about a minute and a half and could not decide whether it was a point of law or a pamphlet, and then he read the corridor of the seventeenth day of the month back to himself — *there are districts in this county that have not come to this office with anything since the flood year* — and understood that if he wanted the Office's own record of its conduct then the *conduct* had to be the thing the point was about, because the Office will fight about a question it can answer and will concede a question it cannot.

So the point was not *may the Registry honour a return*. The point was the smaller one, the duller one, the one that had a number in a volume somewhere if anybody had ever gone and looked for it.

He went upriver at ten.

---

The district office record room is two floors below the counters and it is a long room with a table down the middle of it and shelving on both sides, and the shelving is not alphabetical, it is by *class of instrument*, which is a system designed by somebody who expected to be the only person in the room for forty years. Continuances on the left. Transfers in the middle. Instruments under seal behind a wire on the right.

Precedents are at the far end, on a shelf, in one row, and it is one row, and it is four volumes, and it is the only set of books in Docketford that cannot be read by a person who is not arguing a case.

That is not a rule about the books. There is no rule about the books. Anybody may take a precedent volume down. What a precedent volume will not do is answer a question that has not been put to it in the form the Office recognises, and Owen had spent a month learning that every institution in this building is like that and had not yet worked out that this one was like it more than the rest.

The volumes are cited by number. A precedent has no name. It has a number, and the number means nothing, and the number is in a printed list on the inside of the front board, and the list gives the number and a heading in the compiler's hand, and the compiler's hand is the hand of a man who died before Owen was born and whose idea of a heading was a *situation* and not a subject.

*12/A. Where a district is unable to meet a duty and the office has not the means.*
*31/B. Where a return is offered for a work in a district other than that of the filer.*
*37/C. Where a district has performed a duty in public and has asked the office to say so.*

He read the third line four times.

Thirty-seven, letter C, and the heading was in his own trade's flat unpunctuated English and it was the first line on the list he had ever read that was not a sentence about a place.

But you do not get a precedent because it is on a list. Owen put his finger on 37/C and understood that a list of numbers is a list of numbers, and that what stood between him and the volume was the fact that a precedent is produced on a point of law and not on a curiosity, and that the Office had a form for the asking of it, and that if he filled in the form he would be a line in a request register in a room he did not work in.

He filled in the form. He put the number on it and the volume and the point of law in his own words, and he wrote the reason as *matter before the district office, District Return 487-LL, review due*, which was true, and which was the first true sentence he had put on a form in six weeks that was not also a manoeuvre.

The clerk at the record room table read it and did not go and get the volume.

"The point has to be a point," she said. "That is a subject."

"Then the point is whether this office may refuse a district return without publishing a schedule of the refusals it has made in the quarter."

She looked at him for a second and a half. "That is a point," she said. "It is also the first anybody has put in this room in about two years, and I am not saying that as a complaint."

---

They did not give him 37/C that afternoon. What they gave him was 12/A, which is the precedent that decided everything against him, and they gave it to him in ten minutes with the volume open on the table, and he read it standing up.

It was very good. That was the thing that undid him for about an hour.

*12/A. A return made by a district of this Registry is a statement by that district of what it has done. It is not a claim upon the office. It does not suspend, restore, or vary any status, and no sum is payable in respect of it. The office acknowledges such a return and enters it.*

Four paragraphs. It said, in the plainest language the Office has ever used about anything, that a community return does not make anybody owe anybody anything, and that is true, and it is the truth on which the whole of the transfer rests, and Owen had been arguing against it for a month and had never once been given the sentence in print.

He read it twice and said, out loud, in an empty record room, to a woman he did not know:

"That is a load-bearing wall and it is made of a true sentence."

"Sir?"

"Nothing," said Owen. "I said it to myself. It is a good precedent and it is the wrong one for me and you have given it to me on purpose."

"I have given it to you because you asked a point about refusals and a refusal is the first half of a precedent and the second half is what the office may do about a return, and you did not ask that half, and there is a reason not to ask for that half in a room with two people in it." She turned the page round. "Thirty-seven C is on the counter after the lunch hour and you may read it. Not take it out. Read it here, and take as long as you want over it, because there is nobody behind you in this room at two o'clock on a Wednesday. And I am going to tell you what the reason I gave you twelve A is, because you are going to spend the rest of this afternoon working out whether you were obstructed, and you were not."

"Go on."

"You were not, because twelve A is true, and it is the second half of thirty-seven C, and a man who reads only one half of a precedent is a man who will build a case on a quarter of a wall." She capped her pen. "Both halves are in the same volume. They have been in it for the best part of a century. Nobody has read them together since about the year I started."

---

He read 37/C at ten past two on the Wednesday afternoon, the same afternoon, at a table in a room two floors below the counters with the volume open in front of him and a chair he was not supposed to bring up from the floor.

It is two hundred and nine words long.

He had expected — he had not known what he had expected, and he found the having-not-known-it the most interesting thing about the afternoon — he had expected four pages, or a schedule, or a memorandum with a finding at the end of it.

It is two hundred and nine words and it is one paragraph and it is written in the hand of the compiler, which is a small upright hand that does not hurry and does not editorialise, and he read it, and then he read it again, and then a third time over a period of about forty minutes with three bad cups of water, and then a fourth time at the copying floor that evening with a sheet of paper and a sharp pencil he had had to sharpen because it was the pencil he had not sharpened in a month and it was blunt.

This is what it says, in substance and as near as a man may bring a two-hundred-year-old hand down to the present without cheating:

The Registry of Continuant Works has, in the case of a district return made for a named public work, done four things together and has never done any of them separately, and the four things are the precedent.

*That it has recognised the return.* It is entered in the office's own book under the district's number and the work's name, and thereafter the office treats the named work as one the district performs, and the district's performing of it is not a claim upon the office and is not a status and confers no right in respect of the work's condition or use.

*That it has entered a sum against the named work.* An annual sum, in the district's case one hundred and forty pounds, entered against the work and not against the district, called in the instrument a **maintenance grant**, and paid on the district's production of the work's own record and not on the office's.

*That it has done both of these in writing, in the district's own quarter, on the district's own day.* Not afterwards. Not on a review. On the day the district asked.

*And that it has done them on four grounds, which are the whole of the ground there is.* A roll. Two witnesses with interests separable from the district's. The duty performed in public, at the place, on the days, in the manner the return names. And a schedule of the refusals the office had made in that quarter, published.

Owen Rusk put the pencil down.

The last one. The *last* one. Not last in the paragraph, which it was, but last in his head, and it sat there, and he turned it over.

The first three are the ward's. A roll — the ward has one, in Marguerite Bray's hand, and it has been kept for thirty-six years. Two witnesses with separable interests — the ward has two, and one of them has written a page about how *not* separable she is. The duty performed in public, at the place, on the days, in the manner the return names — the ward has been doing that for three weeks at seven forty in the morning in the rain, and it is going to do it on Friday, and it is going to do it on the Friday after that.

The fourth is not the ward's.

He sat in the copying hall on the second floor at half past six in the evening of the thirtieth day of the month with a clean sheet in front of him and wrote the precedent out properly, in the order the Office keeps things in, which is the only order in which anything in this building can be examined.

**What the precedent does not do.** It does not say that the Registry is obliged to honour a return. There is no *must* in it anywhere. Owen asked for the volume a second time on the Friday morning and read 37/C a fifth time with a pencil in his hand to look for one, and there is not one, and there is not one anywhere in the two hundred and nine words, and the absence of a *must* in a document of this kind is not an oversight, it is the architecture.

**What the precedent does do.** It records what the Registry has done before, four times over, in one place, in one instrument, and the fourth ground of the four is a schedule of the office's refusals for the quarter, published.

And a precedent in this Office is not a rule. A precedent is a record. It is the Registry's own entry of what it has done, and it compels nothing by itself — the seal-stair clerk had said something to that effect about a hold once, and Owen had not understood it then — and the reason a precedent works is not that it obliges anybody. The reason a precedent works is that it is the Registry's own hand, and a clerk may not rely on an argument that the Registry's own record contradicts, and a district may stand in front of a refusal and say *show me the schedule* and the refusal has nothing else to say.

So the precedent does not compel the Registry to honour a return. It compels the Registry, before it refuses one, to have published a schedule of what it has refused.

A schedule is a list. A list of every district the office has refused in the quarter, with the reason, in one sitting, in a numbered column, published.

Owen sat with that for about a minute and a half, and then said out loud, to the emptying hall:

"I have not won anything."

He had won something. He had found out that a ward which performs a duty in public is the kind of district this Office has paid before, and that it paid in writing, and that the sum went on the work and not on the ward, and that the ward's repair would still be the ward's repair and the crossing would still be closed, and one hundred and forty pounds a year is not four thousand one hundred and sixty, and the grant for Lower Lock's pump is not made whole and is not going to be.

What he had won was this, and it was worse, because it was smaller and colder and had nothing to do with his ward at all: the Registry of Continuant Works keeps a published record of its own refusals, or is required to, by its own hand, in its own book, in the Concord Year the precedent was made.

He wrote four lines in the register and stopped, and then wrote a fifth, which he marked:

*I have spent a month as though the fight was to be forgiven. It is not. Nothing in this office forgives anything. The fight is to be LISTED. Not forgiven — LISTED. A refusal that is on a numbered column in a published schedule, with a reason, in a quarter, cannot be quietly repeated, and cannot be explained afterwards as a mistake, and the man who strikes it is on the same line as the thing he struck. That is all this document gives me and it is more than I have had in a month.*

---

He did not go up the hill and tell the ward. He read the precedent four times and he went to work and he did a hundred and forty sheets of comparison between four o'clock and half past seven and he did not tell Pell, and he told nobody at all, and at half past seven on the Wednesday evening he went up the hill on the footpath with a sheet of copying paper in his coat with the schedule of the Coldwater matter on it in his own square hand, because a schedule is a list of numbers and dates and a ward's acts, and it is the sort of thing a man takes to a hall, and he had carried it up the hill in the inside pocket on the left and he was going to leave it on the table and go away and let it be read.

He stood in the doorway of the ward hall at half past eight on the thirtieth day of the month and gave it to Marguerite Bray and explained in about ninety seconds what it was, and she read it standing up, which takes longer than sitting down, and she said one sentence, which was *that is somebody's good week*, and gave it back to him, and then, after Owen had put his hand on the door frame and was about to leave, said the second sentence, which was:

"Leave it out on the table tonight and do not take it up at seven forty tomorrow, and go home and sleep, and let Ada find it, because I have been in this ward thirty-six years and I know which of the two of you is going to be able to use it and it is not the one with the Registry's card."

And on the Wednesday night, while Owen Rusk walked down the hill with his hands in his coat pockets and nothing whatever in the inside pocket on the left, because he had done as he was told, Marguerite Bray read a schedule of a precedent case aloud in a granary to a dozen people at the double count, because a man from the mill had asked what the paper was and she had handed it to him, and he had read the second column out loud because it is a column of dates and hours and a column of dates and hours can be read aloud to anybody at all.

And by the Thursday morning the ward knew.
