# Chapter 22 — Forty-One

The answer to a narrow question on a Tuesday is fast, and the answer to the question Owen Rusk went down to the ground floor with at ten past seven on the twenty-ninth day of the month was not a question at all. It was a single sheet.

A **schedule of a redacted batch** is a list of entry numbers and nothing else, and it is the only part of a redaction the Office gives away for nothing, and Owen had worked out why by about half past seven while he was walking down to the counters and had it in his own words before he got there: because a list of numbers is not a record of a person, and a list of numbers costs the Office nothing to make and can be made by a clerk at a counter in ninety seconds.

What it cannot do is tell him anything. He knew that. He had gone down there anyway, because the size of a thing is the first thing a man needs and everything after it is detail.

The clerk brought it out in about four minutes, on a single sheet, initialled and dated and handed across the counter with the two fingers that clerks use to indicate that a thing is going away from them. Forty-one lines, numbered from one to forty-one in the officer's own hand, and against each line a **residence-status entry number** and nothing else. No names. No addresses. No quarter-by-quarter reason, because the ground is on the batch in the Redaction Register and repeating it forty-one times would make it longer and not truer.

Owen read it standing at the counter because there was nowhere to sit, and it took him about four minutes, and he did not copy it, and he did not take his notebook out in the room, which was the second time in two days that he had made himself do a thing he wanted and he was becoming aware that he was making a habit of refusing himself things and that a man who is always refusing himself things is a man who has begun to need a rule.

"Thank you," he said.

"That's the whole of it," said the clerk. "You may have a copy of that, if you ask, and if you ask, we make one, and it goes in a file with your name against it, which you already know."

"Then I will not ask for a copy," said Owen, "and I would like that noted somewhere, because somebody in this building is going to look at the schedule and see that I have seen it and is going to assume I have a copy."

The clerk looked at him for a moment longer than the transaction required.

"That's the first time anybody has said that sentence at this counter," she said, "and it is correct, and I am not going to write it down, and you should know that not writing it down is not the same as it being untrue."

---

He read the schedule twice more in the index room with a pencil he did not take out of his pocket, and the thing he was looking for was not in it and he found it anyway.

The third column on the batch had been *days pending*, and every one of the forty-one rows had said eleven. He had assumed — he understood, sitting under the slope of glass, that he had assumed it on Monday in a room with a chair in it — that eleven days was somehow the interval attached to his mother's particular row. His mother's claim had been about her address, and hers had been pressed by a boy of sixteen who had come upriver in the wet, and if the Office had let a claim sit for eleven days that was a thing about how busy they were in a flood year, and Owen had a whole month of practice at understanding the Office as a mechanism that jams.

It was not his mother's.

It was the batch's. All forty-one of them, every one, without a single exception, the same number in the same column in the same hand: the quarter's arrears, cleared in one afternoon, by a man who went down a column of forms and put a date against forty-one of them and did not open any of them.

Eleven days is not a delay. It is a *sitting*. Somebody cleared a backlog the way you clear a backlog: not one at a time, with a decision, but in a single afternoon with a straight edge, and every family that waited that afternoon waited exactly as long as every other family, and the equality of it is the thing he would carry around in his pocket for the rest of his life.

He wrote it down in the notebook on the stair, in the four lines, and then wrote under it the sentence he actually needed:

*The eleven days is the batch. If it had been twenty-six I would have felt better and I would have been able to argue about it. Forty people waited the same length of time on the same afternoon and not one of them chose it and not one of them was asked.*

---

And then, because he had a whole day and no reason to go up the hill until the evening, he did the thing that was actually going to be the shape of the next eight days, and it happened by accident, and he did not know it for four days.

The Fourth River Desk keeps a **route list**. It is not a filing and it is not a record; it is the working list of the week's correspondence, by route, by bag, by hand, and it is the one thing in the copying hall that a clerk reads without thinking, because it is read on a Friday and thrown away on a Saturday. Entry numbers go on it. A letter that will not go up the route because the person is not at the address, or the ward is unfiled, or the address is a place rather than a house, comes off the route and into a tray, and it goes on the route list the following week with the same number and a mark beside it.

Owen Rusk had kept the Fourth River Desk's route lists for nine years, and had copied two hundred and forty of them, and had never once read one as anything other than a list of things that had to go somewhere.

He went and got the sheet he wanted out of nine years of route lists he had never had cause to keep. It is a Thursday sheet, the eleventh of a month in Concord Year 471, because a Thursday is when the week's bag closes for the Friday post upriver, and he sat on the end of the second desk with it flat on the board and read the columns.

He was looking for residence-status entry numbers, which is not a thing route lists are sorted by, and he found them anyway, because he had spent nine years reading this office's numbers and the eye for the shape of an entry number is the only qualification anybody in this building actually gets.

Three of the forty-one numbers on the schedule were on it.

Not in the current week. In the *return* column, at the right-hand end, under a head that said *carried*, with a mark beside them, and a carried entry on a route list means one of two things: the item could not be delivered and is being held, or the item is a person's own paper being carried to a counter by somebody else because the person could not come.

He read the three marks. He did not copy the three numbers; he wrote them in his notebook, once each, and the notebook is a register and a register is not a library and he had made that decision on a Sunday with a reason he could still argue for.

The first was a quarterly roll notice carried to a public counter on a carrier's authority. Ordinary. That happens perhaps twice a quarter and it is a form that anybody in a ward hall can read out to whoever is holding the pen.

The second was the same, a different quarter, a different route, a person in a place rather than a house.

The third was a form 12-B, carried, unmarked, in the week of the eleventh of the month in Concord Year 471.

Owen Rusk put the sheet down very carefully, squared it to the edge of the board, and sat there for the better part of a minute and a half without moving, and then he did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was the worst thing that had happened to him since a boy had come out of a spillway alive on the second week of the month.

Thirty-seven of the forty-one on that schedule were a stranger. Three of them were a form in a bag. One of the three was a **form 12-B** — which is the form a person presents at a county intake sub-table asking for a district consolidation schedule naming their district's public works — and it had been carried to a counter upriver in the week of the eleventh of a month in the fourth quarter of Concord Year 471 by somebody who was not the person it belonged to.

He had copied that line. Nine years ago, when he was twenty-three and in his second year on the desk, he had copied four hundred route lines in a week and had done the form 12-B line in the same pen and at the same speed as the rest, and had not looked at it twice.

He had been a mechanism. That was the shape of it and it was exact. He had carried no knowledge and had decided nothing, and he had been one of about nine pairs of hands that made a piece of paper travel from a house in this city to a counter two miles upriver on a Tuesday, and whatever had come of that line, if anything had, had come of the paper. Owen sat on the end of the second desk and understood, in the way he understood most things, about nine seconds after it happened and about two hours after he needed to, that he had spent a month building a case in which every wrong thing in it was somebody else's deliberate act, and that somewhere under the case there was a layer made of his own handwriting.

He wrote it down. He wrote: *I carried one of them.* He wrote it and looked at it and did not improve it and did not cross it out.

---

He found Pell at the second desk at about half past two with the tray between them and nothing in it, which is the only hour of the day she is ever unoccupied.

He said it straight, because he had learned in four weeks that she will not give you a courtesy and that the attempt to earn one costs a whole conversation.

"I have a schedule of forty-one entries in my notebook as three numbers. Three. I did not copy the list. I went through nine years of route lists and found three of the forty-one numbers on them, and one of them is a form 12-B carried to a sub-table counter in the week of the eleventh in the fourth quarter of four hundred and seventy-one, and I copied that line myself when I was twenty-three."

Pell did not say anything for a moment. Then she took the tray off the board and set it on the floor on her own side, which is what she does when she is going to say something she has said before.

"Yes," she said. "That is what the desk is for. I will tell you the thing nobody tells you, and then you will not be able to un-hear it, so I am telling you it straight instead of being kind about it."

"All right."

"You are not going to make a list of forty-one names." She said it as an instruction and then, immediately, as a fact, and the second half was the part he needed. "You have no right to forty names you have never spoken to. Not by the register rule — I do not care about the register rule; the register rule is the easy half. You have no right to them because a name in your hand is a thing you can use, and you will use it, and the day you use it will be the day you stop being the person who noticed."

"I was not going to."

"You were going to have thought about it for a day and a half, which is worse, because by then you would have had reasons."

He had, in fact. He did not say so.

"Here is the procedure, and it is the whole of what exists, and you are going to hate it because it is arithmetic." Pell put her elbows on the board. "A person who may be affected by an entry may ask for it. That is the rule. You used it on Monday and you were entitled to and you were the only person in the world entitled to. Now: the other forty would each have to ask. Separately. Each of them, at a counter, by name, in person, one at a time, over a period of weeks, and thirty-seven of them do not know there is a schedule, and three of them are on a route list in a bag somewhere, and one of them has been dead for fifteen years."

"And the Office knows that."

"The Office built it," said Pell. "Not dishonestly. That is the thing you keep having to learn. Somebody sat in a room and decided that a clerk should be able to be told the size of a thing without being able to read the thing, and the reason is a good one, and the result is that forty-one people were put in a batch in one afternoon and the only lawful way to find out is forty separate acts of courage by forty separate people, and the cost of that is a queue at a counter and a clerk's time and a day off, and it is paid by them and not by the office."

She stopped, and looked at the corner of the board, and Owen understood that she was about to say the thing she had come down to say and had been building to for about a minute.

"So I am going to ask you the question you have not asked and you are not going to like the answer, and if you do not like it you are not to argue with me about it, because I have thought about it longer than you have."

"Ask it."

"Has anybody ever asked forty people at once."

Owen did not answer straight away, and the delay was not because he did not know.

"No," he said. "Not in this building. Not in my time. There is no instrument that compels it, there is no register that would hold the forty requests together, and if a clerk went up to a ward and said *forty of you have been in a batch*, the answer from the hall would be a question about whether he was mad, and the answer from this office would be that he was wrong, and both of those would be correct and the forty people would go home."

"Right," said Pell. "And that is the answer to your question, and the answer to the question I have not been asked is worse. **Nobody has ever needed to until now.**"

"Which is not a compliment."

"It is not a compliment," said Pell, "and it is not a criticism, and I have been turning it over for four days and I cannot make it into either one, so I am going to leave it on the table. Four hundred and eighty-seven years, and forty-one people in a batch is not a scandal, it is a Tuesday. That is what I want you to hold. Everything we have done for a month has been built on the assumption that somebody did this *to* somebody, on a day, for a reason. And the truth is that this Office has been doing this as a matter of routine for longer than the ward has had a name, and it will go on doing it on a Friday, and the reason is not that anybody is careless."

She got up and picked the tray off the floor.

"Write down what you actually have," she said. "Not what you feel about it. What you have."

He wrote it out that night, in the four lines, and then he read it and added the last line, which was the only speculative thing in the register and which he marked as speculation because that is what a register is for:

*What I have: a batch of forty-one, one sitting, one officer, one interval throughout. A schedule of forty-one numbers, seen, not copied. Three of the forty numbers in my own hand from route lists, one of them a form 12-B carried in the week of the eleventh. The batch number, the officer's initials, the date struck. Nothing else. Nobody has asked anything. The Office has not explained anything and is not going to.*

*What I do not have: a name, an address, a person's face, a second day in the same row, or any instrument on this earth that would let one man see the other forty.*

*What would: a district return's schedule, or a ward roll produced in a review. Both are public documents when a review is held. There is a review on Friday. There is a ward in this city that keeps a roll by hand and has eight branches in it, and a granary committee that has been writing down what the ward's paper says for three weeks, and I am not going to put my book on that table and I would like to be struck for saying it out loud.*

---

He was at the tray at a quarter to eight that evening when the copying floor had emptied and the lamps were still on over the second desk, and he did the day's work out of the drawer the way he had done it every day for a month, and the drawer was where the unrouteable correspondence went, and the unrouteable correspondence is the only part of the Registry's daily work that arrives already finished: it has been to a counter, it has been returned, and it is going nowhere, and it does not have a schedule, and it is the reason a man posted to a tray is in the building at a quarter to eight.

The rack on the ground floor at Mill Ward had seven returns in it. He had counted them in the fourth week of the month, in a queue, with his back to a man holding a parcel wrong. There was a bundle on top of them and there had been a bundle on top of them then, and the bundle had been there most of a year, and nobody had come back for it.

He thought about that for about a minute — about the route lists, and about a batch struck in one sitting, and about a bundle on a rack that nobody has come back for — and understood that those are the same shape and that he was tired and that he was going to bed.

Down at the top of Fetter Lane the standpipe was running and there was a light in the pump house, and the granary book said *3 weeks and a bit*, and the marker would be set at seven forty in the morning by two people on the top path, and the return came back to the tray on Friday whether anybody thought it was worth anything or not, and thirty-seven people in a building two miles upriver would never know that they had been in a batch, and one of them was his mother, and he had said his own name out loud at a counter on Monday and could not un-say it.
