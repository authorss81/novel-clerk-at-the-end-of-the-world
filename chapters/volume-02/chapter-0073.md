# Chapter 73 — What the Room Is For

He worked out on the Saturday morning of the seventeenth day of the second month of the Concord Year 488 what the question was, and it took him a couple of minutes, and then he spent the rest of the day and the whole of the Sunday being certain of it in the wrong way. The question was this. **Does the Count have a word for a settlement, and if it has, where is it written.**

He was certain this was about arithmetic. He was certain of it because a definition is a procedure and a procedure is the second limb, and because he had been six weeks in a building learning that everything in this county is a document and a document is a thing you may put in a box, and because the sum was made and the copy was got and the letter was written and none of it had once produced a definition of the thing that had been chosen. He was also certain that he was not examining the schedule, because a definition of a settlement is not the schedule and is not a choice of places, and there was a difference and he knew exactly where it was, having drawn it on the back of a blank form about a month ago in about ninety seconds.

He got it wrong, and the reason he could not see that he had got it wrong was that he wrote the question down seven times over two days and every one of the seven was the same question.

---

The records room of the count at the Glass Stair is one floor below the counting clerk and it is a public room, and its door stands open on a latch because it is open to anybody who asks at the counter. He had stood in the doorway of it twice. Once on the Friday afternoon of the second day of the month for about four minutes, and once on the morning of the seventh for rather less, and on both occasions the book on the table by the window under a weight was the **schedule of draws**, and on both occasions he had identified it, and on both occasions he had walked away, and the reason was the same on both occasions and it is the whole of his standing in this season in one sentence: an examiner may not examine the schedule, and the schedule of draws is where the schedule comes from, and if he opened that book and it was what he thought it was then he would be holding a document about the schedule, and there is one thing he is permitted to do with a document about the schedule and it is nothing.

So on the Monday morning of the nineteenth he did not open the schedule of draws. He went in, because a public room that you stand in the doorway of twice is a room you are making a decision about twice a week, and there was a question in him, and the question had to be asked of a person and not of a book, and the person who answers at a counter in that building is a counting clerk at the second counter who has a slate and no coat and no name on anything.

He went in at a quarter to eleven. There were four people in the room and a queue of two at the public stand, and the standing extract was not in the room, and on the table by the window under a weight was the schedule of draws with its covers off, and a second table had a bound ledger on it that he did not identify and did not try to.

Along the wall under the window there was a range of drawers, and one of them had a cord wound round the handle and through the hasp, and the cord was black, and it was not this season's, and he did not go within four feet of it and he did not touch it and he did not think about it, because there is no version of this in which thinking about it helps anybody.

"There is no room in here," said the counting clerk, from the corridor. "And there is going to be a queue at that stand by the middle of the month, and if you have come to read a book then you have come on a good day, and if you have come to ask me something then you have come on a bad one."

"I have come to ask you something."

"Then ask it at the counter and not in here, because a question asked in a room with a door is a conversation, and a question asked at a counter is a record, and you are an officer of the record and you know the difference better than anybody in this building."

---

It took a couple of minutes at the counter, and there were nine people in the room, and the rail has three sides and a public stand on it, and about four of the nine looked up.

"**Does the Count have a definition of a settlement,**" said Owen Rusk, "and if it has one, where is it written. I am asking about the procedure and not about the schedule, and I want those words on the record."

The counting clerk did not answer for a moment.

"Say the second part again."

"I am asking about the procedure and not about the schedule."

"Write it on the slip in your own hand and give it to me and I will put it in the tray." She took a blank slip off the spike and pushed it across. "**You have just made a document in a public room and I want you to understand that you have made it and that there is no putting it back.** Everything said at this counter is a record. That is not a complaint about you. It is the design. The rail is there so that a person may say a thing in front of nine other people, and it works, and that is the whole reason for the rail."

"I know there is a rail."

"Then you know what you have done, and you are going to find out what it costs in about three hours, because a question like that one has a form sitting in a drawer with its name on it, and the Office has got carefuler, and careful means the same day." She wrote nothing on the slip. "I am not going to answer it here. I am going to answer it in a form, because the form is what this office has, and the form will be correct, and the form will be sufficient, and it will be the answer in every quarter for as long as I am on this counter, and nobody is going to improve it, because a sufficient form is the hardest thing in this city to move."

---

She came back into the corridor about ten minutes later and stood with her back against the wall, which is not a thing a clerk at a counter does, and Owen Rusk stood about four feet off and let her.

"I want to say one thing to you and it is not a complaint and you will know that in about three seconds," he said.

"Then say it."

"You told me on the second of the month that two hundred people have been into that room in nine years and not one of them has read down a column. I have been thinking about it for a fortnight and I have had it wrong twice and I have got it right this morning, and I would like to tell you what it is, because you are the only person in this county who could use it."

"Say it, and then I will tell you what I can do with it, which will be nothing, because I am a clerk at a counter."

"**It is not that they are careless.**" He said it flatly, the way a person says a thing they have spent a day arriving at. "A column you may read down is a column of things you are entitled to. You are entitled to your own figure and you know your own figure before you come in, and you go straight down to it and you go away, and that is not stupidity and it is not discourtesy. That is a person behaving correctly. That book is a column of things somebody else has. **Reading it is an act. Acts have to be wanted. And there is nothing in this building that has ever made anybody want one, and there is not going to be, and you and I are not going to change it, and it is not a moral failure. It is a counter with a rail round three sides of it and nine people in it.**"

The counting clerk did not say anything for three or four seconds.

"**Nine years**," she said.

"Yes."

"Nine years, and I have wanted somebody to work that out since about the third year, and I have wanted it about once a year, and I have never said it to anybody." She folded her arms. "Because a person who comes in and says a thing like that is a person who is about to leave, and I have watched four of them leave in nine years and three of them were right to. And I am not going to write it down, because there is nowhere in this building to write it down. **There is no column for a thing a clerk has worked out about her own counter, and that is the same defect as a fifth column, and it is the same defect as a reason box, and it is the reason I have wanted it for six years and the reason it has not happened.**"

"Yes."

"So you will take that away and use it, or you will not, and either way nothing in this room is different on Tuesday, and I would like you to have said it out loud here, because I have not had anybody say it out loud here and I am going to remember that somebody did."

She went into the corridor and then stopped and came back three steps, which she had not done all morning.

"One more thing, and it is smaller. You said you wanted *about the procedure and not about the schedule* on the record. I have read the slip. I did not put those words anywhere. **I am not going to put them anywhere. If you want them on a record then the only place they can go is the form, and the form does not have a line for what a person is not asking about.** In about four years somebody is going to read that form and see a question put by a Continuance Examiner of the Count, Docketford district, and there will be nothing in it saying that he was careful, and nothing in it saying that he was not careful, and that is the correct outcome, and I would like you to notice that I could have made it the other one and did not."

---

The form came back at twenty past two on the same afternoon, at the second desk, out of the tray, in the hand of a counter.

Owen Rusk read it standing up in a minute and then sat quite still at the end of the desk for about five minutes, with the form face up in front of him, and then turned it over, which is a thing a man does to a form when he wants to be sure he has read every line of it.

**THE OFFICE DOES NOT MAINTAIN A LIST OF THE SETTLEMENTS OF THIS DISTRICT. A settlement, for the purposes of the Count, is a settlement of record; that is to say, a settlement appearing in a return of premises of record made by the district office and received by this office. The district office is not required to define the lines of its own return. A line against which a quantity is authorised is a line. The Office does not examine the basis of a quantity. This is information and is not a filing, and the Office will not answer it as one in any other quarter.**

He found the fourth clause in about nine seconds and then spent the rest of the afternoon on it.

**A line against which a quantity is authorised is a line.**

He got the district's own **schedule of premises of record** out of the room on the second floor he has the key to and has been in about nine times in nine years — a bound book, four columns, number, place, street or lane, date of entry, the district's, not the count's — and he went down the second column with a pencil, one line at a time, and asked of every line the only question the form had left anybody.

*Is a quantity authorised against this line?*

He did it in about two hours and forty minutes and he did not copy anything out, and the answer is the true shape of the whole of this season and he is the only person in this county who has it.

**The schedule of premises of record in the district office contains a great many lines that are not places at all, and a quantity is authorised against a number of them, and nobody has ever gone down that book asking, because the book is a column of things somebody else has, and going down it is an act, and there is nothing in this building that makes anybody want one.**

**A building where the count is administered has a standpipe in it, and a stove, and a yard, and a person who washes in it, and it is a line in a book, and a quantity is authorised against it. That is all a settlement has ever been in this county.**

He put the pencil down and did not write it anywhere, because if he wrote it anywhere it would be a document about the schedule, and there is one thing he may do with a document about the schedule.

---

He went down to the field at about half past four because a board is a thing a man can look at instead of a person, and on the smaller frame at the foot of the standing one was a standing extract that has been on that frame every season for a hundred and forty years.

**PLACES STRUCK FROM THE COUNT OF THIS DISTRICT. Place. Year of the correction. Premises struck. Reason.**

Four hundred and six rows. He read the heading, which he had read before, and then he stood about two feet off it for about two minutes and did not read the fourth column, and the not-reading was deliberate and it was expensive and the reason is on the page and is not a mystery: a fourth column on a board and a drawer in a building are the same decision, and he made that decision on a stair in the last month of last year, and a man does not get to make it twice in a season.

The year four hundred and sixty-one is in the third row from the bottom and he did not go and look for it, and the place in that row is a ward that was officially closed in that year, and the man of that ward still has no name, and Owen Rusk stood in a field for two minutes on the Monday afternoon of the nineteenth day of the second month and gave the whole thing up for a second season in a row.

---

He did the tray on the Monday evening and was on it at a quarter past six, and there is a thing he has wanted all season and had not said, and he said it out loud to a tray at about half past seven with the lamp out, and it belongs in the place it happened because it is the only part of that fortnight that was his own fault.

The fourth column on the board at Cutter's Fields is his. It went up on the ninth day of the first month in a hand that is not a clerk's and is not a printer's, on the word **granted** written in pencil, with no authority in the form behind it and no condition and no note. He asked for it in a room and got it. Ten of the eleven reasons on it are the premises column written twice and the eleventh is a figure of four doors of nine, and it is a column of figures, and he has said so in a hall and in a field and in a room with high windows, and it has never once contained anything he wanted, and in four years somebody is going to use it as an example of a thing an officer was allowed to do.

And on the Monday afternoon a building in another city put six words in the middle of a form — *a line against which a quantity is authorised is a line* — and every one of the five clauses around them is sufficient and one of them is correct, and the form will now be the answer in every quarter.

**The Office has got carefuler. It has not got kinder, and it is not going to, and the reason it has got carefuler is a season in which a clerk in Docketford asked for things in public rooms and got them.**

He had wanted the fourth column to have his name taken off it. He had said so in a room with high windows on the twenty-fourth day of the month and had meant it as modesty, and it had not worked, and it was not going to work, because that column is the only thing in this season that any office in this county gave anybody for nothing, and there is not going to be a second one.

Then he thought about the word *granted*, in pencil, with no authority behind it, and about the tick in the top right corner of the district return with the fifth column on it, three days before this district was asked to complete it, and he worked out that the two of them were the same fact. **An office that grants things in pencil to a person who asks in a public room is an office that has found a way of answering a person without saying no.** And he was the person who found it, by asking, in a field, in about nine minutes, in front of thirty people, on the ninth day of the first month.

He put the pen down at about eight and did not leave until about ten past nine, and the week had produced one definition and the definition was six words in the middle of a form, and it says that the list of places this county will look at in a season is made out of the buildings somebody happened to write down, and that nobody has ever been asked whether a building where the count is done is a place, and that there is nowhere on any form in this county to put that question, and that a person who asks it at a public counter gets five clauses back the same afternoon, and that the five clauses are correct.
