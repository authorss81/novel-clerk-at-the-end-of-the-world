# Chapter 35 — The Line in Bray's Book

The marker went in at seven forty on the Thursday morning of the forty-fifth day of the month with three people on the top path and two at the path end, and the pump had been primed at four and took, and the water at the step was level with the sill, which it had not been on the thirty-eighth, and the whole of the river was doing what it had been doing since the flood stopped, which was nothing, quietly, at the same height, like a man who has finished an argument and won it.

The granary count had been done on the Wednesday night, two counts, bins against the book, by two people who are not the same person twice, and the write-up was the Thursday afternoon's work and took two hours and ten minutes, and it is the dullest act in the ward's return and it is the one that everybody reads.

It came out at **a fortnight and a bit**.

Bray wrote the day under it in the same pencil, in the pump book, on the page where she had been keeping the figure since the fifteenth of the month.

It was the difference between two weeks and a bit and a fortnight and a bit, which is about four days of a ward of two hundred and forty people eating, and it came off the board at about ten minutes to five on the Thursday afternoon in a hall with four lamps in it, and Owen Rusk was standing at the end of the long table and did not say anything about it, because it was not his number and had never been his number and the only thing he could have said about it was that a warehouse somewhere was full, and that is a thing that stops being true about a week after a flood and nobody in this city had thought about that yet.

And underneath the figure, in the same hand, Marguerite Bray wrote six words, and they were not about the granary.

*And then it will be a fortnight.*

---

She had the Fetter Bend sheet out on the table at four o'clock, under the lamps, with the seven household references on it and the delivery book open beside it, and she was doing the thing she had ruled in the book on the thirty-seventh: she was putting names to references.

*Household 14-B, Fetter Lane.* *Household 22, Fetter Lane.* Four of them were Fetter Lane. One was a court off Bell Row. One was a house on the water side. And one, the seventh, was not a house at all.

"It is a street," said Marguerite Bray.

"Read it out."

"*Alder Row.* No number, no house, no household, and the column says *place*, and in nine years of copying that column out of a county book I have never once seen it say *place*." She had her hand flat on the page, which she did instead of pointing. "It is a place. Not a house. And Alder Row is not in this ward and has not been in my roll since before I was keeping it, and it is not two miles upriver, and it is not a thing I have to write about."

"Is it a street that was closed?"

"It is a street that was *administratively closed*," said Marguerite Bray, "and there is a difference and I am not going to argue it at a table at four in the afternoon. It appears in one line of a county intake register against a year I have never had cause to look at, and the year is the year the transfer came, and I looked at it because I was doing the seven and the seven are done."

Owen Rusk was standing at the end of the long table with a hand on the back of a chair and he did not move for about a second and a half.

It was a second and a half. In six weeks he had been in rooms with women who read rooms for a living, and Ada Rusk had told him on the fourth week of the month that a man who writes less has decided that there is less worth having, and Marguerite Bray had once, in a doorway, refused to tell him why she would not be his.

In a hall with four lamps in it, on a Thursday afternoon, two and a half dozen people were watching each other, and in that second and a half every one of them noticed that Owen Rusk had stopped.

"Well?" said Ada Rusk.

---

He did not answer her. That is the part he could not afterwards make sound right, and he wrote it down in four lines that night and crossed none of them out.

He got the ward hall emptied by being useful at it, which is the only way anybody empties a ward hall: he asked the question everybody was holding, in the worst possible way, and asked it out loud, and made it Ada's to answer.

"Alder Row was closed in the Concord Year 461," he said. "Twenty-six years ago. It has been a name on a card in this city for a quarter of a century. And it has turned up in a county intake register in the intake year of the transfer, in a column that says *place* instead of *house*, in a column I have been copying out of that book for a fortnight because a woman of sixty-one asked me to leave it alone and then asked me to read it out instead."

"Somebody was living there," said somebody at the back.

"Somebody filed," said Owen Rusk. "And those are different, and I am going to say why, and then Mrs Bray is going to tell you the part that is on her page and not on mine, and I am not going to be the man who keeps it."

He put all four of the things he had on the table at once, and Ada Rusk looked at the table, and then at him, and said:

"Mr Rusk. You have just told thirty people that you know something. Stand up and say the rest of it, or sit down and say nothing, and if you sit down and say nothing then I am going to make you stand up and say the rest of it in about four minutes, and it is going to be worse, because by then I will have had time to think about it."

"I wrote it in my book at a quarter past four this afternoon," said Owen Rusk, "in four lines, and there is nobody in this room I would rather read it for me than me, and that is the answer to the question I was asked in this room a fortnight ago, and I would like somebody to write down that I answered it that way."

He put his hand on the page.

"It says: *Alder Row — 461. A district return was made out for that place in the intake year 486, in the same month as the transfer, and was brought to this ward's door, and this ward was asked to witness it, and this ward refused. It was taken two miles upriver and received for intake as a number in a column, in the same unbroken column as the transfer, four weeks after the transfer. The office that received it did not examine it. It has never been examined. The drawer is shut and I did not go near it. I am writing this as a filing and not as a mystery and I do not know what it is yet.*"

"That is four sentences too many," said Ada Rusk, "and you read them all in the same voice, and you did not do the thing I stopped you doing at twenty-four people in this room a fortnight ago, which is to choose."

"I read them word for word."

"You read them word for word, which is not the same as reading them, and you know the difference now, and I am not going to have the argument in front of them." She turned round to the room. "The Registry's man has written four lines in his own book and I have found one line in mine, and mine is older than his by about a year, and I am going to read it out, and then this room is going to do whatever it is going to do, and Mr Rusk is going to stand there and let it."

---

Bray turned the delivery book round and put both hands flat on the page.

"Inward column," she said. "A thing that came in. Seventeenth day of a month, Concord Year 486, about eight in the evening, in the wet, the same week the transfer came to the ward."

She read it.

"*Received at the door from a person not of this ward, one folded sheet, being a return made out for the place Alder Row, with two leaves and no witness to the return. Asked this ward to witness it. Not witnessed. Returned to the person the same night.*"

And then she turned the book about forty degrees so that the room could see the second line, and it was in a heavier hand, and it was not her hand, and it was not Ada Rusk's hand, and it was not Owen Rusk's hand.

"*I am a man of Alder Row. My name is my own. I am asking you to sign that you have seen me.*"

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

"That is his hand," said Marguerite Bray. "Not mine. A bad hand, and a slow one, and it took him about ten minutes, and he would not sit down, and he wrote that at the bottom of the entry because I told him that a person who brings paper to this door writes out what the paper is, so that the ward has a copy of what it said no to. That is the practice and it is thirty-six years old and I have never had to explain it to anybody before tonight."

"You wrote a rule for it."

"I wrote a rule for it," she said, "on the second week of a flood, for a man who came in with a paper in the wet, and I did not know then that it would ever be used again, and I have used it about nine times in thirty-six years and eight of them were people who could not read what the office sent them."

"What did you say to him?"

"I said what I have said twice in thirty-six years and once in this month, which is: this ward is not a district and cannot witness another district's return, and a return for a place that was closed in the Concord Year 461 cannot be witnessed by anybody in this ward, and here is your paper back." She squared the book. "And I said the true thing underneath it, which is: take it to Fetter Bend, because Fetter Bend will take it, because Fetter Bend is a table and a table receives."

---

Nobody moved for a while, and then Bram Tillet, who is a ferry cook and one of the four who voted no on the seventh day and had said nothing at all in this hall since, said:

"Then who was the hand?"

The question went round the room and nobody answered it and it was not meant to be answered, and Owen Rusk stood at the end of the table with his hand on the back of a chair and understood that the whole of what he had done in six weeks had been to put questions in rooms and that he had just watched a room ask a question that nobody would ever answer, and that it was better than any of his.

"I do not know," said Owen Rusk, "and I want to be exact about the next sentence, because I have spent a fortnight being exact about sentences. I could find out. There is a schedule of duties in that building with a name on it and a duty about **forms**, and the name on that duty is not a person I can be blamed for and the duty is not a filing anybody is waiting for. I could find out inside a week and I would not be able to say so afterwards, and I have told this ward twice in six weeks that I do not go looking for the hand that wrote a thing, and I am not going to start in a hall on a Thursday afternoon."

"He is not a clerk," said Ada Rusk. "There is not one thing in that line that is a clerk's hand. A clerk would have put his name. A clerk would have put a number on it. He wrote that he was a man of Alder Row and that his name was his own, and that is the whole of what a person can say at a counter when they have been told to say it a hundred times and have not."

"Then he walked two miles in the wet," said Ilse Mern, "with a return for a place, to a ward hall, at eight in the evening, to ask two women to sign that they had seen him, and Mrs Bray said no, and it was correct, and then he walked two miles in the wet the other way and put it on a table in a room upriver, and the man at that table wrote a number in a column and did not open it."

"And was it wrong?" said somebody.

"**No**," said Owen Rusk. "It was not wrong. That is the part I have been getting wrong for six weeks and I have only just worked it out standing at this table. Mrs Bray's refusal was correct. This ward is not a district and cannot witness another district's return. And the office upriver did the only thing a receiving table is built to do, and it received it, and in receiving it the office did not examine it, and the not examining was not a decision anybody made. It was the default. A ward refused it and a table took it, and between those two things nobody in the world looked inside a piece of paper, and it has been sitting in a drawer in this city for a year and a bit with the number on it in a column and *never opened* written on a card by somebody who wanted whoever came after to understand that it was a decision and not a failure of filing."

He stopped, and then he said the part that he had come in the room to say, and he had not known he was going to say it until it was out.

"And that is not a mystery. I wrote it in my book as a filing and I meant it, and I am going to hold to it, because the moment it becomes a mystery I will go and look in a drawer, and the drawer is four feet from where I sleep and it has a cord on it and I am not permitted to touch the cord and I am not going to ask anybody in that building for permission." He took his hand off the chair. "I would like to say the other half out loud, because there are people in this room who have been let read a roster and I have not been let read one, and that is fair. I have been in this ward for six weeks arguing about a bridge, and this is a line in a book. It is a line written by a man of about fifty in a bad hand at eight in the evening, in the wet, with a worse day than mine, on a piece of paper that got a number and nothing else. And I have to say that when I read it I felt — " and he stopped for a second, and did not find a better word than the one he had, which is a thing he has been able to do about three times in a month — " **I felt it in my hand.** Because a line can be picked up. A line is a thing you can hold, and a bridge is not, and I have been arguing with a bridge for six weeks and I have wanted, every single day of it, to be given something I could pick up, and that is the most frightening thing anybody has said in this hall this month and I have said it and Mrs Bray can put it in the book if she wants it in the book."

Marguerite Bray had the pencil out before he had finished the sentence and she wrote one line, in her own hand, with the day and the hour, and read it back, and it said:

*Received at the door from a person not of this ward, one folded sheet, being a return made out for the place Alder Row, asked this ward to witness it. Not witnessed. Returned the same night. The ward was correct and the return was received for intake and has not been examined.*

She ruled a line under it, and wrote the day underneath, and then she wrote one more thing, in the same hand, at the foot of the page, and she said it out loud as she wrote it so that the room had it.

"And I did not write his name," she said, "and I did not ask him his name, and he did not offer it, and I have thought about that every day for a year and a bit, and if he came to that door tomorrow I would not know him from the man who brings my coal."

---

Owen Rusk did not sleep much that night, and he did not go to the ground floor.

He got in at about half past ten and the plate was on the table and the lamp was on and he put the register down and did not open it, and then he opened it, at the last page, and wrote the date, and then he wrote the four lines about Alder Row again, and then he read them and struck out the last four words of the fourth line, which had been *and I do not know what it is yet*, because he did know what it was. It was a filing. It was a sheet of paper, made out by a man for a place, brought to a door, refused correctly by a woman, received for intake by a table, numbered, and never examined.

He wrote the words *as a filing* again, and underlined them once, and then he wrote one more line underneath, and it was the only speculative thing in the book and he marked it as speculation because that is what a register is for.

*It is not in Docketford and it is not on a table and I have told nobody in that building that I have read a line about it, and the only person on earth who knows I have it is a woman of sixty-one and a bridge mechanic, and neither of them can use it, and I can, and I am not going to, and if I find myself going to then somebody had better come and take this out of my coat.*

He shut the book and put it in the inside pocket on the left.

The drawer was on the ground floor of the Registry building, in a tray he was trusted with, with a black cord round the handle and a brass tab on the face, and he was forty feet from it at half past ten at night in a building with nobody else in it, and he went down the stairs and across the ground floor and stood in front of it for about a minute and a quarter, the way he had done in the second week, and then he went back up and sat down on the end of the second desk in a hall with the lamps off.

The index card said *never opened*, and the drawer said the same thing in a different hand, and a favourable reading is a thing a Registry can do to itself, and he had learned that on Monday in a hall with a runner's pencil going. The card was in the fourth volume on a table under a slope of glass and he had read it himself on the Sunday of the third week with his coat on: the number, the shape, the ward's name, the year, no forwarding office, no Registry witness, not examined, never opened. Every part of it he had got out of a book, and it had all been a line of numbers and a printed word with a space in it, and not one word of it had ever been a thing anybody had done.

The door in Lower Lock was not on the card. The refusal was not on the card. A man of about fifty at a ward hall door at eight in the evening in the wet, saying that his name was his own, was not on the card and was not on any card anywhere in this city and never would be, and that was the second frightening thing of the day, and it was worse than the first, and he had not said it out loud in the hall because a hall of thirty people is not a place to say the second one in.

At the bottom of the hill the standpipe was running and the granary said two weeks and the crossing was shut and the sixteen were still sixteen and the return came up the next morning and a woman in the hall had asked for nothing in six weeks except a sheet of her own hours, and a woman at a door a year and a bit ago had asked for two signatures and got a correct answer and a piece of paper back.
