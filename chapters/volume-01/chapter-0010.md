# Chapter 10 — Unfiled Correspondence

The continuation roll went on the board at eight on the morning of the fourteenth day, in the ordinary way, on a sheet of blue carbon with a weighted edge, and the first thing Owen Rusk read on it was his own ward.

*Ninth Weir public crossing. Lapsed — no supported district.*

And underneath, in the small column the Office uses when a thing has an explanation: *No district return has been entered performing the maintenance duty. The receiving district return has not entered performance. The last accepted district instrument for the maintenance of the named work is the transfer of the date entered, at the Mill Ward annex.*

It could not be argued. He tried, standing at the board with his hand flat on the carbon, and found nothing to put his hand on: no committee had struck the line, no clerk had entered it in a hurry, and nobody had to be blamed. The receiving return had not performed the duty and Lower Lock had not filed one, and the roll did not care which of those was anybody's fault, and it did not care that a petition sat in the Glass Stair docket a floor away with a docket number on it, because a petition is a request and this was a roll, and the roll went on every week whether anybody had asked or not.

The cruelest thing on the sheet was on the second line, where the roll records the documents it has considered.

*Considered: ward ratification, Lower Lock, entered as an act of the ward meeting, witnessed and sealed, resolution nine in favour and four against.*

Consent to a transfer. Not a district performing a duty. Both were true and both were in the file, and the roll had weighed them and found that one of them had nothing to do with a crossing. Thirty people had stood in a hall with a crack in the flue and voted and sealed and attested, and the city had written down every word they said, and what they had said was *yes, you may take the crossing away from us*, which is not the same document as *we will do it*.

Nobody else in the hall had noticed. He looked down the line of men reading the board and they were all reading their own wards, because a roll is a hundred and forty lines and a man's own ward is the only line that is not a stranger. He was the only person in the building who had read both documents.

So he said it out loud, to the hall, to nobody in particular, in the flat voice.

"It's here. The ratification. Two lines down. And it doesn't stop the roll, and I want it written somewhere that it doesn't stop the roll, because in three weeks one of you is going to come in here and say *but we voted*, and you did, and it wasn't enough, and that is not a reason to be ashamed, it is the entire design."

Pike, at the top of the stair, with his hat under his arm, said, "Write it in your own book, Rusk, not on my roll," and went up.

The rest of it came down the sheet in the ordinary way, four lines, and each of them was a small door closing.

The Fetter Lane pump grant was **suspended**. Not extinguished — suspended, with a date and a review and a route, which means nobody has decided about this yet, and while nobody has decided you get nothing, and nothing is not a refusal and is much harder to argue with.

Every household in Lower Lock could not be continued, licensed, or witnessed on a district act. Not struck. That was the part Owen read three times. Cobb Rennie's tin cup was still on the shelf above the pump and Ilse Mern's tarred board roof was still going soft at the ridge, and the ward was still there, and every adult in it was frozen in place like a photograph of a ward, because there was no district behind any of them now, and a person with no district behind him cannot be licensed to trade, cannot have a death entered, cannot have a birth acknowledged, cannot witness anything that matters, and can still be got up in the morning.

And underneath both, untouched, in force, entirely unaffected: the load-restriction suspension served on the tenth. So the crossing was closed *and* unsupported, which is worse than either alone, because a closed crossing at least has a status, a date and an appeal window, and an unsupported crossing has nothing behind it at all, and now it had both.

And the fourth thing was not on the roll at all. It was in a lane in Lower Lock at six the previous morning, with a light on in the pump house.

---

They had been at it since midnight. The pump had gone on the twelfth and had not come back; the reason was a rod that needed a new pin, and the pin had been at the mill's factor since the ninth, and the mill's factor had gone to Fetter Bend on Friday and was coming back on a Tuesday. So half the ward had been turning a hand pump in the dark for twelve hours, in shifts, in the rain, with a bucket and a lantern and a tally on a slate.

Owen had been there for four of them. He had wanted to give them money and Ada had stopped him at the pump house door with one flat hand, and when he said that his mother's burial had been disputed for eight months over a residence status, and this is the same, she looked at him for a long moment and said: "Yes. It is. And if I take your money tonight, then in six months when the return is entered there will be a sheet somewhere with your father's money in it, and I will have to hand it back or lie about it. I have a crew of nine and a hall stove that only burns when somebody pays for the coal. I am not doing any of that tonight."

So he turned the handle for four hours and went home wet to the elbow, and he learned, in a way no document had taught him, what twelve hours at a hand pump does to a person in a ward of two hundred and forty. He wrote it down, because he was a man who writes things down.

The granary was the other thing. Marguerite's book said nine weeks in a good year and four in a bad one, and what was in the bins on the fourteenth was a little over four weeks, and she had told nobody that number, and the nine-to-four vote had been nine in favour partly because half of them had been promised a roof and partly because the granary was four weeks from empty and nobody wanted to say so in a room with four witnesses.

And the four who had voted no were in a position no clause of any instrument in Docketford had been drafted to describe: they had refused to consent, the transfer had gone ahead on nine signatures, they were not on the return and they were not off it, and every day they walked into a meeting they were the people who had been wrong. The ward's own book says so, in four named lines, forever, on Owen's advice and at Marguerite's insistence.

---

At noon on the fourteenth day a runner from the Deputy Registrar's office put a posting slip on the rail of the Fourth River Desk, and it said that the Fourth River Desk copy clerk was detached to the tray of unfiled correspondence at the ground-floor counter, effective from the end of the day, on the ground of reorganisation of the copying floors.

"Unfiled correspondence," said Pell, reading it upside down. "Returned petitions. Notices for people the Office can't find. The one tray nobody watches, because the work has no status and so no schedule and no reviewer. You have been made the one place in this building where nobody looks at what you read."

"Yes," said Owen.

"You will be furious about this in a day," she said, "and you are not going to be furious now, because you have worked out that it is also the only posting in Docketford where a man can look at a closed file's index without a reason being asked for, and neither of us is going to say that out loud."

He did not appeal it. Appealing it would put Marguerite Bray's petition back in front of the Deputy Registrar, and the petition's whole value was that it was on a docket nobody had yet been forced to look at.

He worked the afternoon out. He copied the whole of the Day 14 roll entry into his own book — the lapse line, the four lines of explanation, the pump suspension, the freeze, the ratified line that did not count, the load-restriction suspension still standing — in full, with the date and the carbon number, because the carbon is the roll and the roll is not kept for anybody, and a ward's standing can be taken away in seven words on a blue sheet with no second copy anywhere.

And then he took the ward's finished return out of Marguerite's hands and carried it down to Pell's tray and put it at the back of it, square, with the top sheet visible.

"What's this."

"Lower Lock district return. Nine branches, two witnesses, four recorded dissents, a scope, a review date and an appeal route. Finished. It cannot be entered, because there is no qualified officer in this Office who is not named in it or in the case it answers." He put his hand flat on it, and then took his hand off, which was a thing he had made himself do. "There's a supervised tray on your desk and no assisting clerk on it. When one is appointed, that's the case, and it goes in the front of it, with the date it waited."

"You're not entering it."

"I can't. And if I were appointed in time, it would be a return entered by the clerk who copied the notice that caused the whole business, and somebody could say in a year that I filed my own case." He squared it again. "But I'm not leaving it in a drawer in a hall either, because a thing delayed on purpose is a different kind of thing from a thing forgotten, and if it sits on your tray with a date on it, then the delay is on the record, and it is the ward's delay and not mine."

Junia Pell looked at him for a long moment.

"That is the first sensible thing anybody has said to me about procedure since I came here," she said, "and I am not going to be able to tell anyone you said it."

"Put my name on the covering slip as the person who delivered it."

"That is not the same as entering it."

"It is not," Owen agreed, "and it is the only thing available, and I would like it in writing that those are two different sentences."

At half past three she came round the desk with a folded slip and did not sit down.

"Six names," she said. "Out of the thirteen. You asked me once and never came back, which I noticed and did not chase."

He unfolded it. Six names, and against each, in Pell's square hand: *custodian — none. current care act — none witnessed. two unrelated witnesses — cannot supply. appeal route — none.*

"That is a protective return with every part left out."

"Yes. A protective return needs two unrelated living witnesses, a current care act and a named appeal route, and it holds a child or a person who cannot answer for seven days at a time, and it never binds their future consent, labour, name, vote or private life. Lower Lock has none of the three parts. The one person in that ward who could be a custodian is sixty-one and handwrites a roll, and the child who matters most is twelve, and lives two doors from her with an aunt who is one of the four people this ward has already decided were wrong."

"So it cannot be done."

"It can be done in six weeks with a custodian, two witnesses who do not live together, a care act and a route to a complaint. None of those are in this ward, none are in this Office, and the man who could appoint an officer to help is the man currently writing a list of sixteen wards." She folded her hands. "You will not solve it, and nobody will solve it in the month you have. I am telling you now so that you do not spend three weeks believing you are two steps from it."

"Ivy Sarr is the twelve-year-old?"

"Wenna's niece. Tal's granddaughter. And he had no authority over her either, and there is nobody left who ever had any authority over her, which is not an accident. It is the design, and the design is a man in a good coat with two Registry witnesses and a leaf." She turned to go. "Do not tell me what you intend to do about it. I would rather find out from the ward."

---

The docket runner brought the Council circular up at four and put it on the rail, and it was still sealed, and Owen had no business opening it, and it sat on his desk for an hour and a half because he was working, and then he carried it to the seal stair and had it opened by the officer on duty in front of him, which is the correct procedure and which he followed exactly.

Two sheets. The first gave the countersignature standard a name at last: it was issued by the Provisional Council, it was a Council review standard and not a Docketford one, it was in force in all district offices, and every clerk in the hall who had been applying it for years now had an author, and the author was not a man in Docketford.

The second sheet was headed *local returns filed after the close of the present review*, and said that no district, ward, household or personal return submitted by a community after the close of the review quarter then current would be received by the district office, and that any such return would be returned to the filer unexamined, and that the quarter then current closes on the eighteenth.

Owen read it twice and then went and stood at the stair rail for a while.

It was not a law and it was not addressed to him. It was a Council instruction to a district office, perfectly regular, sealed and dated, and the second signature at the foot of the flap was in a small steady hand and was not the Registrar's, and there was nothing on either sheet to say who that second signature belonged to except that the Council has one Chancellor, and a Chancellor's name goes at the bottom of the Council's own instruments — and whoever reads it is therefore expected to have an idea what a Chancellor does, which is: send down a piece of paper that in four days will make it impossible for any ward in the delta to file anything at all.

"Is that the only thing that could supersede a stability order?" he said, out loud, to the stair.

The clerk on the seal stair, who was about fifty and had been there twenty years, did not look up.

"Form Zero," she said. "The Common Return. It is the only instrument that can take a stability order out of the file, because a stability order is a district instrument and a district instrument is answered by a return, and Form Zero is not a return, it is the end of the Mandate." She dipped her pen. "It has no valid signature path. It has never had one. There is a form and a schedule of witness conditions and nowhere on it to put anybody's hand. It is due in thirteen years, if the Mandate runs to it, and every clerk in this building has a copy of the first page in a drawer because you are required to have one, and nobody has ever filled it in, and that is the whole of what I know and I have been here twenty years."

"Half the ward is frozen this morning."

"Yes."

"And nothing you can do about it."

"No," said the clerk, "and I would not do it if I could, and the reason is on the first page in a drawer, and it says that no person may answer for another without that person's right to refuse. It is one sentence long, and it is the only sentence in this building that anybody has ever been afraid of, and I have never met a Chancellor and I do not want to."

---

Nell Arbour came to the ground-floor counter at about half past five, which is not a thing anybody does, and stood on the public side of it.

"You have got about four minutes," she said, "and I have walked here, and I am not staying, so say what you are going to say and then let me correct it."

"The return is finished and it cannot be entered, and no qualified officer in Docketford is not named in it, and the Council has just closed the route for community returns after the close of the quarter, which is in four days, and the ward lapsed this morning, and I have been put in a tray, and I have signed a statement against myself, and I am out of things I know how to do."

"Correct as far as it goes, and wrong about the order of it, which is your habit." She put a sheet of paper on the counter between them. "You have written the ward's appeal route. You did it on the first page, because I told you to, and you did it properly, and it is the only part of that return that will still work in a year."

"Meaning what."

"Meaning the return does not need to be entered this week. It needs to be *valid* eventually, and it needs an appeal route that is open now, and it has one, and a route that is open now is worth more than an entry that arrives after the eighteenth and is refused because of a circular." She tapped the counter. "So the thing you are going to do is not the entry. It is the route. And the route needs a person at the other end of it who is not you and is not me and is not the Office, and there are about four people in the delta who could be that person and two of them are women I have had words with."

"Ellis—"

"No. Here is the condition, and I am giving it to you in the street because in a room you would negotiate it. I will help with the appeal route. I will not be its witness. And I will not be yours." She said it without any weight at all, the way you read out a thing settled a long time ago. "Not because I don't believe you. Because an advocate who becomes one ward's person is that ward's person, and I told you why and you did not listen and I am not explaining it twice in the rain. And because the hardest part of this is not entry. It is asking people to be responsible in public, in a room, with their names on it — and you are not going to be able to do it for them, and every single thing you do from this counter is another thing you do for them."

Owen looked at her for a moment. There was a great deal he could have said, and what he said was:

"I drafted the notice. On the seventh day. I filled the scope box with a pointer to a document ten people had never read, and I initialled the clerk's line, and I let somebody else tick the box that says the thing is accurate, and I did not name the crossing and I knew it was one of the three, and I filed a statement saying so on Saturday, and that is the whole of what I have done for your ward and it is not enough."

Nell Arbour's face did not do anything at all.

"I know. I read it on Saturday. It is the first thing you have written in ten years that was not a way of not saying something."

She went. He stood at the counter with a sheet of paper in his hand that he had not asked for and had not read yet, and the ground floor emptied out around him, and the drawer he was now trusted with was four feet away with a cord round it and a brass tab on its face.

---

There is a drawer in the tray, and it is a good drawer, and it is the only one in the room with a lock on the side of it, a braided black cord round the handle and a brass tab screwed to the front, and the tab is not Docketford's brass and not Docketford's shape.

*ALDER ROW — 461.*

Owen stood with his hand an inch from it for about four seconds. It is not permitted to a clerk at the tray, and the cord is the notice: black cord is what the Registry puts on a record under an active redaction dispute, and a thing under a redaction dispute is not a thing a man opens because he has been posted to a tray where nothing is watched.

He did not open it. He took out the notebook and wrote off the tab its number, its shape and the date, and shut the drawer with the heel of his hand.

Alder Row. Four streets from his own front door, and in twenty years of walking past the end of that street he had never once spoken to anybody who lived there or bought anything in a shop there, and had thought, in the way everybody thinks it, *that's just the end of that street*.

461. Twenty-six years ago. A ward that officially closed, four streets from where he slept, and in this building, in a locked drawer in the tray where a clerk is not permitted to look, there is a file with its name on it.

He put the pencil down on the rail.

And then, in the last half hour before the lamps were lit, on his way through the tray with a bundle of unrouteable post in his arms, a name he had been copying for six years came at him out of an envelope and went straight out of his head, and he stood in the middle of a floor with nineteen letters in his arms and could not have said, if a man had asked him, who the letter was for.

It frightened him for about four seconds. Then he got the notebook out and found it — his own hand, three weeks ago, in the Fourth Desk's weekly route list, *Emery Tallack, counter, Fetter Bend, Tuesday* — and the name came back whole in the middle of a second. He stood there holding it, and could not have told you, if you had asked him at two that morning, whether he would have lost the next one.

He wrote the letter's number down. Then he did not know what to write next, and wrote, in the margin, in a hand that was not quite steady, the sentence he had said out loud at two in the morning of the eleventh to Junia Pell on a wet step: *I wrote it and I copied it down and I tore it up. If anybody ever asks, it was me, and the words are here.*

Then he shut the notebook, put the bundle down, and went home upriver in the rain to a room where there was nothing on the table but his mother's good plate and a lamp.

Behind him the Day 14 roll stayed on the board, in carbon, in blue, with twenty-six words on it that had taken a ward's standing away in the time it takes to read a line. Nobody was going to argue with it and nobody was going to unwrite it.

He was going to have to go and ask nine people for permission to stand in a room with their names on a page. He had four days before the Council's deadline, five before the answer was due, and no authority at all, and in his coat pocket a notebook with a dead man's receipt in it and a forgery in it and sixteen ward names and one brass number — and he had never in his life been less able to be the person who carried a case, and he had never been more certain that somebody was going to have to.
