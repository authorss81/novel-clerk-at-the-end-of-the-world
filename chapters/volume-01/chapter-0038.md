# Chapter 38 — The Far End, and a No

Arbour Kitchen on the Sunday morning of the forty-eighth day of the month had nine people in it, which was a Sunday, and Nell Arbour was at the back with her ledger open and a cup of something she had not drunk, and Owen Rusk came in at about ten with a wet coat and stood at the end of her table and did not sit down, and she let him stand there for about twenty seconds before she said anything.

"You have a face on," she said. "You have had that face since the Friday and it is a new one, and I am going to guess at it in front of you so that you can tell me I am wrong. You have been told to do a thing and you have worked out that you are the only person in this delta who can do it, and you have come up here to see whether a woman who has said four sentences to you three times would say a fifth one that is not a comfort but a standing."

"Yes."

"That is remarkably close and I am not going to ask how." She turned the ledger round. "I have been sitting here since Saturday morning with the same thing I have been sitting with all week, which is that a reviewing officer with a clock on the wall has put a question in a file in words somebody has to answer, and the answer is a person, and the person has to stand at the far end of a district's route, and there are four people in this delta who can do it and three of them have been used and one has not been asked anything at all."

"Who is the one who has not been asked anything."

"That is the last sentence I am going to say on that subject," said Nell Arbour, "and I have said it out loud instead of thinking it, which is what I do about once a year, and I would like you to notice that I have not given you a name and that you did not ask me for one, which is the first time in six weeks that you have come up this hill and not asked me for a name. I do not know what to do with that and I am not going to reward it by giving you a name."

"I came to ask you for something and I am not going to do it."

"Say it anyway, so that I can say no to it properly, because I have found that if I am not allowed to refuse a thing it turns into something else in about a week and I have enough of those."

---

Owen Rusk stood at the end of a table in a kitchen in Mill Ward on a Sunday morning and asked, as he had asked in the same room six weeks before, for the only thing he had.

"Will you go and ask her yourself," he said. "Not send her anything and not write to her. Go and stand on that landing the way you stand on landings, and say the ward's name, and she will come. You said in the second week of the month that when you send somebody they are yours, and that is the only reason it works, and I have four names in a column in a book and one of them is a woman you have had words with and one of them is a woman you have had words with who is not that woman, and neither of them will open a door for a Registry clerk, and you will."

"No," said Nell Arbour.

"That was fast."

"That was not fast, that was four seconds, and I have had it ready since about the fourth of the month." She put the pen down, which is the thing she does and the thing Ada Rusk does and the thing Marguerite Bray does, and Owen had noticed the resemblance in three different rooms and had never said so to any of them. "I am not going to explain, because I have explained, and I explained it to you in this room in the second week of the month in about six sentences and you wrote one of them down and have been carrying it since, and you are here again asking me for the same thing in the same words with a different woman, and the only difference is that this time you have framed it as a favour to me."

"It is a favour to you—"

"It is a favour to you," said Nell Arbour. "You are here on a Sunday morning and you have not asked me what I think. You have told me what a door will not open and you have observed that I am the sort of person who opens doors, and that is a description of me you have made in order to use me, and I want you to know that I can see it, and I am not going to be angry about it, because you are the only man I know who would rather be refused and told why, and that is a thing I have decided to keep liking about you and not to act on."

She put her left hand in her coat, which she did when she was going to say the true part.

"Here is the thing, and I am going to say it once and I am not going to soften it, and then I am going to go back to my ledger. The woman at Coal Hill and the woman at Wexford Landing have not spoken in two years, over a tide table, and I have been in the middle of that, and neither of them has ever blamed me out loud. If I go up that landing on a Sunday morning on a Registry clerk's errand then I have made the two of them into a thing that happened between me and them, and I will have done it in order that a man who lives in Lower Lock can get a Wednesday. And that is the one thing I have not done in nine years and I am not going to do it in a rain season, and you would not have wanted me to and that is the part of you I am not going to be sentimental about."

"Go and ask her," said Owen Rusk.

"Go and ask her," said Nell Arbour. "And do not tell her I sent you, because I have not sent you, and if you tell her that I have sent you then in about a year it will be true, because that is what happens, and then it will have been true and I will have sent you."

"And the four sentences."

"**I will help with the route and with nothing else. I am not a witness to your return. I am not an advocate for Lower Lock. I am not yours.**" She said them without looking up, at the pace she says things she has had to say more than once, and then she stopped and looked up, which she had not intended to do.

"I am going to add one thing and I have never added it before, and I am adding it because you are about to go and ask a woman for a standing and I would like there to be one sentence in the world about what a standing is that is not mine. I have said those four sentences to you three times as a **comfort**. I said them the second time, and I meant it, and I was glad to. That is what I am for; I am a person people come to, and I make the edges of the thing smaller, and that is a service and it is paid in bread and in eleven families at a time and I have never pretended otherwise.

"But that is a comfort. A comfort is a thing a person does **for you**, and it is done in a kitchen, and it is between two people, and it can be withdrawn on a Thursday by a woman who is busy. A **standing** is a thing a person does in a room where you are not the subject. It is done in front of strangers and it cannot be withdrawn, because withdrawing it does not undo it, it only stops the next one, and everybody in the room has already seen it. And I have been that, since Stonecross six years ago, and I would like you to go up that hill on a Sunday afternoon and ask for the second one, and I would like you to notice that you have spent six weeks asking me for the first one and there is a woman at a landing who has been giving you the second one to a stranger in a doorway in a yard ever since and you did not know which one you were asking for."

---

Coal Hill is a stone stair and a landing and a hut at the top of it, and the tide table is nailed to a post outside the hut in a frame with a glass in it, and the woman who keeps it is about fifty and has kept it for nineteen years, and she was on the boards outside the hut with an oil can and a rag when Owen Rusk came up the stair in the rain, having got the early boat and walked the last mile because a taxi would have cost a day's wage he did not have and would have arrived at two in the afternoon, which is too late to be told no kindly.

She did not look up.

"Registry," she said.

"Yes."

"You are the one from the bridge."

"Yes."

"Then put your bag down there and do not stand on my boards, because the rain comes off that hut roof onto about a foot of them and I have just got the whole landing mopped." She put the can down and looked at him. "And you have come up a stone stair in the rain at about half past one on a Sunday with a wet coat and something in your face, and I have not had anybody up here in two years who was not selling me a tide, so whatever it is, say it, and say it in about a minute because the light goes at three in this month and I have oil to do."

Owen Rusk put his bag down off the boards and said it in about a minute.

"I am asking you to stand at the far end of a district's appeal route. A ward in Lower Lock has a return with a route on the first page. The route is open and it has a place on it and a name at the front of it and nothing at the back. A reviewing officer has put in writing that a route with nobody at the far end of it is a piece of paper with an address on it, and he has asked the ward, once, to name a person. The ward does not know how to find one. I have four names in a column in a book and I have used one of them for another thing, on a day of the ward's own choosing and not for this, and one of them said no kindly to me in a yard in the rain and gave me the reason, and one of them is the woman I am standing in front of, and the fourth I have never been given a surname for. And the woman who gave me the four of them is standing in a kitchen in Mill Ward at this moment and she has refused to do this and she told me to come and ask you. And I am asking you, and I am asking you because the case is right, and I am asking you before I tell you anything else about it, because that is what I am supposed to do and I have got it wrong twice in six weeks."

Hester Lund looked at him for about four seconds.

"No," she said.

"Right."

"**No**," she said again, "and I am going to tell you why, and I have told this to two people in nineteen years and one of them was a man who wanted me to see a thing about a sea wall, and I am going to give you the whole of it, and you are going to stand there and take it, and then you are going to come back and ask me again, and if you do not come back then I will have wasted a good morning on a wet stair and I will not think less of you for that than you deserve."

"Go on."

"First, you have asked me the wrong question and you have asked me the wrong question in the exact words a Registry uses, which is *stand at the far end of a route*." She picked the oil can up. "Nobody stands at the far end of a route. That is what a route is, a road, and a road does not have a person at the end of it in the sense you mean, or there would be a man at the end of every road in this county and there is not. What there is, at the end of a route, is a **name on a page** and a person who has agreed to be that name, and the whole of what the name does is this, and I am going to give you the whole of it because you have not got it and everybody who asks me has not got it.

"It is not an advocate. It is not a representative. It holds no office and cannot be cited and cannot be suspended and has no seal and nothing in it but the fact that she was there. It is not a hearing and it is not an appearance and it is not a line in a document that says somebody speaks for a district. It is a person who will stand in a room and say, in the ordinary voice, *this is what the ward did, on this day, here is the document, and I have read it.* That's all. Four sentences and a piece of paper on a table in front of them.

"And the only power it has is that it is theirs and not the office's, so the office's answer has to be made to it and not to the district office, because the district office is the office. And the only cost it has is that a person's name goes on a page, and a name on a page is what a district return is made of, and every single thing that has gone wrong in this delta for four hundred years has gone wrong because a name went on a page and somebody's. So you are asking me to have my name on a page, and you have not once said that word to me, and I have been standing on my own boards for nineteen years and I know exactly what a name on a page is worth and I have watched two women lose a house and a boy and a trade over a name on a page."

"I am sorry."

"Do not be sorry, it is the most accurate anybody has been with me in a year." She put the can down again. "Now the conditions, and then you will go away, and you will come back, and I am not going to pretend to myself that you will, and I am not going to be disappointed if you do not, because a man who comes up a stair once has not asked me anything, he has told me a story."

She said them in about fifty seconds and she did not number them and she did not slow down.

"**One.** A person of that ward asks me, in writing, on that ward's own paper, naming the day. Not you. Not that building. Ever. If it comes out of a coat like yours I go home and I do not say why.

"**Two.** I am not a witness to their return and my name is not on it and I do not sign one single thing they perform. I read the sheet they give me. I do not read their book, because their book is theirs and I have no business in a woman's house.

"**Three.** I do not speak for them. If I am in that room and the ward and I differ, the ward is right and I am wrong, and I will say that out loud in front of the strangers, because the day somebody finds out that the person at the end of a route is the person who agrees with the route is the day this stops being worth anything.

"**Four.** I come on the day I have said and not otherwise, and nobody may ask me on the day why I have not come, and I will not answer it if they do.

"**Five.** I take nothing. Not a wage, not a meal, not a bed, not a coat against the rain, and if anybody in that ward offers me a thing I will not come again.

"**Six.** Nobody asks me who sent me and nobody says in a hall that the Registry's man found me. I have stood at the end of another ward's route once. Sixteen years ago. I am not going to say where and I am not going to say what happened and you will find out if it matters and I am not going to tell you now.

"**Seven.** If my name goes on a page in a return, the standing ends that day, and nobody asks me why, and I do not say that the word standing to either of us again."

She stopped, and wiped her hands on the rag, and then said:

"That is seven and your ward's book has five terms in it and a condition and I have read about them because somebody in a kitchen at the bottom of the river told me to read them, and the woman at Wexford Landing read them twice and wrote to me about them, and we have not spoken in two years and I am telling you that I have read her line about the wage in my own copy of her own hand, which she wrote on a gatepost at a landing ten days ago, and if you tell her I said that I will know you have been talking."

"Seven," said Owen Rusk. "And the sixth one I would not have guessed at, and you have not told me the sixth one."

"No," said Hester Lund. "And you are not entitled to it and I am not going to give it to you, and there is a reason, and the reason is that if I tell you how it ended then you will be careful with me, and I have been careful with myself for nineteen years and I would like to keep it that way, and a careful person is a person who has been got at, and I have been got at."

Owen Rusk stood on the boards of a landing at Coal Hill in the rain and took it, and took all of it, and did not say one word for about ten seconds, and then said:

"That is fair. Thank you."

"You have about an hour and a half of light," she said. "And you are not going to ask me again this afternoon, because it is Sunday and it is raining and you have said the wrong question once and you have come up a mile in the rain to hear seven things from a woman you have met for four minutes, and that is not a man who is going to come back at four o'clock today and get the maximum out of me." She put the can under her arm. "Come back at eight in the morning on a weekday. Come back with the actual question. And before you say that you do not know what the actual question is, because I am going to tell you what it is, since you asked me and I have answered, and it is the whole of what I said on the boards and it is fifteen words: **a standing in the room with a document on the table in front of it.** Not a hearing. Not an appearance. A standing, in a room, with a piece of paper on a table, and the ward asking me for it in their own writing on their own paper with the day named on it. Anything more than that and you have come to the wrong landing and there is a woman on the next one down who will give you a great deal more than I will and none of it will be worth the boat fare."

---

He got the midday boat down and walked the mile and sat on the wall at the bottom of Fetter Lane in the rain for about a quarter of an hour with a sheet of copying paper in his coat that he had not written anything on, and then he went up the hill.

The ward hall was shut and the hall does not open on a Sunday unless there is business, and there was no business, and at about half past four in the afternoon a lamp came on in the room at the back, and Owen Rusk stood in the doorway, and Ada Rusk was in there with a slate and Marguerite Bray with the delivery book and nobody else, and Ada said "come in and shut it" and he did.

"I have one job this evening and it is to write down what I have," he said, "and I am going to read it to you both, and I am going to read it with my hand on the page, and I am not going to be asked to summarise it, and I am going to be corrected if I get it wrong."

"Sit down," said Ada Rusk. "You are dripping on Mrs Bray's floor and I have got a slate and four days to get a schedule onto and a route with nobody at the end of it, and I have about four minutes before I have to be at the top of Fetter Lane."

He sat down and read it.

He read out the whole of the Sunday, in order, the way he read out the Council's circular in the hall on the Wednesday — Nell Arbour's four sentences and the addition she had made to them; the wrong question and the right one; the landing, the boards, the hut, the tide table in its frame, the oil can; the seven conditions word for word; the sixth condition he was not entitled to; the fifteen words on the boards; and the name. He got it right the first time, because he had written it down twice before he left the landing, on the back of his own hand in pencil, and read it out.

"**Hester Lund**," he said. "Coal Hill. Nineteen years on that table."

The delivery book had a pencil in it and Marguerite Bray had it ready, and she wrote the name in her own ruled hand, with the day and the hour under it, and read it back.

"And the standing," said Ada Rusk.

"A standing in the room with a document on the table in front of it. Not a hearing and not an appearance. And it comes from the ward, in writing, on the ward's paper, with the day named on it, and if her name goes on a page in a return then it ends that day."

"Then it is not ours yet," said Ada Rusk. "It is hers, conditionally, and it is conditional on us writing to her, and I am not writing to her on Monday, because I have got a schedule to get onto and I will not do two things at once in a rain season." She put the chalk down. "And I am not going to write to her on Monday and I am not going to write to her until the schedule is in, because the first thing we hand that woman is a piece of paper saying what we do, and not a letter saying please come."

"She will want to know what she is being asked to stand in front of," said Owen Rusk.

"She will want to know the day and the room and what is on the table," said Ada Rusk, "and she will get all three, and if she asks me what it is worth I am going to tell her the truth, which is that it is worth a wage of four months and a repair at four thousand one hundred and sixty and a thing at four in the morning, and that a woman from a landing standing in a room will not move any of the three, and that is the whole of what I have got to offer her and I am not going to dress it up, and if that is not enough then she is right not to take it and I will rule her refusal in the book with the day on it the same as I ruled Mr Tillet's."

Mrs Bray wrote that down too.

Owen Rusk walked down the hill at about six in the evening with his register in his inside pocket on the left and the lamp behind him on the boards, and the standpipe was running, and the granary said two weeks, and the sixteen were still sixteen, and the return came up on the Wednesday, and somewhere in Docketford there was a sheet of paper on a table by a door that a hundred and ninety people had walked past in eight days, and in a ward hall a woman of sixty-one was ruling the name of a woman she had met once into a book she had kept for thirty-six years.

He had been put in a room by people who owed him nothing, twice, in one day, and both times he had come away with less than he had come for, and the second time he had come away with more, and the only difference between the two visits was that on the first one he had wanted the maximum out of a woman he had met four minutes, and on the second one he had wanted a standing in a room, and he had said so in fifteen words, and a woman who keeps a tide table for nineteen years on a stone stair in the rain had said *that is the whole of it* and then not said anything else, and had made him come back at eight in the morning on a weekday, which was the first time in six weeks that anybody had told him when to turn up as though it were ordinary.
