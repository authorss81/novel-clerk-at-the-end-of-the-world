# Chapter 15 — What Ivy Needs

It took him all of Sunday night and most of Monday morning to work out how to answer Wenna Sarr, and the reason it took that long was that the first three answers he had in him were all complaints, and she had told him on Saturday evening that she was not going to let a month go by the way a fortnight went by, and Owen Rusk spent Sunday night and the first half of Monday in an empty ground-floor building being angry with a woman who sells nothing in particular four doors from Marguerite Bray's.

The complaint answers were these. That the ward's business was the bridge and the crossing was a matter of a public work and a named place and a status on a continuation roll, and that a child was on a second leaf of a transfer annex and not on a ward return, and that if he started arguing about a twelve-year-old in the middle of a case about a bridge the bridge would be lost and the child would be exactly where she was. And that there was no instrument in Docketford that could hold her, and that a man who says otherwise is selling something. And that everybody in that ward had known this for a month and had said nothing because saying it was expensive.

All three of those were true, and all three of them were the sound a person makes when they are managing a situation instead of being in one, and by Sunday afternoon he had worked out that the reason he could not get past them was that they were all addressed to her.

So on the Monday morning he went up the hill with nothing in his notebook except four lines he had written out at the table in his own front room at two in the morning, and he did not get to say any of them, because Wenna Sarr had her shutter up and her boards out and a basket on her hip and she said, "You have come to do arithmetic. I have done it. Sit down on the step and I will tell you my working and you may tell me where it is wrong."

She put the basket down and stood over him and told it out loud, in the flat unhurried way of a woman who has been thinking about a thing for fourteen days and has got it into the order she wants it in.

"A child cannot go in a household return. That is the rule and I am not arguing with the rule, because a household return is grown-ups deciding about themselves and a child is not a grown-up deciding about anything, and I have read that much." One finger. "Two people who are not related to her and are not living in the same house, who can say they have seen somebody look after her this week. I have got no two. I am her aunt, and I live with her, and I am the one person in this ward nobody will count."

Second finger. "Somebody doing something for her this week. Not me. Me does not count, or it would be a ward of two, and it is not a ward of two, it is me and a child who cannot read what they hand her."

Third finger. "Somewhere to go if somebody wants to complain. And not here. If I am the route then the route goes to me, and the whole thing is me again, and I am the person it is about."

Fourth finger, and she held it up longer than the others. "Somebody who is not me and not her family and who can be put out of it without asking this ward. That is the one they do not tell you about, copyist, and I found it in the front of a form that Mr Voss read to me at his counter, and it says the custodian may be replaced by the office at any time and the ward is not asked."

She put her hand down.

"Now," she said. "I have been four months on that and everybody in this ward has been very kind to me about the roof and the pump and about my brother, and about this they have said nothing at all, and I have worked out the rest of it on my own, because I am not a fool and I had nothing else to do with a wet fortnight. And I have got down to the bottom, and here is the bottom, and you may tell me if it is wrong."

"The only person in this ward who could stand in that box is Mrs Bray."

Wenna Sarr looked at him.

"Yes," she said.

"And she is sixty-one, and she is the steward, and she has a hand-kept roll, and she is the only adult in this ward who has ever written any of this down, and every clerk who has ever come up this hill in a month has wanted her ledger more than they have wanted any of us."

"Yes."

"And in four years, or nine, or whenever it is, that box is empty, and Ivy Sarr has been kept by an empty box, and a person is not a thing a Registry keeps. She has got a name and she will outlive whoever is standing in it, and the whole of what a Registry does is write down a name against a duty, and if the name is an old woman then the thing she has been protected by dies with the old woman and there is nothing in the drawer at all." Owen turned his gloved hand over on his knee. "That is the bottom. You have got it exactly right and it took you fourteen days and I have had it since the tenth of the month and I did not come and ask you, and that is on me and not on you."

Wenna Sarr stood in the rain on her own boards for a moment with her hand on the basket.

"Well," she said. "You have not come to tell me it is all right."

"No."

"That is more than anybody else has managed this month and it is a very low bar." She picked the basket up. "Now say the other thing, because I can see you standing there with four lines in your mouth and you have not said any of them, and I have been talking for ten minutes and you have not once interrupted me, which for you, from what Ada tells me, is a miracle with a cost attached."

And so Owen Rusk, on a step outside a stall in Lower Lock on the twenty-first day of the month, at about half past nine in the morning, with the river audible behind everything, said the only thing anybody in Docketford had said about Ivy Sarr in a month, and it was not a complaint.

"A protective return does not keep a child," he said. "That is the part everybody gets wrong, including me, and I have been wrong about it for a fortnight. It does not keep her, and it does not keep anybody, and the person it is for is not an object that a Registry holds while a household gets on with its life. What it does is this. It records who is looking after her this week, and it makes them do the looking after, and it puts two people in the file who have to say next week that they still have, and it gives her a road to complain along. And then it reserves everything. Her name when she is grown. Her hands. Her vote. Her own marriage if she wants one. Her own wages. Whoever she lives with, and whether that is anybody in this ward. Every one of those is put aside and named as hers, and no box can be filled in with her name in it, and the moment anybody tries to fill one in, the whole thing is void."

He stopped, and then he said the rest of it, and it was the first time in a month that he had spoken about this case without an accusation in it.

"It is reviewed every seven days, which means that if the person in the box stops doing it, somebody in this building has to notice inside a week and somebody has to come. And it expires, and it has to be asked for again, and there is no version of it where a person is put in a drawer at twelve and comes out at twenty-eight with a life somebody else has been keeping in the meantime." He put the glove off, because the hand had started again, and looked at the yellow under the edge of the cut. "It is not a way of owning a child. It is the only instrument in this city that is built so that it cannot own her, and it costs a Registry two witnesses, a custodian, a route and an act, every week, and there is no version of the sum of that which a ward can pay."

Wenna Sarr put the basket down again.

"Say the last part of that again," she said. "The part about a ward."

"A ward cannot pay it," said Owen. "Not a ward of thirteen households in a flood year. The custodian is a Registry function. The route is a Registry function. Two of the four things are things a Registry officer has to be paid to do, and this Office has not got anybody to do them, and the man who could appoint one is a man writing a list of sixteen wards. That is not a complaint, that is the arithmetic. The four parts are correct and three of them are yours and one of them is a hole in somebody else's budget, and the hole is the whole of why your girl is not on anything."

"It is not a hole in a budget." She said it quietly. "It is a hole in a decision. Nobody decided to leave her off. She was left off the way the seed stall was left off."

"Yes," said Owen. "That is exactly what it is, and that is why it will not get fixed by anybody being angry at a budget, and why it will get fixed, if it does, by somebody deciding to pay for a woman to hold a box that a twelve-year-old is allowed to leave when she is old enough to say she wants to leave it."

---

He went up at eleven to find out what the Office actually wanted, because he had a form in his notebook with four blanks on it and he had been carrying it for a fortnight, and the blanks were: custodian, two unrelated witnesses, a current care act, an appeal route, and it turned out there were five, which he had not counted, and one of the five was the seven-day review and he already had that.

The seal-stair clerk was on duty and let him up without asking, and he stood in front of a drawer on the third landing with a small plate on it and did not go near it, because the plate said, in the Office's own words: *Instruments for persons unable to answer. Sealed. Not to be copied, quoted, or removed from this landing.*

"I want to see one," said Owen.

"You want to see one and you may not see one. That is what sealed means, and I am not being obstructive, I am the only person on this stair and there is nobody in this building who could let you, including the Deputy Registrar, and if he stood here and asked me I would tell him the same thing, and you may put that in your book with the date on it."

"I am not asking to copy it," he said. "I am asking what it requires. There ought to be a requirements sheet. A sealed instrument has a requirements sheet in front of it. Every instrument in this building has one, because the people who fill instruments in are not the people who are examined on them, and the requirements sheet is how one office tells another what it wants."

The clerk went down the stair and came back with a sheet of foolscap, folded, and gave it to him at the landing and stood four feet away while he read it, which is the correct procedure and which she did without comment.

Owen read it with a good right hand and a bad one, and it took him a long time because the second half of it is nearly identical to the first half of it with the nouns changed, and by the fourth time through the top of the page he put his hand flat on it and stopped, and looked at the landing wall, and waited for something to arrive that he had been able to read for six weeks and which had never once arrived.

Nothing came.

Not because there was no pressure in it. There was plenty. There was a person standing on a landing holding a requirements sheet about a person who could not answer, in a building, in the rain season, with a defect in a file in a drawer two floors down that he had promised himself he would not open. There were four blanks on a form in his notebook and a woman in a stall who had counted them herself. And the page in his hand was a real page, about a real instrument, that he had asked for by the correct route from the correct person.

And a closed file, and a held file, and a sealed instrument, all say the same thing, and the thing they say is that the difference between a page that answers and a page that does not is not how well you read it.

Owen Rusk stood on the third landing of the seal stair on the twenty-first day of the month and discovered for the second time in a fortnight that he had no instrument for a silence, and that the discovery was no use whatever, and that he was frightened of it in a way that had no shape in a notebook.

Because the first time, in the hall, the silence had been an answer to a question he already had. He had wanted to know what the ward's copy of the transfer said, and it said nothing, and the nothing had a date on it, the fifth of the month, and a stamp.

This silence did not have a date. It had no date at all, and no stamp, and no reviewer, and it was not anybody's decision and it was not anybody's failure. It was a drawer on a landing, with a plate on it, and the plate was correct, and there was nobody in the entire Registry of Continuant Works who could open the drawer for him, and therefore there was nobody he could be angry at, and therefore there was no act of his own that could change what was in it. He could go and learn the words. He could not go and learn the words before somebody chose to teach him, and nobody was going to choose, because nobody was harmed by his not knowing.

He got the words, in the end, from the sheet in his hand and from Wenna Sarr's four fingers and from what Nell Arbour had said to him in a kitchen in the fourth week of the month that a child who cannot answer is a child who is being spoken for, in practice, by whoever is standing nearest the counter.

He wrote it in the notebook in the flat voice and then he stopped and did not write anything else, and he understood that this was the third time in three days that he had put something down and written nothing beside it, and that he was not going to be able to explain to anybody, in a form anybody would accept, why a man who had spent a fortnight taking notes on a bridge could not make a list of a child's requirements.

---

He found her at six in the evening, in the lane behind the hall, sitting on the granary step with a slate on her knees and a piece of chalk, doing what she had done every day since the flood and had done every day since Owen had known her, which is a sum.

She was twelve. She had a coat that had been her father's and had been let out twice at the shoulders by somebody who was not a tailor. She looked up when he stopped and she said, "You're the copyist," and then, before he had answered, "Mern's is the only one you can do and mine's the one where the answer's nine and I've got eleven, and I'm not wrong, there's two of the seed trays in with the pump ones."

"All right," said Owen. "Show me."

She showed him. She was right. There were two seed trays in the pump tray because Nessa Kray had put them there to keep them off the wet floor, and nobody had ever counted them.

He stood in the lane behind a granary in Lower Lock for about four minutes doing arithmetic with a twelve-year-old on a step, and it was the best four minutes he had had in a month, and he understood afterwards that it had been the best four minutes because he had not once been thinking about a status.

"Right," he said. "Listen. I am going to ask you one question and I want you to think about it properly and not to give me the first thing."

"All right."

"Somebody in this ward is doing something for you this week. Not your aunt. Something somebody else does. Feeding you, mending your things, walking you somewhere, teaching you something, keeping you out of the water. Something that happened in the last seven days." He crouched down so that he was not taller than she was, which he had not planned to do and which he was aware of about two seconds too late. "And I want you to think about it as though I were going to write down the name of the person, because I am, and because it is not the ward and it is not a document. It is a person doing a thing that other people have seen."

The chalk went on working while she thought.

"Mern takes my washing in with hers," she said. "Since the flood. She doesn't count it and I don't count it, it's just in with hers and she hangs it."

"Anything else."

"Kray lets me have the seed trays that go over," Ivy said. "For the ones that don't sell. And the mill factor, he lets me stand at the counter and he reads the notices to me when my aunt can't go, which is every Tuesday, and he doesn't count that, and he doesn't have to."

"Two. Good. Keep going."

"Mrs Rusk lets me sit in the shed on a Sunday," Ivy said. "She makes me do the weights off the granary boards and write them down, and she says if I ever want to know what a thing weighs I am to go and stand next to it and not next to the man who tells me what it weighs." She looked at the slate. "I don't think she knows she says that every time."

"Three."

"Bram Tillet taught me to swim in the shallow bit behind the granary in the summer before the flood and he does it after his shift now, on Thursdays, and my aunt doesn't know and it doesn't matter."

"Four."

She stopped with the chalk.

"Mr Rennie," she said. "Cobb. He walks me back at night when it's dark. That's every night, and he doesn't know why, and I don't tell him, and he walks me back and he stands at the pump and he goes on. And he's not on anything, is he." She said it as a flat fact, not as a question, in the voice of a child who has spent a fortnight carrying notices for her aunt and has clearly been listening to the adults around her for years. "He's not on any of it. There's not a thing with his name on it and everybody knows where he sleeps."

"No," said Owen. "There isn't."

"Then he's the best one to ask, isn't he," said Ivy Sarr. "Because nobody can say he wants anything."

Owen wrote the four names down. He wrote them in the notebook and he wrote them slowly and he did not write, next to any of them, the word that was in his head, which was *custodian*, because a word written next to a name in a Registry clerk's private book is a claim about that person's life, and he had spent a month learning that the only thing worse than writing nothing is writing the right word in the wrong book.

Ada Rusk. Bram Tillet. Ilse Mern. Cobb Rennie.

And then he shut the book and sat on the step next to a twelve-year-old with a slate and said, "I am not going to tell you any of this is going to work. Three of those four are people who have said no to something this month in a room with witnesses in it, and one of them is a man no record in this city has placed anywhere for two years, and if anybody tells you that any of that is a kindness, it is not. It is a hole in somebody else's budget and the hole is the reason you are not on anything. I have said the only thing I am able to say and it is that the four of them did something this week that two other people in this ward have seen, and that is three of the five parts, and the other two are not yours and not mine."

"Are you going to write it down?"

"I have written the four names down. I am not going to write anything else down tonight, and if anybody asks you tomorrow whether a clerk has been asking questions about you, you can tell them exactly what he said, which is that you had eleven trays and there were two in the wrong stack, and that you were right and nobody had counted them in two years."

She laughed. It was a short, entirely ordinary twelve-year-old's laugh, and he had not heard one in his own front room in eleven years.

"Mr Voss will be cross," she said. "He says nine."

"Nine's the district's number. Eleven's yours. Both of them are right and it does not matter, and I am not going to be the man who makes you agree with a Registry."

---

He got down to the copying floor at half past seven to file four names against a form and found that he had a different problem, which was that the first review of District Return 487-LL fell due the next day, and the form for it had a box he had not looked at properly.

The box was on the second page, under the heading of the person who may be affected, and it said: *Name a person who may object to this return, or state the class of such persons. A return reviewed with no such person named is reviewed with the door shut.*

And Owen sat at the end of a bench on the second desk with a cut hand and a good deal of time and understood that he could not fill it in.

It was not that he did not know the names. It was that the box asked for a person, and the four people who were in the ward and outside the return had made a position for themselves in public in front of witnesses on the seventh day of the month, and had been right to, and were now, by that fact, the only people in Lower Lock who could not be asked to consent to a document in which they did not appear.

A person who has said no cannot consent to a return that does not contain them, because the saying no is the whole of what they have said and it was said once, in public, and it was sealed.

So the box could be filled with a class, and the only honest class in the world was *any adult of the district who is not in this return*, and a class that broad was a door that had been left open rather than shut, and he had been in a hall four nights ago and told twenty-eight people that the answer was a piece of paper and that paper was a thing you could carry upriver.

Also in Mill Ward, on the same afternoon, a clerk behind the Mill Ward annex counter had begun telling people in the queue that a return could be withdrawn, and had said the word without once stopping to say by whom, and by six o'clock there were three people on Fetter Lane who had heard it and one of them was a woman of seventy-one who had been told at that same counter, five days before, that there was no such thing at all.
