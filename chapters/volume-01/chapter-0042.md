# Chapter 42 — The Cost of a Good Week

The hall was open at eight on the Sunday morning because the hall is open on a Sunday when there is damage, and there were about thirty people in it, and nobody was talking about the hearing.

They were talking about ten inches of water in a granary and about a tray of seed that had gone to slime in the dark and about whether the wall at the back of the pump house was standing, which it was, and about a man on Bell Row whose floor had come up in three places and who had been carrying oakum since Saturday and did not want a fuss and was given one anyway.

Owen Rusk stood at the back with his register in his coat and did not take it out, and it was the fourth day in a row he had been in a room in this ward without needing to write anything down, and he noticed that he noticed it, and he did not make a note of that either.

The count is done in a hall by talking, and it was done badly on the Sunday and well on the Monday, and Owen understood the reason on the Sunday and did not say it.

It is done badly when everybody wants their own thing first, and it is done well when somebody makes a column. Ada Rusk made the column on the Sunday afternoon, in chalk, on the slate she brings to the double count, and the column had four headings and no headings at all beyond those four: *water*, *food*, *work*, *paper*. It is the most Registry thing that has ever been done in this hall and nobody in the room said so and she did not say so either, and she wrote the four headings in her capitals and told the room that anything that did not go under one of the four was not a loss, it was a grievance, and grievances are for Mondays, and she has four in a book and knows the name of every person who brought one.

That is what the ward's page is for. It is not for the Office. It has been on a table in Docketford since Wednesday and readable at any counter and every person in it has signed the disclaimer three times about a bridge, and none of that is what it was for.

---

**The food.**

That is the number, and the number is not interesting, and the number is the entire subject of the second day.

The granary holds nine weeks in a good year and four in a bad one, and the committee's figure is a clock and not a token, which is a distinction Marguerite Bray has been making in a pump book since the fifteenth of the month and which nobody has ever thought to thank her for, because a clock does not thank you and neither does a token and the difference between them only becomes visible at the moment the clock stops.

The write-up is the Thursday afternoon's work and takes two hours and ten minutes, and the last one came out at **just over two weeks**, twice, on the forty-ninth and the fifty-second of the month. Bray wrote *and then it will be a fortnight* under the Day 45 figure in her own hand, and she wrote it in a pump book on a page nobody has ever asked to see.

On the Monday morning the granary committee did an unscheduled count, which is not the same act and is a worse count and is the only count available on a Monday, and it took forty minutes and it was done in the wet with the bins open and a lamp on the third one, and nobody counted twice because there is nobody left to count twice.

It came out at **a fortnight**.

Marguerite Bray ruled it in the pump book in her own hand, with the day and the hour, and then she read out the six words she had written under the figure four days before, because a woman of sixty-one who has kept a book for thirty-six years does not leave a sentence she has already written.

"*And then it will be a fortnight,*" she said, "and it is a fortnight."

And then she took the pencil and wrote the next line, and it is in the book on the page where the figure lives, and it is the line that Owen Rusk read back in his own head on the Tuesday morning and has not stopped reading.

*Water stood in this granary from two till six on the fifty-fourth day of the month. Nobody asked me to write that and I am writing it because I am the only person who will.*

---

**The paper.**

The ward's page is a page because somebody has to be the person who answers for it, and for six weeks that person has been Ada Rusk, and Ada Rusk has now said in a hall, out loud, that she is not going to be able to do it on the fifty-ninth.

That is the whole of what Saturday did to the schedule and it is not a small thing.

The roster is read in public in this hall at ten past six in the evening, and the reading is an act of the return, and the reading is a duty, and a duty is somebody's own act and a duty can be performed again on a Tuesday by anybody who signed it. On the fifty-ninth, at ten of the clock in the morning, in the long room at the Glass Stair, in front of forty people, a woman of sixty-one is going to read four weeks of hours out of a book in the ordinary voice, and at ten past six that same evening somebody is going to stand at the end of the long table in this hall and read the week out, and the two of them are three hundred miles of river apart and about ten hours of daylight.

"There is not a day in this week when the hearing and the reading are the same day," said Ada Rusk, "because the clerk chose a Thursday out of a list of rooms, and the fifty-ninth is a Thursday, and a Thursday is the day the whole of this ward's life is on."

"Then the reading does not happen," said somebody.

"It happens at ten past six in the evening in this hall with the people who have come back in it," said Ada Rusk, "and the number in it will be smaller than any other number in that book, and Mrs Bray will rule the number and the day, and that is what the record will say, and it will be true, and it will be the first Thursday in seven weeks that this ward has performed a smaller thing in front of fewer people."

"Which is worse than not doing it," said Holm Ferris, from the wall.

"It is *narrower* than not doing it," said Ada Rusk, "and I have been in this hall since the seventh day of the month and I would like everybody in it to notice which of those two words I used, and why."

---

Then she put the other thing on the table, and she put it down herself, and she did not ask the room whether it wanted to discuss it.

"**We can cancel Thursday.**"

Nobody made a noise.

"I am not going to pretend that is a stupid idea, because it is not a stupid idea, and if I do not say it out loud then somebody will say it out loud on Wednesday night in a worse mood than this one, and it will be said as *the ward is tired and the ward has been asked for a great deal and let us be sensible*, and that is the sentence that ends a ward, and it ends it in public and in a good cause." She put her hand flat on the table. "So here it is, in a hall, with Mrs Bray ruling it. The district may cancel the performance of Thursday the fifty-ninth. The return does not fall over. The return is renewed for review every seven days and it has never been binding, and a district that does not perform on a Thursday is a district whose interval falls due and is not met, and the interval is a review and not a closure, and I have read the small print and so has Mr Rusk and so has every person in this room who has been to a counter in Docketford this month."

"And that is all it means," said Owen Rusk.

"That is all it means procedurally," said Ada Rusk, "and it is not all it means in here, and I am going to say the second part and then I am never going to say it again.

"Everything this ward has done since the seventh day of the month has been done in public on a calendar, in front of somebody who was not us, because Mrs Bray wrote *and the wage* in a book and a woman from a landing wrote on a gatepost that being legible is not the same as being paid, and the Council of this country wrote down that it requires watching. And every one of those acts was an act. Not a claim. An act. And an act is a thing that was done.

"If we cancel the fifty-ninth, then the first thing this ward does when it gets hard is the one thing that is visible, and there is no version of the seventh week in which a district that has performed every Thursday for six weeks does not perform in the week it goes to a hearing. And I am not saying that because the hearing is coming. I am saying it because **the roster is a fact and the fact is the whole of what we have**, and a fact that is dropped the first week it is inconvenient is not a fact, it is a habit, and we do not have a habit, we have a bin count and a bar on a Wednesday night and a marker at seven forty and those are all facts and they are all that we own."

"Then do it," said Dory Anstruther from the door, nineteen years old, with a chest that has gone the colour of candle wax and a broken collarbone and a coil of line in his hand, "and stop talking about it."

"I am going to do it," said Ada Rusk. "Mrs Bray, put it in the book that the district has resolved to perform on the fifty-ninth, and that the resolution is not a promise about the hearing, and that the hearing is at ten in the morning and the marker is at seven forty and the two are the same day, and that the district has never in seven weeks had both on one day and is going to find out what that costs on Thursday and rule it in."

---

**The work.**

The crew is nine and the wage is four months in arrears and the wage is written on a page of the ward's own book in two hands as the condition of agreeing to be read in public, and it is not funded anywhere in this city that anybody has found.

A wet week does not pay anybody. That sentence was said out loud in a hall on the Monday afternoon by Marguerite Bray, in the middle of a list, and it is the truest arithmetic anybody did that day, and it got no argument at all.

There is not a fund in this city for people who did not get wet. There is a fund idea, which is a different thing, and the difference between a fund and a fund idea is about four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and a clerk's handwriting, and this ward has learned more about that difference in seven weeks than in four years.

Nine crew, four months, and the ninth week of the season, and the repair is four thousand one hundred and sixty, and the notice nailed to the weir path still says that any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it, and the weir path was under water from four in the morning on Saturday until about two in the afternoon on Sunday and nobody read the notice on Sunday and nobody is going to read it this week.

---

And then, in the last half hour of the Monday, with the lamp turned down and about six people left in the hall, Nessa Kray said the thing.

She did not stand up and she did not clear her throat and she had her apron still on, and she had been in the hall for the whole of both days, which she had not done since the twenty-third of the month.

"You are all being very careful with me," she said, "and I want it stopped."

Nobody said anything.

"You read out on Saturday that the stall is gone, and Mr Rusk had a smaller word in his mouth and I stopped him, and that was correct and I have thought about it since and it is the only time in a month that anybody in this room has said a true thing about me without making it soft first." She turned round. "So here is the true thing. The ward's paper does not claim my pitch. It never has. The transfer moved the crossing and the pump and the granary, and it left me where I was, and a pitch with no instrument behind it is not a ward's loss, it is my loss, and there is no column for it and there is not going to be one, and I have been on this pitch forty-one years and I have not once in forty-one years needed a piece of paper to stand on it."

"Which is fine," said Ada Rusk. "And I am not going to pretend to you that it is fine."

"Then what are you going to do."

"The truth, and it is not much." Ada Rusk did not look away. "I am going to say the thing that is the whole of what this ward has got and I am going to say it in a hall and you are going to hate it. **Being on the ward's own page is not being owed anything.** You are in this ward's book. You are in the schedule. Your name is on the front of the delivery book and on a roster that a stranger from a landing read out loud on the thirty-eighth and that any person in this county may read at the weir path on any day without notice and take a copy away. And the ward's paper does not claim your pitch, and it cannot, and it will not, and if this ward's paper is stretched over your pitch it stops being a document about hours and starts being a claim about a place, and a claim about a place is what the transfer was, and the transfer is what took the crossing off us."

"So I am legible," said Nessa Kray.

"You are the most legible person in this ward and you are owed nothing," said Ada Rusk, "and that is the worst sentence I have said in this hall this month, and I have said it out loud because you asked me to and because it is true, and I would rather say it than be kind to you."

The room sat with it, and the lamp ticked, and about four minutes went by, and nobody filled them.

Then Nessa Kray laughed — one syllable, without any humour in it at all — and said: "Aye. I have been on that pitch since I was thirty. I have buried a husband on the ground behind it. And I have never once been owed anything by anybody, and I did not think I was going to be, and you have all spent a month being proud of a piece of paper about your own hours and I have spent a month watching you be proud of it."

"Yes," said Ada Rusk.

"Then that is a fair summing up and I will not have anybody try to improve it in a hall." She stood up. "And I am going to tell you the other half, because Mrs Bray will rule whatever I say and she is not going to rule half of it. I am going to be back on that pitch. Not in a week. In a month, if there is a pitch. There is gravel under it, because it is a market pitch and it was made by the market, and the market is two streets up and the market has a factor and a factor reads notices." She stopped at the door. "And I am not going to ask this ward for a thing, and if any of you try to put my name in a document about it I will read it out in a hall and it will be the last document any of you ever files."

Nobody put her name in a document.

---

Owen Rusk walked down to the bottom of Fetter Lane at about ten that night and stood in the lane for a while, and the water had gone down about a foot and a half and the hall behind him was shut and the lamp in the room at the back was on and Mrs Bray was in there with the book.

He did the arithmetic, standing up, in about two minutes, and it came out the way arithmetic does when a man has spent seven weeks learning that the only number anybody can put on a page is the one somebody decided.

**This ward is not poorer because of the transfer.** That is nearly a lie, and he let it sit for a second and then took it apart. The ward is not poorer because of the transfer in the sense that a family's rice is shorter this month than it was in August. The ward is poorer because ten inches of the river came into a granary on the fifty-fourth day of the month, and because a pitch of forty-one years went into a foot and a half of water, and because a bar went into a bend and four crew are off the water and the crew are not paid.

And the two are different, and the difference between them is not a matter of degree. One of them is weather and one of them is a decision, and a decision has a person in it, and a person can be asked about it, and a bar in a bend cannot.

So: the transfer took the crossing. The transfer took the pump's grant. The transfer took the granary. And everything the transfer did was done in a form, by an office, in a column, with initials and a date, and it can be read, and it has been read, and two things have happened to it in seven weeks — a schedule of the office's refusals is on a public table with a queue in front of it, and a district return has been renewed five times for review and certified or not certified or whatever the next two days produce, and the sixteen named failing returns are on a public docket with a date against them.

And the water did what water does, and there is no instrument for it, and no Registry officer is going to be asked about it, and no form is going to be maintained against it, and nobody in this city is going to write *November, the river* in a column of reasons, and the figure in a pump book in a ward hall is going to go from a fortnight to less than a fortnight and nobody is going to ask for the reasons.

"Visibility is not remedy," said Owen Rusk, out loud, to an empty lane, on the fifty-sixth day of the month.

And then, because it was raining again and because he had a long walk on Monday and a Tuesday in front of him and because a return comes up for review on the Friday and has to be narrow enough to survive a room, he went up the hill.

The date had become a problem. That was the shape of it and it took him about four hundred yards to get hold of. The fifty-ninth had been a gift on Wednesday, the only day of the week on which this ward can be shown to have done anything, chosen by accident by a woman with a list of rooms, and on Saturday night the ward had gone out in the dark with a bucket chain, and on Monday it had made a resolution, and on Thursday it was going to do two impossible things on one day and write both of them down.

And the renewal was on the Friday, and it was renewed for review and not binding, and it had been renewed five times in seven weeks, and the sixth renewal was in four days, and the ward could not afford to keep a duty it could not afford, and the Office had said so in the correct form and would say it again on the Friday in the correct form, and the correct form was going to be true.

The return had to be narrow enough to be certified. That was the arithmetic underneath everything, and he had been avoiding it for two days because Saturday had taken the floor out from under the building, and because on Monday afternoon a woman of seventy-one had told a hall that she was owed nothing and had been right, and because there was no version of Thursday in which a ward with a foot and a half of river coming through it and a stall gone and a bar in a bend could hold a claim that a Deputy Registrar's office could put its hand on and answer for in a room.
