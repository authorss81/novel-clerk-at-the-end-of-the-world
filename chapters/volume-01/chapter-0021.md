# Chapter 21 — The Same Batch

The renewal was on the tray in front of him at ten to seven on the Monday morning of the twenty-eighth day of the month, and it had been put there overnight by nobody, because the tray has no schedule and a thing on a tray is a thing that has been put there.

It was a sheet of copying paper with a heading on it and five lines under the heading and nothing else, and the five lines were the ones a clerk writes at the start of a week so that the rest of the week can be argued with.

*Marker — Thursday and Friday, seven forty, two at the top path, three at the path end.*
*Channel clearance — Wednesday night, after the mill silts down.*
*Pump prime — Thursday, four in the morning, and again if it fails.*
*Granary double count — Wednesday night, bins against the book, written up Thursday afternoon.*
*Standpipe rota — carried.*

Then, in the space underneath, in Ada Rusk's capitals, two words: **third week.**

Owen read it twice and understood both of the things in it and neither of them was the reason it was on his tray.

The marker at seven forty was set on the Thursday and again on the Friday and had been set every week since the fifteenth, and would be set next Thursday too, and did not need a clerk. The channel clearance was on the Wednesday and had been on the Wednesday twice and the mill's outfall had begun to drop a bar of silt across the channel in the middle of the week, which meant the night work could not be moved to a day when it was convenient. The pump prime was on the Thursday and had been on the Thursday, and had failed on the twenty-second, and had been done again at four the following morning, and there are four households and one man of fifty-eight on the standpipe rota at the top of Fetter Lane who would be carrying water in the dark for as long as the grant stayed suspended, which is to say for as long as anybody in the building could be relied upon.

It was the third week of the four months the crew's wage was in arrears, and the third week of a granary that had four weeks in it on the fourteenth of the month and had three weeks in it now, and the third week of an abutment repair at four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds that no district in the county had accepted, on a notice at the weir path in letters big enough for a man to read at forty feet telling any district that can do the job better to come and do the job.

The renewal was on it because the return came back every seven days from the day it was entered, and it was entered on the fifteenth, and the twenty-eighth was the last day of a week the interval had already spent, and the interval did not care about the crossing or the pump or the granary, and the crossing and the pump and the granary were what the interval was measuring.

He put it flat on the board and squared it and did the thing that four weeks of this had taught him to do before anything else, which was to look at the sheet and ask what it was not.

It was not the ward's log. It was a clerk's summary of a ward's log, and a review on the twenty-fifth had put on the file in a reviewing officer's own hand that the record be the ward's own and not an office's summary of it. So this sheet was not evidence of anything. It was a piece of paper Owen Rusk had made in order to have something to put in front of somebody, and it was going to be in a file on Friday, and if somebody read it in three years they would read it as a document of the ward's week, and it would be a document of a clerk's week, and the difference between those two things is the whole of what he had learned in a month.

He wrote at the top of it, under the heading, in the small print the Office uses for a thing somebody must not be misled by: *Summary of the ward's own record, made by the assisting clerk. The ward's log is the record.*

And then he did the other thing, which was to go and find out what a redacted batch is called when you are not the person in it.

---

The Front Desk index room is on the ground floor, under a slope of glass, and it is open every day the building is open, and it is open to anybody, and the reason it is open to anybody is that an index which can be locked is not an index. Owen had learned that on a Sunday from a duty clerk of twenty-six who had told him so before he had thought to ask, and who was not on the counter this morning because it was a Monday and Mondays are the busiest day the Front Desk has.

The counter clerk on Monday morning was a different pair of hands, and he asked what a redacted batch was, and Owen said he wanted to know what the form of the thing was called and not what was in it, and the clerk told him that if he wanted to know what was in it he was in the wrong room, and that if he wanted to know what the *form* of the thing was called then the answer was a book about four feet high on the second shelf along and anybody might take it down.

He took it down.

It was called the *Redaction Register*, and it is not a register of things that have been redacted, it is a register of the *sittings* at which things were redacted, and the distinction is the first thing a clerk learns about it and the reason the book is four feet high instead of a drawer. A redaction is a decision. A decision has a date, an officer, a subject, and a reason. But in Docketford the Office had discovered about ninety years ago that a redaction of one person at a time is a piece of paper and a signature and a great deal of work, and that the great deal of work is what stops redactions from happening, and so redactions are done in batches: a sitting, an officer, a list.

A batch is a set of entries struck in one sitting by one officer.

Owen stood in front of the second shelf for a while with his hand on the spine of it and understood that he had spent a month learning the grammar of this Office from its failures and had not once gone and looked at the machinery of its successes, and that the machinery of a success in a building like this is a man with a straight edge and a bad afternoon.

The columns were: batch number; the quarter; the date of the sitting; the officer's initials; the number of entries struck; the ground; and then, at the right-hand edge, in a narrower column with a different head, a single line that read *schedule lodged*.

Schedule lodged: Y or N.

He did not have to guess whether a schedule was a document that cost something to produce. He had spent a morning on a Sunday writing down the fact that a list of numbers is not a record of a person, and that a list of numbers is the cheapest thing the Office knows how to make, and he stood there and let the two facts sit next to each other on purpose.

He went back up to the ground-floor counter and asked for the quarter.

Which quarter, said the clerk, is not a question anybody can answer for you, and Owen said, C.Y. 471, fourth quarter, and the clerk said that is not how it is kept, it is kept by flood quarters and ordinary quarters, and Owen said then the fourth ordinary quarter of Concord Year 471, and the clerk went and found it in about ninety seconds, which is a thing about this Office that he had noticed for nine years and had never once been able to put anywhere: the answer to a narrow question is always fast.

---

Marta Rusk had no entry in the front of the book. She had one in the body of it, and the body of it is the fourth quarter, fourth quarter, fourth quarter, and it is a column of numbers and nothing else, because the book does not record who; the book records how many, and the list is somewhere else.

**Redaction batch 471/Q4/19. Sitting of the 12th day of the fourth quarter. Officer: H.L.P. Entries struck: forty-one. Ground: revision of residence status, flood quarter arrears. Schedule lodged: Y.**

Owen read the line four times and then had to put his finger on it, because the ground was a **ground** and a ground is the standard reason a redaction is given and the standard reasons are short on purpose, and he had been in this building nine years and had never once seen a ground that named a person's medicine.

*Revision of residence status, flood quarter arrears.*

Not *fraud*. Not *no supported district*. Not *claim unsupported*. **Arrears.**

A quarter's arrears. A backlog. The condition of an office in a flood year, when a great many people at once changed address, or lost address, or were carried somewhere, and came to a counter and asked to be placed at a place, and the counter had a great many forms and not enough hands and therefore somebody, one day, in one sitting, went down the queue and struck a number of entries without opening any of them.

Forty-one entries. Struck in one sitting. Struck by an officer whose initials were on the line.

And he knew the initials, because he had known the man behind them for four weeks, and had watched him put a flat hand on a table in a ward hall and make a room stop talking, and had heard him say *every district I stabilize is a district that does not drown somewhere else*, and had refused a lateral posting at better pay on the strength of him.

H.L.P.

---

Here is the part that took the rest of the morning, and it is the part that could not be hurried, and Owen understood while it was happening that it was the first thing in a month that had happened to him rather than been done by him.

A redacted batch is not a document a clerk may read. That is the ordinary rule and it is a good one and he had never wanted it changed. What a clerk may do, and may do without asking anybody's permission, is ask for the **schedule** of a redacted batch, because a schedule is a list of numbers and a list of numbers is not a record of a person and costs the office nothing to make.

But the schedule is not the only thing.

"There is one other thing," the clerk said, when he had got the schedule out for him and initialled it and was closing the volume. "You have not asked for it because you do not know it exists, so I am going to tell you, and I am telling you because in nine years I have watched about four men find this out by accident and every one of them did it in a room with two other people in it."

He put his hand flat on the counter, which Owen had learned to read as a woman who keeps a hand roll does and as a seal-stair clerk with a card does, and which is, he understood that afternoon, simply what people do who are about to say a thing they have said before.

"A batch may be produced for inspection by a person named in one of its entries. Not to an officer. Not to a supervisor. To the person. In the room, alone, once, and a clerk stands at the door and does not read it and afterwards writes that it was produced and to whom and at what hour."

Owen did not say anything for a few seconds.

"Do not," said the clerk, "do the face you are doing. There is nothing in it for you. There is no news in there. Nobody has been found. It is a list of numbers with your own family's name in the first column and eleven days in the third and that is the whole of the paper, and I am telling you that now, in this room, at a counter, before you go up, because it is cheaper for both of us if you hear it from me than if you hear it off a sheet in a room you have to sit in by yourself."

"Right," said Owen.

"And you will have to give your name to have it produced. That goes in the production note and the production note is kept. You are not asking for a thing for the ward. You are not asking as an assisting clerk. You are asking as the person named in entry nine of batch 471/Q4/19, and the moment you say that sentence out loud to a person in this building it does not stop being true, and there is no form on which either of us could take it back."

"I understand," said Owen Rusk.

"You do not," said the clerk, comfortably, and gave him the slip.

---

He asked for it in the request register, in his own name, in the correct form, with the number and the volume and the reason, and he put the reason down as *person named in entry 9 of redaction batch 471/Q4/19*, and the counter clerk wrote it out without a change of expression and initialled it and gave him the counterfoil, which is the copy that goes to the office, and told him that his own copy would be posted to him in a week by the ordinary post because anybody may have a copy of anything he has asked for.

A request is not a filing. A filing is a thing somebody can answer and this is a thing somebody can only note, and he wrote that on the counterfoil as well, in his own hand, before he put it in his pocket, because in a year he was going to want to have written it down somewhere.

That was at twenty past ten. It was produced at half past two, because production of a batch under the named-entry rule is done on the half-hour and not before, and he was made to wait through the whole of the morning with three things in his notebook and a hundred and sixty sheets of copying in front of him, of which he got through about ninety.

The three things were: the batch number, 471/Q4/19. The officer's initials, H.L.P. And the date the batch was struck, the twelfth day of the fourth quarter of Concord Year 471.

The floor of the registry building at half past two on the twenty-eighth day of the month is the coldest room Owen works in, and the room they took him to was not the index room, it was a small one off the copying hall with one window, and the window looked out at the yard and at the wall of the next building, and the light in it at half past two in the afternoon in the fourth week of the rain season was the colour of weak tea.

A clerk stood at the door. It was a young man and he had a sheet and a pencil and he did not read anything and he did not leave and he wrote the hour down when Owen had gone out.

---

The first column was a number. The second column was a number. The third was a number of days.

Forty-one rows. Entry number, residence-status entry number, days pending. The rows were in the order the officer had gone down the queue, and not in the order of the numbers, and the first row was therefore not entry one, and Owen understood what that was in about a second and did not enjoy understanding it.

Owen read the first row.

*Entry 9, residence status 471/2216, 11 days.*

And beside it, in the hand of the officer who had struck the batch — a small, level, very fast hand, the hand of a man who has never in his life had to slow down — there was one word in ink, and the word was:

*Agreed.*

He had to sit down, and there was one chair, and he sat in it.

He had known the shape of this for a month. He had known it the way a man knows the shape of a room he has walked through in the dark: the floor, the door, the distance. What he had not had was the number. The number was eleven, it sat in a column called *days pending*, and it was not the fever.

He said that out loud in an empty room, on purpose, to hear whether it was true, and it came out as what it was: *Eleven days. Not the fever. The fever had a medicine shortage in it and the medicine shortage had a flood year behind it and the flood year is not in this document. Eleven days is when she stopped being at her address for the purposes of priority medicine, and priority medicine is what she was not given, and the two are the same fact and they are not the same number.*

A residence status is not a life. Owen sat in a chair and understood what the entry actually was, which he had understood before and had not been able to hold: a residence status is the entry that places a person at an address for the purposes of priority medicine, priority ration, and burial. Three things. That is the whole of the vocabulary of a woman of thirty-four in a flood year, and the Office has a column for each of the three and a form for the three of them together, and the form is in fact a **priority instrument**, and a priority instrument is the document that decides whether a person is counted as being somewhere.

She had been at Fetter Lane. That was the status. And for eleven days in the fourth quarter of Concord Year 471 the entry that said so had been sitting in a batch on a shelf in a building two miles upriver of Fetter Lane with a one-word note in ink against it, and while it sat there she was a person who was not counted as being anywhere, and a person who is not counted as being anywhere is not a person at an address, and a person who is not a person at an address does not receive priority medicine, and does not receive priority ration, and — and this was the line that took the breath out of him and he had to stop for it — **and can be buried by somebody else.**

The burial was disputed for eight months.

Owen Rusk sat in a chair in a room with one window in a Registry building and did not cry, and he had expected to, and he had a right to, and he did not, and what happened instead was that he sat very still for about four minutes and then asked himself, with real attention, the question of what he was going to do about the fact that there was a person at the other end of this row.

And there was not.

Row one was a number, and a residence-status number, and eleven days, and the word *Agreed*, and there was no name in it anywhere at all, because a redacted batch is a list of numbers and the numbers do not have names in them. The name was on the *entry*, and the entry was in a volume, and the entry had a presenter column, and the presenter column said, because it had been presented by a boy of sixteen in the fourth quarter of Concord Year 471 on behalf of a woman who could not get upriver in the wet:

*Presented by: O. Rusk, of Fetter Lane. Relationship: son. Claimant informed.*

*Claimant informed.* Somebody had written down, in the correct column, on the correct form, that the person whose status it was had been told. He had not been told. He had presented the claim and gone away up the hill and she had been dead or dying or lying in a room on Fetter Lane, and some clerk in some office had ticked a box that said the claimant was informed, because the claimant was present when the form was presented, and a boy standing at a counter holding a form is present, and the word *informed* is in the vocabulary of this Office and it means that the paper has been spoken aloud at least once to somebody in the room.

He is the only living person named in that entry. Not Ada; Ada was eight and had not left the ward. Not his father, who had gone upriver in the year before and was not a party to anything. Him.

And that is why a batch may be produced to a person named in one of its entries. It is the only door in this building that opens on a record of a person without a person's consent, and it opens because the person is the only living holder of a fact, and the Office would rather show one person their own row than show a clerk a column.

He read the other forty rows, standing up, at the pace of a man reading a menu, because he was not going to read them fast and he was not going to read them the way he reads the copying floor. Forty names that were numbers. Forty-one rows including his mother's. The same interval on every one of them, without exception, in the days-pending column, in the same hand, in the same sitting.

He did not copy it.

He stood there and made himself want to and then did not do it, and the reason, which he wrote down that night in the notebook he had already told a woman in a hall was a register, was the reason from the Sunday in the index room with the county intake register: a page of a redacted batch in a sewn notebook with a bad clasp makes the notebook a document that contains a redacted batch, and from that day it cannot be lent and cannot be read on a table in a hall and cannot be handed to a counter clerk, and it is a shorter step from a notebook to a search than people who have never done it imagine.

So he wrote down three things and nothing else, and squared his chair under the table, and went out past the clerk at the door at ten to three, and the clerk wrote the hour down, and did not look at him, and said nothing at all, which was the single most decent thing that happened to Owen Rusk that month.

---

He did not tell the ward. He want to be exact about that, because it was a decision and not an accident, and because a decision that gets made by accident is not a decision.

He did not tell Pell. He did not tell the ward because Ada Rusk would have understood it in about four seconds and would have said something true about it, and he had spent four weeks discovering that when Ada says something true about it she is usually right and it is usually expensive, and he did not tell the ward because on the Friday of that week the ward had to renew a return for the second time in a fortnight, and a renewal is a Friday in this office whatever the ward would rather it was, and a room does not need to know that the man who keeps their paper tray in the middle of it has found out what the Office did to his mother in a flood-year quarter, and there was no version of telling them in which the telling was for them.

He told nobody in the Office. He told nobody anywhere. He went up to the copying floor at four and compared ninety-one sheets and got through them, and at half past five he put the tray card in the drawer it lives in, and the tray had seven things in it, none of which had a schedule, and one of which was a folded letter addressed to a name he had copied four hundred times a quarter for nine years and which came, immediately, without any difficulty at all, into his head, and he wrote it out on the routing slip, and got it right, first time, in under a second.

He noticed that. He had been braced for the opposite and he got the name in a second and there was nothing at all wrong with it, and he stood at the end of the board for a moment with the wet pen in his hand and felt, for about the length of a breath, something he would have called relief if anybody had asked him what it was.

Then he thought about the third column and about forty-one rows and about the same interval on every one of them, and understood that this was not a thing that had happened to him. That this was a Monday in a month in which the man who had done it was a floor above him writing a Friday, and that a batch of forty-one residence statuses had been struck in one sitting by a careful man in a room with a door that shut properly, and that the carefulness was not the point and had never been the point.

It was four hours. That was all it was. Four hours out of a month in which he had spent every waking hour thinking about a bridge, and when the four hours were over he was still a man in it with a tray and a card with four forbidden lines on it and a ward owing him a Friday.

He walked down the hill in the rain at ten past six and stopped at the pump house to see the book, because the book is a thing he has read every day since the fifteenth and reading it is now the closest thing to thinking he has got, and the book said *prime, Thursday, four, Mern and B. Tillet, and again at seven because it did not hold*, and there were four names on the standpipe rota and Cobb Rennie was on it, and above the rota, in the same hand, three weeks old and not rubbed out:

*Bin count this week: 3 weeks and a bit. Nobody has asked me this and I am writing it down because if nobody asks me I will forget it in a month and then it will be two weeks.*

Marguerite Bray had written that. In a pump book. On a pump.

He stood in a pump house in the rain and read a sentence written by a woman of sixty-one who had ruled a delivery book for thirty-six years and had apparently, on some evening in the last month, come down to the pump house and written down the state of the granary in a book about a pump, for nobody, because nobody had asked.

And then he went home upriver to a room where there was nothing on the table but his mother's good plate and a lamp, and he did not put the batch number in the notebook, because the notebook does not go on that table. He did not put it anywhere at all that night except the four lines at the bottom of a page of his own register, which he was by then writing in a flat unhurried hand about an hour a day, without a heading, as a man writes when he has finally understood that the thing he has been frightened of is not the notebook.

It is that the notebook is a copy of his attention and that a copy of his attention is a thing that can be searched.

*Batch 471/Q4/19. Struck the 12th, fourth quarter, C.Y. 471. Forty-one entries, eleven days each, one sitting, one officer, ground: revision of residence status, flood quarter arrears. Three things and no names. I am the only living person named in entry nine, and entry nine is the first row because that is the order he went down the queue in, and I have not decided yet whether that is a comfort and I do not think it is. I have been one since I was sixteen and I did not know it was a fact with a number on it.*

*Eleven days is not the fever. Eleven days is when she was not at her address and a person who is not at an address does not get the medicine and cannot be buried by her own people. She was buried in March and the dispute ran eight months and the correct number for that is eight months and it is not eleven days and I am not going to be the sort of man who swaps them.*

*Forty-one people. Not one of them has been asked anything. I am the only one who has ever been asked, and I did not know I was being asked, and I said my own name out loud at a counter on Monday to a woman who had to write it down.*
