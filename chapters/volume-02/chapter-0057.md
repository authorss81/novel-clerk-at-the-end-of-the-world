# Chapter 57 — The Objection

He wrote it on the Saturday night and it was four sheets and it was correct.

That is the first thing and it has to be said first, because everything else in this chapter is about a man who did everything right.

It was in the right form. The form is a single sheet printed one side and it is called an Objection to a Reconciliation, and it is item fourteen of a schedule in a book in the district office, and he had read it in the count room on the Wednesday and he had copied it out longhand and read it back to himself twice and there were four lines on it that wanted filling in and he filled in three of them correctly and argued with the fourth for about an hour and a half and then filled it in.

**One. The objector.** Name, and the premises at which the objector may be asked.

**Two. The settlement.** Name, and the year of the last reconciliation.

**Three. The ground of the objection.** *A reconciliation is a statement of fact. State the fact you dispute.*

**Four. The figure you say should stand instead.**

And the fourth line is the one the form was built for, and Owen Rusk had it, and he knew he had it, and he wrote it at about two in the morning on the Sunday morning with the lamp out and the rain going on the roof of a room in Fetter Lane, and what he wrote was:

*That the settlement be reconciled of premises as four (4), and not nine (9), and that the six premises unoccupied and unstruck since the Concord Year 480 be struck before the reconciliation, and that the district office be required to consider the making of a dwelling of record of two rooms at the head of a working mill, there being nine houses upon a road on that bank which is not a street of record and has never had a number.*

He read it back. It is correct. Every clause of it is true, and the third clause is the one he had been carrying in his coat since Saturday afternoon and it is the clause that was going to work, and the reason it was going to work is that it asks a district office to do a thing a district office can do.

And then he sat in the dark with it and worked out that he had no standing to file it.

The ground of an objection to a reconciliation may be taken by a person of the district named in it. He was a person of Lower Lock. He lived two miles from the Fourth River Desk in which the district office of Docketford sat and he was the officer of the record of its district returns and he was a stranger in the building, and neither of those put him in Nether Sill. He could not file it. He could not sign it. He could not witness it. He could carry it up the river in a boat and put it on a table and a counting clerk would have read the first line and given it back.

---

A thing of this kind requires a name, and the names available on a bend with four buildings on it are two, and he asked both of them on the Sunday morning and was refused by both, and the refusals were about eleven minutes apart and neither of them was rude.

Hanna Roe's was at about a quarter past eight, at the head of the cut, with the water coming down and the wheel going round, and she said:

"No."

"I have written it so that there is no name of yours in it anywhere. It is one sheet of figures and a request that a district office consider a thing. There is not one word of you in the four pages."

"I know there is not," said Hanna Roe. "I have read it. You read it to me twice yesterday in a boat and you read it right, because you read everything right, it is what you are for. And in the fourth line it says *the district office be required to consider the making of a dwelling of record*, and it does not say *by the district office*, and it does not say *and its officers shall attend*, and it does not say a name, and it is a request, and the request has a second column on the other side of it, and the second column is empty, and it is going to want a name in it by Wednesday.

"And when it wants a name it is going to want mine," said Hanna Roe, "because the sluice is the only work on that sheet with a person in it, and I am the person in it, and there is no name in this county that anybody would put in a column instead of mine, and if there were one I would not want it there. So the form is going to come to me, and it will come to me asking, and I am telling you now so that you have heard me say it and not guessed it on Wednesday when it is in front of me and I am in front of eleven people from a field."

"The second column wants the person who performs the act," said Owen Rusk. "A sluice does not have a person. A sluice is a cut with a gate in it."

"And who opens the gate," said Hanna Roe.

"You do."

"Then the second column wants me," said Hanna Roe, "and that is the end of it, and it is not an argument and it is not a complaint and it is not even a refusal, it is a thing I have said for twenty-six years, and I would have said it in the year before last and I will say it in the year after next, and you may write on your four pages that I said it."

---

Neve Searle's was at about half past eight, in the front room of the mill, with the wheel going through the floor, and it took nine minutes and it was about a person of twenty-six with a stripe of grey at the front of her hair who had been eleven days without a night off in the ninth week of a rain season in the previous year and who had not asked anybody for anything since.

She read all four sheets standing up. She is a person who reads slowly and completely and who does not perform comprehension, and she read the third line and the fourth line and then went back to the second one and read it again, which is the right thing to do and which Owen Rusk noticed and could not afterwards stop noticing.

"Third line," she said. "*That the district office be required to consider the making of a dwelling of record of two rooms at the head of a working mill, there being nine houses upon a road on that bank which is not a street of record and has never had a number.*"

"That is the clause that is going to work."

"It is a good clause," said Neve Searle. "I want to say that first, because I can see it is and I am not going to be the kind of person who cannot read a good clause. It is the only sentence anybody from Docketford has said about this bend in four years and it has got a number of houses in it and it has got a mill in it and it has got a road in it.

"I am not signing it," she said.

"Because of the fourth column."

"Because of my father," said Neve Searle.

She put the sheets down on the table and squared them, which is a thing people do when they are about to say the true thing and it takes four seconds to do.

"He is sixty-eight. He had a stroke on the night the water came up to the sill and he was in the mill because I was in the mill and he came to see whether I was all right, which I was, and he is at the top of that stair and he has been in a chair by the window since the ninth week of the rain season, and he can see the water from that window, which is the reason he is alive and is the reason he is not asleep, and he can be asked four things a day and he can answer about two of them, and one of the two is his own name, which he has in the twelfth week of a Concord year, and the other is whether the sluice is open, which he can say, and he cannot say anything else and he will not be asked anything else, and I have not asked anybody for anything since the ninth week of the rain season, including this.

"The fourth column of your form says: *the care act now performed for them, in one sentence, and the name of the person who performs it. This line may not be left blank. If there is no such person, enter none.*"

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"There is a person," said Neve Searle. "There has been a person since the ninth week of the rain season and she is twenty-six and she has not had a night off. And if I put my name in that column then the care act is Neve Searle's care act, and the column is what the count is made of, and in a fortnight the number in the reason will be four and the settlement will be struck, and then the office that struck it will come and ask me how a thing in my own name was allowed to fail, while my father is in a chair by a window watching a cut that is not in any record.

"Enter none," said Neve Searle. "I have thought about it for four days and it is the only answer that is true, and it is not a gap, and it is a sentence about a person in a chair by a window, and it will be on a public file, and whoever reads it in a hundred years will know there was a man there and nobody was keeping anything for him. I would like that on the file more than I would like the water."

Owen Rusk stood in the front room of a mill with the wheel going through the floor and had the four sheets of the best document he had ever written in his hand and no one to give them to.

"I am going to have to say something," he said, "and I want to say it before we go up there, and I am saying it now so that it is not a speech in a room in front of a warden. I have not got standing. I am a person of Lower Lock and I have been right about a column for two days and I have no standing at Nether Sill and there is nobody here who will put a name to a piece of paper, and I am not asking you to, and I am not going to pretend to you that the reason I am up here is a form."

"The reason you are up here is a boy in a chair," said Neve Searle.

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"Then be on the slip at ten," said Neve Searle. "And take the sheets, and do not put my name on them, and if it all goes wrong today you come back here on the Tuesday and tell me what they said in the room, because nobody on this bend is going to know what was said in the room, and my father will ask me, and I will have to tell him something."

---

They came up at about ten and the reconciliation was at eleven in the front room of the mill because the Warden would not do a reconciliation in a house with one door if it could be done anywhere else, and the room with a table and a chair and a place for the stone was the mill office, and the office is six feet by nine and has a window and a stove and the wheel.

There were nine people in it. The man of sixty, who brought the case and opened it and took the stone out and set it on the table with the flat face up, and put it down the way you put down a thing you have carried a long way. The woman of about thirty with the slate. Prue Hallam, who was told to wait on the slip and waited on the slip and was the only person at Nether Sill in the room who was not accounted for. Hanna Roe, who came in wet and stood by the stove. Neve Searle. Ivor Searle, fifty-one, who is her brother and who works the mill and who had said almost nothing for two days. A woman of about thirty-five who keeps the tide table at Nether Sill and who is not the woman at Coal Hill and is not related to her and has not spoken to her since the flood year. An old man. And a girl of about nineteen who had come down from a house at the head of the cut and who was the fourth person at Nether Sill who cannot be asked and who nobody in the room could say the name of.

And Owen Rusk, who was there because the recitals say that a reconciliation is conducted in the presence of the officer of the record of the district returns out of which it is taken, and who said so out loud in the first minute, and who was therefore not a party and was not an objector and could not file anything.

"Do you know that you cannot stop this," said Warden Kade, at about a quarter past eleven, without any edge at all, and not unkindly.

"I know," said Owen Rusk.

"Then say what you have come to say, and I will stop you if it is not permitted, and I will tell you when I do."

---

The recitals permit a person present to state a fact, once, and it goes into the note, and the note is on the file, and the note does not enter the arithmetic.

Owen Rusk said it once. He said it in about four minutes, standing at the side of a table with a stone on it, in a room six feet by nine, to nine people and a girl of about thirty with a slate. He said the nine on the sheet and the four on the ground, and he said the three empty premises unoccupied since the Concord Year 480 and asked about four times in nine years, and he said the mill, and he said the room and the tap and the drain and the door to the outside and the number on the door, and he said the road with nine houses on it that has never had a street of record, and he said the margin note in the counting clerk's hand in the Concord Year 486 with the six words in it, *and I would rather it failed on nothing*, and he said that a man in a sub-table in the Concord Year 486 had written down the whole of this case four years ago and had not been asked and had not been thanked and was not on a board at Cutter's Fields, and he said it as facts and not as an argument, because he had worked out on the Saturday night that an argument is the one thing that cannot go in the note.

When he had finished, Warden Kade said:

"That is a complete statement and I am going to put all of it in the note, and I am going to do it in your words and not in mine, and it will take about six minutes, and then I am going to do the thing that decides it, and I am going to tell you before I do it that it does not matter what is in the note."

She wrote for six minutes. It is a long note. It is on the file. Owen Rusk has never seen it and did not ask to see it and there is no form on which a person may ask.

Then she turned the sheet round and showed the first column, and there were two lines in it, and one of them was a figure, and the form says in a line above it in the printing shop's hand:

**REASON (a figure).**

And the figure was **4**.

And below the reason, in a box the same size, there was another box with a line above it that said **NOTE**, and the note was six minutes long, and the note sat underneath a figure in a box the same size, and the two boxes were the same size, and nobody in the room said anything about that, and Owen Rusk looked at it for about four seconds and then had to look away at the window.

"That is the whole of it," said Warden Kade. "Four premises produced of nine recorded. The settlement is not reconciled of premises. The settlement is reconciled of nothing this morning and the figure goes to the office on Wednesday and the correction is dated the twelfth and it is attested by me and the stone has been on it since a quarter past eleven this morning, and the form is dated, and the water will be back in the district's main by the fifteenth and the share of the fund will go with it and the standing of the households in the addresses will go with it, and I have written your four pages in the note and the note is on the file and the note is not the reason.

"Now I am going to say the four things that answer you, and I am going to say them in the form because that is the form, and none of them is mine, and all four of them are the law of this place and I could not get a better answer out of them if I had been asked to argue about them for a month, and I have been asked to argue about them for a month, four times, in three districts.

"**One.** A reconciliation is a statement of fact about premises and the fact is four. I am not permitted to record a fact about a thing that has not been counted, and a dwelling of record does not exist at the top of that mill because nobody has ever made it one, and I cannot make one, and neither can the office I answer to, and the only body that can make one is a district office, and a district office is not before me.

"**Two.** A room with a tap and a drain and a door to the outside is a dwelling, and there are nine houses on a bank that is not a street of record, and I have known that for about four years, because I have been to this settlement twice and I have seen the road, and I have not written it anywhere, and the reason I have not written it anywhere is that a Warden who writes down a thing she has seen but cannot act on is a Warden who is doing a district office's work badly and at a distance.

"**Three.** Fourteen days starts now, at the hour the stone went down, and it runs to the eleventh of the month, and on the twelfth the correction is dated, and I cannot extend it, and there is no officer of this service or of any other who can extend it, and I have tried to extend it once, in the Concord Year 479, in a place I am not going to name, and I was refused in eleven days and the place is not there.

"**Four.** And I am going to say the fourth one last and slowly, because it is the only one of the four that is not the law. **You have asked the right question to the wrong room for two days.** You have asked it in the correct form to a form. A dwelling of record is made by a district office, in a book, on an application, and the book is a schedule of premises of record and it is kept in the district office of Docketford, and the form is item nine of a schedule in the same book, and there is a copy of that book on a public stand in the count room at the Glass Stair, and the copy is four hundred years out of date in about nine places, and one of the nine places is a road on that bank. I have known that for four years. I have never mentioned it. **I have never mentioned it because I have never had to sit in a room for two days with a man who could open a book.**"

Nobody said anything for about three seconds and the wheel went round through the floor.

"Enter none," said Neve Searle.

Warden Kade wrote **none** in the fourth column, in her own hand, and put the pen down, and did not look at anybody.

---

They were on the slip at about half past two and the boat was going to be let go in ten minutes and Owen Rusk was standing at the slip steps and had said nothing for ten minutes, and Warden Kade came out and stood at the top of them and let him get about four words out and then stopped him.

"Say it to the water," she said. "Not to me. I have to be able to say in a room in eleven days that a person said a thing to me and meant it, and I will not have it said to me on a slip where the man of sixty can hear it."

He turned round.

"That is the first true thing anybody has said in front of my count this season," he said, "and it is not a fact about the count. It is a fact about a man in a boat on Saturday. I have been arguing with a form for two days when the thing that was needed on Saturday was a woman in a mill telling me her name, and she told me her name — she told me a great deal more than her name — and I did not stop once to put it down, because I was writing an objection, and I have spent eight years being the person who is good at forms, and there is not one of the four things you said that a form could have said, and three of them I could have found on Saturday if I had opened a book two floors below me."

Warden Kade looked at the water for about four seconds.

"Nether Sill is struck on the twelfth," she said. "The rest of the eleven go in the order they are on the board. Four of the four in this district are yours, and the fourth of those is four streets from where you are standing, and I am not going to pretend that I do not know that, and I am also not going to pretend that it changes the order, because if I change the order for you then I have changed the order for eleven places and the eleventh of them is a woman of eighty-one who is not on your side of this river and has never heard of you, and she would have got the same treatment as the four of yours and she would not have had a man in a boat."

"The counting clerk's note in the Concord Year 486," said Owen Rusk. "You have read it."

"It is on the file and I read every note on every file I reconcile, and I have read that one about four times," said Warden Kade. "A man in a sub-table wrote down that a settlement would fail on arithmetic and not on fact, and he would rather it failed on nothing, and he did not move the six, and the district did not move the six either, and the six are still on the sheet this morning, and I put the figure in a box and the figure is four, and the arithmetic is the arithmetic, and I have carried a counting clerk's sentence about a settlement I have never been inside for four years and used it twice this morning and both times it did not help and once it was the reason I spoke to you at all."

She got the last of it out, and it was the last of it because the man of sixty had a hand on the rope.

"Write to me about the schedule when you know," she said, "and not to the Council, and I will do nothing with it and I will not pretend to. And I am going to say one more thing, because you have been honest with me for three days in a way I am not used to and I would rather pay you back now than later, when it costs me something.

"**Do not write to me yet.**"

"I had not intended to."

"You had intended it by about four o'clock yesterday afternoon and you have not written it because you have not got anything to write, and if you write to me on Wednesday you will write to me with a figure, because a figure is what you have got, and I have had eleven letters with figures in them this season already and every one of them came from a person who had read a column and thought they had read a river." She looked at him. "Go and read the schedule. It is nailed to a board behind the tanneries. It is a printed sheet and it is nine feet long and there are eleven settlements on it and against each of the eleven there are two numbers, and one of the two numbers is the year of the last reconciliation and one of them is a figure of premises, and I have had that board up eleven days and I have not read one of those figures, because I do not reconcile a settlement by its premises, I reconcile it by the people in it, and I have a quota of eleven and eleven days and I have not got time to read a column about doors.

"**There are thirty-one settlements of record in this district.** Twenty of them answered last year. Eleven did not. And the eleven are on a board in a field and I have never once asked myself why the twenty that answered are the twenty that answered, and neither have you, and you have been here for four days and you have read four of my columns and one of my files and about four hundred sheets of correspondence that cannot be routed.

"So go and read the eleven, and read them as a man and not as an officer, and when you have read them, and not before, you may write to me, and I will read whatever it is you have written and I will do nothing with it and I will not pretend to, and that is what I have."

The boat went off the slip at about ten past three and Owen Rusk sat in the bow with a sheet of copying paper on his knee on which he had written seven lines on Saturday and had not added an eighth, and he did not add one on the water either, and Prue Hallam took them round the bend and did not speak for an hour, which he was grateful for in a way he could not have explained to a person in a field behind the tanneries.

He got back to Cutter's Fields at about half past six and the man of sixty was on the trestle table with the lamp lit and the case was shut and the board was up in the last of the light with its eleven settlements and its two columns, and the woman of about thirty was on a stool at the tent door with her slate and four figures on it, and she said:

"You went up at first light and you came down at six and you have not asked me a single question about the arithmetic, and I want to tell you that the man from the Glass Stair who came out last year asked me nineteen questions about the arithmetic in twenty minutes and left knowing more about Nether Sill than anybody in this county, and he is the reason I do not talk to the Glass Stair."

"And what should I have asked," said Owen Rusk.

"Whether the eleven had a shape," said the woman with the slate. "Whether the eleven on that board are the eleven that have never answered, or whether they are the eleven that answer worst, and whether the twenty that answered are the twenty that are small, and whether the eleven are all **upstream**, and whether there is one figure on that board that appears eleven times out of eleven, and whether that figure is a draw."

She put the pencil behind her ear.

"You will find there is," she said. "I have not looked. I have been doing this eleven years and I have never once read that column, and I have told the Warden about it three times and she has told me to do my own work, and she is right that it is not my work, and it is not hers either, and the only person whose work it is has a tray of correspondence upstairs and came out to a field on a Monday because there was a board on it."

Owen Rusk did not go and read the board. He noticed, at about ten to seven in the evening on the Sunday of the eightieth day of the month, standing at the west gate of a field behind the tanneries, that he had not gone and read the board, and that it was the fourth time in nine days he had failed to do the thing a woman had told him to do, and that he had a tray of two hundred and forty sheets that was going to take him until half past eight, and that the season opened in eight days, and that Nether Sill was struck on the twelfth.

Then he went and did the tray, and he was on it at about a quarter to seven, and he was fifty minutes late, and he noticed the lateness on the Thursday and not on the Sunday.
