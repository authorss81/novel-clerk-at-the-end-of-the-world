# Chapter 48 — What the Office Owes

The sixteen names were read out in the long room at the Glass Stair on the Thursday morning of the sixty-sixth day of the month, in the ordinary voice, by a clerk of the docket, because a clerk of the docket reads what is on a sheet and a sheet with sixteen names on it is a sheet.

*Stability Order 114 of the twenty-fourth day of this month, made under the solvency authority of the Deputy Registrar of Docketford, upon sixteen district returns of the quarter, suspending them together. The sixteenth in the schedule is the return of the district of Lower Lock, which is the sixth upon the schedule as it was struck.*

Sixteen. Still sixteen, on the sixty-sixth day of the month, in a public appeal, with a ward's witnesses standing behind a ward's own schedule, and the count had not moved since the morning of the twelfth day of the month when the schedule was struck and no document can move a line on it.

The room was the same room, because there is one long room at the Glass Stair, and the difference was in what was said in it, and it had about thirty people in it, and about nine of them had come from Lower Lock over four hours of river in weather, and the ward's own schedule of performed acts was on the table in a bundle with a covering page and a disclaimer printed three times.

Halden Pike was on his feet with his hat under his arm and a folder in front of him, and he had not come with a lawyer, because a Deputy Registrar of Docketford does not have a lawyer, he has an office.

---

The thing that happened in that room, in the first twenty minutes, was procedural, and it was the thing Owen Rusk had spent seven weeks learning to see coming, and he saw it come and could not stop it and wrote it in his register that night under the date with nothing above it.

It is this. A stability order is an instrument of the district office, and an instrument of a district office is examined a second time by an officer having no district before it is acted on. Item fourteen of the Schedule of Instruments in Force, issued by the Provisional Council in the Concord Year 44, in force in all district offices, answering to no local office, superseded nothing, superseded by nothing, **amended never**.

There are nineteen names on the roll of countersignature. Eight of them are officers of other districts. Nineteen names, and a roll that is a district-exclusion roll, and appointments that continue, and a line in each officer's own hand by which a person may withdraw and may not be withdrawn by anybody else.

And on the sixty-sixth day of the month, in a public appeal, a Deputy Registrar's order of the twenty-fourth could not be argued by anybody at all, in any room, unless somebody on that roll looked at it. There is no other mechanism. There has not been any other mechanism for four hundred and forty-three years, and the reasons for it are sound, and the reasons for it are in the recitals and the recitals are that flood-quarter returns were made in numbers and haste, commonly by a person with an interest in the work to be supported, and commonly witnessed by the same district, and the office could not distinguish on the face of an instrument a duty performed from a status asserted.

So: the same rule that has ruined Lower Lock for seven weeks is the rule that put sixteen names into a room on a Thursday morning, and the ward's own witnesses were standing behind the ward's own schedule, and the rule was about four hundred and forty-three years old and had never been amended, and everybody in that building, including the man at the table, knew it, and nobody had said it out loud until Owen Rusk said it, and he said it in the fourth minute, standing up, as a co-petitioner and an assisting clerk, and he says in the record that he described himself in those words because describing yourself is free.

"The return of Lower Lock is the sixth of sixteen on a schedule struck on the morning of the twelfth day of this month, and I have been in this building for seven weeks telling anybody who would listen that a district return needs a second officer from outside the district, and I have been doing it as a complaint, and I would like to say in a public room that the same rule is the reason those sixteen names are in this room today, and that it is four hundred and forty-three years old and its reasons are right, and that this district has been ruined by it and has also been saved by it in the same breath, and that I do not think anybody in this building can honestly be asked to make one of those two facts louder than the other."

Nobody in the room argued with it, and the clerk of the docket wrote it down, and it went into the transcript, and Halden Pike looked at Owen Rusk for about a second and a half across a table and gave him nothing at all.

---

**Wenna Sarr argued the six.**

She did it because she is the person it is about, and she said so in the first sentence and she would not be moved off it, and nobody in the room could move her, because she is not an officer of anything, she is not an advocate, she is not a witness to the return, and she is one of the four adults of this district who voted against the transfer on the seventh day of the month and who are not parties to it and who are named in the ward's own book as having been wrong, in two hands, for ever.

She had never spoken in a room in Docketford in her life. She had been in a ward hall every day for a month and had said four sentences in it and had stopped one of them in a Registry clerk in a single sentence in front of twenty-two people, and Ada Rusk had made sure everybody knew about that afterwards.

She stood up at about twenty past eleven and said that a protective return for a child required five things, and that the district had three of them, and that the two it did not have were a place where the thing is kept and a person who keeps it, and that the person who keeps it is a Registry function and that this city has never paid anybody to be one.

And she said the number, because it was on a page in a ward hall in a steward's ruled hand with the day and the hour under it and she had read it and she had a right to.

"Five hours a week," she said. "That is what a woman of forty-four in my ward worked out on the Tuesday, out loud, in a hall, when she was asked, and nobody could pay it, and she is not angry with anybody, and she is not going to be asked again. And it is not five hours on a Thursday. It is five hours a week in a house, where anybody at any hour can come to the door and ask where the papers are, and I have to be able to say, and it does not stop when the ward stops, because there is no day the ward stops.

"And the second thing is the power, and the power is the half of it that nobody has said out loud this morning. Whoever keeps it, **this office can put somebody else in their place without asking the ward.** Not without telling. Without asking. And then the hours of the person who was there do not stop, because the hours do not stop; only the person changes."

The room was very quiet.

"I am not here to be sorry for myself," said Wenna Sarr. "I am here because there is a girl of twelve in my house's care who is on a standpipe rota at four in the morning in the dark because the ward's paper is a roster and a roster is not a protective return, and I have done the arithmetic on her myself, and I have been doing it since the third week of the month, and I have got exactly the same five things and two of them are missing, and I have known what they were since the third week of the month and I have not been able to say so in a room in this building until a woman of sixty-one in my ward asked me to."

---

**Cobb Rennie named his own pension.**

He is fifty-eight. He has been in two rooms on the same landing of a house in this district for nineteen years and off the Registry roll for two of them, and he is not among the thirteen households on the last accepted roll of this ward and he is not among the thirteen erased entries, and nothing in this matter has ever reached him, and he said so himself in about four sentences, standing up, in a voice that carried perfectly in a quiet room.

"I am not on your list of sixteen," he said. "I have checked. Ada read the sixteen out in our hall and I am not one of them and neither is my street, and I want that said out loud in this room because it is the only reason I can stand up.

"I cannot draw a pension. I have not been able to draw a pension for two years. There is no record that places me at my address, and a record that places a person at an address is what a pension wants, and the record that would place me there was never made, and it was not made by anybody in this room on purpose, and that is the honest part and I am not going to pretend to anybody that it is the rest of it.

"Everything that has happened in this room this morning is because a ward wrote down what it does. I have been on a standpipe rota every Thursday for four months and every one of my hours is on a schedule of performed acts that anybody in this county may read at a weir path on any day without notice, and I have got four months of it in a public file, and I have read it, and it is a good document, and it is about hours.

"I have nineteen years in a pension book somewhere. Not in this ward. In a book. And there is no column in this city for nineteen years at an address that a man can point at."

He sat down. And then, from the wall, in the voice of a woman who sells nothing in particular, Wenna Sarr said four words that went into the transcript: "That is worth less than money."

And Cobb Rennie said: "It is. And it is more than nothing, and the two of those are both true, and I did not get up to be given something."

---

**Nell Arbour was in the room in a capacity of her own.**

She was at the back, on her own account, and she had not come with the ward and she did not sit with the ward. She had come because a notice of an appeal on a stability order is a public notice, and she is a claimant advocate, and a public notice of a hearing on an order that suspends sixteen districts is the sort of thing a person in her work comes and sits at the back of.

She did not speak for Lower Lock. She said so, twice, in about four words each time, before anybody asked her and once after.

What she said was about the other fifteen.

"This is the argument nobody in this room is standing in a position to make," she said, "and I am going to make it and it is not a thing anybody has asked me to make and I am not going to pretend it is.

"Every person at this table is talking about the sixteenth name. Every argument in this room from this morning is about whether a district of two hundred and forty people on marginal land is a district. And the order suspends sixteen, and the other fifteen are the reason the order exists, and none of the fifteen has anybody in this room.

"Mr Pike signed this on the twenty-fourth because the district cannot fund sixteen of them. That is the ground and it is a real ground and it is probably true and it is not a reason to do it to the sixteenth one. The order is not wrong because it is unkind to Lower Lock. **The order is a rule that picks a place, and the place is picked by a column, and the column is struck at eight in the morning and no document moves a line on it, and there is no instrument in this city that asks a district whether it can afford to exist before it is put in a column.** That is a different objection from the one being argued, and it goes to sixteen places instead of one, and there is nobody in this room who is owed by the other fifteen and that is why it will not be made."

She sat down.

And then she looked at Owen Rusk, and he understood that he was going to be spoken to, and there was nowhere in a room with thirty people in it for him to be anywhere else.

"Mr Rusk. It is a right case."

"Say the rest of it."

"The case is right, and that is why I am doing this, and I want that understood in the room and not in a corridor, because a woman who is doing a thing because it is right is a different animal from a woman who is doing a thing for you, and the difference matters in about a year.

"I am not a witness to this ward's return. I am not an advocate for Lower Lock. I am not yours. I have said those four sentences to you three times in a kitchen and I am saying them in a room, which is different, and the difference is that this one is on a page.

"And you have not asked me for anything since the Wednesday before last, and you came to the back of this room at about a quarter to nine and you stood at the end of my row and you did not say one word, and I have been waiting all morning to see whether you were going to manage it and you have managed it, and I am not going to say anything about that either, and I would like everybody in this room to carry on not saying things about it."

---

The order did not go, and Owen Rusk wants that on the record in his own words, because the shape of the thing is not what people will expect.

The Deputy Registrar answered the appeal in about ten minutes, and the answer was correct, and the answer was a paragraph, and it granted nothing. A stability order is made under a solvency authority and the grounds are the grounds. Sixteen districts of this county came into the office in the quarter with no return of their own performing and no sum against their works, and the district cannot fund what it cannot pay for twice, and a district that cannot perform a duty has no duty, and the order suspends rather than closes precisely so that a district which begins to perform may be restored, and it is not a finding upon the safety, condition or use of any work, and the appeal is not an appeal against the grounds.

And then he did the thing that Owen Rusk had not expected and has not stopped thinking about.

He asked for the file to be made up, and he asked for it to go to the Council's counter with a copy of the transcript, and he asked, on the record, in the room, in a voice with nothing in it at all, for a line to be added to the order's own record.

"Add this," he said. "In my hand, in the file, dated today, and let it be read into the transcript. *I signed this instrument on the twenty-fourth day of the month. It is carried into public appeal and I do not ask for it to be withdrawn. I ask to be entered in a register of the contested closures of this district, and to be the first name in it, and to be the one who signs the entries.*"

The clerk of the docket asked him whether he understood what he had asked for.

"Yes," said Halden Pike. "There is a register. It has ninety-one entries in it going back a century and there are three names in this district on it and the last one was my predecessor, who is dead. Every closure this district contests goes in it and the officer who signed the closure signs the entry, in his own hand, in the year it is contested, and the register is public and it is printed once a year and sent to the Council's counter and anybody may read it at a counter and take a copy away.

"I have got about nine years. I have been a Deputy Registrar for nineteen and I have got about nine and I would like them signed and I would like them printed, and I am not resigning and nobody is going to make me, and if I am moved sideways in the spring then somebody else signs the rest of them and I will have signed the first four."

Nobody in the room said anything, and the clerk of the docket wrote it, and Owen Rusk understood, standing at the back of a room in a building where he is a stranger, that a man had just volunteered the only column in this city with a place in it for twenty-two people, and had done it about nine seconds after the hearing that would have made him ask for it.

---

He got back up the hill at about eight in the evening and went to the copying floor at about half past eight and put his tray down and did not work it for about four minutes.

He had won the argument. That was the plain fact and it was a good one, in the sense that everything he had wanted since the first week of the month was in a transcript: the order on public appeal, the sixteen on the record with the sixth named, the column not being a reason, the schedule struck at eight in the morning, a comparative that nobody had made, the question asked in a room of thirty people by a woman in a capacity of her own.

And he had not felt anything about it at about a quarter past twelve, and he had felt nothing about it on the boat, and he had felt nothing about it on the hill.

What he had done in that room — and he worked it out standing up at the end of the second desk at about ten past nine at night, which is now how he thought, and which Ada Rusk would have found funny — was describe a list.

He had described five particulars to a room of thirty people, and two of the five were not his and one of them was a man of fifty-eight in an oiled coat who got up in public and said that being named was worth less than money and more than nothing, and one of them was a woman who sells nothing in particular who stood up and read a number out of a book in a ward hall in a voice that carried.

And he had won, because a list of unresolved things, described properly, in a room, by a person who is on the page and answerable for it, is the only argument in this case that has never once been made before.

He had been a copy clerk for ten weeks who thought his function was to notice a discrepancy. It was not. It was to be the person on the page who can say, in order, without a sheet, the exact things that have not been resolved, and to be answerable for having said them, and to come back tomorrow and say the same five in the same order with whatever has changed in it.

That is what he was for now. He did not like it and it was not going to improve, and there was a form on his tray with five lines in it that he was the only person in this city who could fill in, and the tray had two hundred sheets on it, and the press was cold, and on Thursday he was going to be at the top of Fetter Lane at seven forty in the rain with three people because a Thursday is a Thursday.

At the bottom of the hill the lamp in the room at the back of the ward hall was on, and the pump had been primed at four and had taken on the first stroke, and the second bin was a quarter gone and written down, and the notice on the weir path was out in the weather, and the sixteen were still sixteen.
