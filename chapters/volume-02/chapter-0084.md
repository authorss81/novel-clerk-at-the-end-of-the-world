# Chapter 84 — The Ward Performs

The post at the head of Fetter Lane has three notices on it and has had three notices on it since the twenty-eighth day of the month before last, and Owen Rusk had stopped at it on the Tuesday morning of the eighteenth day of the third month on his way down and looked at all three of them the way he looks at everything, from the top, and did not take any of them down.

The first is the notice at the weir path, which is a different post two hundred yards further on and which is worth describing on its own. It has been in the weather since the flood. It is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds of repair to the crossing, and it invites any district able to perform the repair to perform it, and it is the only one of the three documents on any post in this ward that is still in the condition it was put up in. The rain has taken the ink out of one line and left the rest. It has been up since the month of rain and it has brought no district, and it is going to be there in June, and the reason it is going to be there in June is not neglect: **nobody has taken it down because the repair is unfunded and the day the notice comes down is the day this ward has stopped asking.**

The second is the counting season's notice with the ten names on it and the column nobody in this county can fill, put up on the twenty-eighth of the month before last, and it is the third of the three and it is not weather-beaten because it is under the other two.

The third is last season's, with eleven names on it, and every printed week on it gone or nearly gone, and it has been on that post since the twenty-eighth of the first month of the year, and nobody has taken it down either, and the reason that one is not taken down is that **a person standing at a post can see that a thing was on a list last season and is on one this season, and that is the whole of what a post at the head of a lane is for, and it is not nothing.**

He stood there for about a minute and a half. Then he went down the lane.

---

The crossing is at the bottom and it is closed to loads and to vehicles above four tons, and it has been closed since the tenth day of the month of rain, and the barrier is two lengths of timber nailed across with a notice on a wire, and the notice says **CROSSING CLOSED TO LOADS** and below it in a different hand, added later, **AND TO VEHICLES ABOVE FOUR TONS**, and the second line is in Marguerite Bray's hand and is the only thing on that barrier in this county that anybody has added to a notice about a public work in nine years.

Under the notice the water is up to the third rung of the ladder on the Fetter side and about two rungs on the other, and the level comes and goes over about forty minutes and he watched it do that, and there is a post in the middle of the cut with a tide board nailed to it that the ward put up themselves in the first month of the year, with a number on it in chalk that is wrong twice a week because chalk goes in water, and the numbers are gone in the bottom half.

Cobb Rennie was on the Fetter side of the barrier with a length of wood and a bit of chalk, because the tide board is his, in the way that most things in this ward are somebody's, and he does not think of it as his and would deny it.

"**Two years and one month,**" he said, to the board.

"Yes."

"And that notice has been in the weather since the flood and it invites any district in the county that is able to do the work to come and do the work, and it has brought nobody, and it is going to bring nobody, and I have stopped waiting, and I want you to hear me say that I have stopped waiting in front of the notice."

"I hear it."

"**Because there is a thing people do,**" said Cobb Rennie, "**which is to keep a notice up as though the weather might bring somebody. And the weather is not going to bring anybody. What is going to happen is that one day that notice will be so old that the ink will be gone on the second line, and then somebody is going to write a new one, because the repair has not been done, and the new one will be a better notice than the old one and the wall will look cared for.**"

He wrote a number on the board and rubbed half of it out with his sleeve.

"**Four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds,**" he said. "**And I have it in this ward's book, because Marguerite ruled it in on the morning of the seventy-seventh day of the Concord Year 487, with the day and the hour, and it was in that book before the month of rain and it has not moved and it is not going to move, and I have read it about four times and I would like somebody to tell me one thing about it and nobody in this county can.**"

"Which thing."

"**What it is for,**" said Cobb Rennie, and he said it the way he asks the price of something. "**I know what the crossing does. I have watched a great many things go over it and I have watched one thing not go over it and that is the only one I think about. I would like to know what four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds is for, because that is a figure and figures are supposed to be about a thing, and every figure I have read this season has been about nothing that anybody could stand in front of and look at.**"

Owen Rusk stood in about four inches of water on a shut crossing and thought about it for longer than he meant to.

"**It is for the timber and the piles and the hands,**" he said. "**It is a figure of works maintained and works maintained is a thing the Count is a count of, and it is the only figure I have ever seen that is about a repair and a repair is a real thing with a real cost, and there is a notice on a post at the top of that lane with that figure on it, and it has been there since the flood.**"

"**Yes,**" said Cobb Rennie, "**and I have read it, and I have walked past it, and I have read the line under it about any district able to perform the repair, and I have wanted somebody to come for about four months, and nobody is coming, and I have worked out that the notice is not for a district. It is for a person who wants to know that we asked.**"

"That is what it is for."

"**Then it did its job,**" said Cobb Rennie, "**and I am not going to take it down, and I would like it noticed that I said that out loud at the barrier, and that it is the first good thing I have had to say about a figure since the month of rain, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and there is nothing to thank.**"

He went along the weir path to the pump house, which is a brick hut about the size of a small boat with a hand pump inside it and a stone trough and a green door that has to be shouldered.

The grant is suspended and not extinguished. Those are two words in a file in a building two miles up the hill and everybody in this ward knows them and neither of them has ever been said out loud at the pump.

**The pump is primed at four in the morning on the Thursday by two people, one of whom is a household with a girl of twelve in it.** It has been primed on the Thursday since the week the grant was suspended, which was in the month of rain, and before the month of rain it was primed on the Mondays and the Thursdays, and before that it was primed on the Fridays by a man from the water gate, and the man from the water gate came on the Saturday after the flood and was sorry four times.

You prime a pump by putting water in it over the top until the head is full and then you work the handle until the whole column of it comes up out of the well, and it takes about eleven minutes, and the water that comes out of the first stroke is the water that was standing in the pipe and it is brown. Two people because one person has to work the handle and the other has to hold the bucket and pull the chain and put the bucket on the trough, and the chain is a link chain about the length of an arm and it is heavy in the cold.

Nobody is paid for it. The crew is nine and their wage is four months in arrears and it is a written condition of a roster that nobody funds, and it is their wage and not the district's, and the district office has been told that in a form and has answered the form in four words with five clauses under it, and neither of those things has touched the pump.

---

The granary is at the bottom of Fetter Lane and it is open and it is empty and it has been empty since the seventh day of the second month, which is **thirty-nine days**, and there is no door on it any more because the door came off in the flood and was used, and it is a stone floor and a dry wall and a smell of old grain that does not go, and the bins are along the back wall and there are eleven of them and they are the same eleven bins and the weights are the same weights.

The fourth week running of nothing was written up on the Thursday afternoon of the sixth day of the third month in Marguerite Bray's own hand, bin by bin, in about two hours and ten minutes, and she did not shorten it.

On the Tuesday morning the eighteenth Owen Rusk went in there with his coat on and counted the bins himself, without saying he was going to, and got eleven, and wrote the eleven on the back of his hand in pencil and then rubbed it out, and the rubbing out took longer than the writing.

**The fourth week running of nothing is already ruled in this ward's book in Marguerite Bray's own hand with the day and the hour against it, and the figure is going to be nothing for the sixth week running on Wednesday night, and nobody is going to buy anything, and the decision not to buy was made on the first day of the second month by a woman in a hall and it was correct, and it is still correct in the third month, and it will be correct in June.**

At the back of the granary, past the bins, there is a slope and at the bottom of the slope there is a flat place about the size of a good-sized room, and it is silt, and it is the pitch.

**The pitch is not premises.** It is on a piece of paper in a building two miles up the hill and it is the thing this ward has instead of a settlement, and the count of this district wants premises of record, and there are eleven doors in this ward and the ward has a pitch under silt, and it is one of about four things in this county that cannot be put in a column and one of about nine that cannot be put in two.

Owen Rusk had stood in it in the first month of the year. He did not stand in it on the Tuesday. He looked at it from the dry wall for about twenty seconds and then went out, because there is nothing about a pitch that four years of looking improves.

Ada Rusk was in the doorway with a lamp she had not lit.

"**You did not go in it.**"

"No."

"**Good,**" said Ada Rusk. "**I want to say that it is not you. It is that there are nine other people in this county who could come and look at it and none of them will, and the only one of the nine who is going to is the one who already knows what is in it, and that is a thing about this county and not about you, and I have said that sentence in a hall twice and I am saying it in a granary with a lamp in my hand and it is the same sentence.**"

"The pitch is a pitch," said Owen Rusk.

"**The pitch is a pitch and it is a pitch with about nine feet of silt on it and the silt came off the flats in the month of rain and the flats are where the marker is, and the marker is the only figure anybody in this ward produces that anybody can check by standing in the wrong place.**" She put the lamp down on the sill. "**And nobody in this county is going to print a column that says how deep the silt is at the bottom of our granary, and if somebody did, the figure would be the only one in the count that a person could go and look at, and there is not one column in the whole of this season's paperwork that anybody could go and look at. Not one. I have been trying all season to think of one and I have got the marker and the marker is ours.**"

---

The channel is a Wednesday.

It is a drain that comes down off the flats behind the ward and goes along the back of the lane and out under the crossing, and it does the same work every week of the year whether anybody is looking at it or not, and it is silted in three places and it is done at night because it is easier at night and because the woman who organised it, who is Ada Rusk and who is twenty-four years old, decided in the first year of it that the ward would do the things it does at the hours it can do them and not the hours an office is open.

They were in it from about nine.

There were about fourteen people, which is most of the crew of nine plus four of their households plus Owen Rusk with a spade he had been lent and did not ask for, and the lanterns were four and the moon did about a third of the work.

The channel is nine feet at the top and two feet at the bottom in the narrow place and about four feet of water in it in the ordinary week. It is not dangerous and that is the honest description of it, and it is filthy, and it is the one thing about this ward that every single person in that channel has agreed to be disgusted by.

He worked the middle section for about two hours and got about nine feet of it down to a hard bottom, and there is nothing romantic about nine feet and it is the whole of the third week's work and it will silt up again by the second week of next month.

**Nobody is thanked for it. It is a channel and there is no column for it and it does not appear on the schedule of settlements to be reconciled and it is not going to and the eleven doors in this ward draw through a pump that a household primes at four in the morning and a great deal of the water in this channel ends up in the same pail.**

It came up about four hundred gallons of silt in about eleven minutes and settled in the bucket as fine as flour and Ada Rusk had it carted away to the flats in a barrow in the week of the flood because that is where the ward puts what comes out of its own channel, and putting it back on the flats where it came from is the single most expensive thing anybody in this ward has ever done and nobody has ever costed it.

"**A mill two hours up a river got NONE on a form in the district's own hand in the second month,**" said Owen Rusk, without meaning to, and about nine people in a drain heard it.

Nobody answered him and he did not finish the sentence, and after about four minutes Bram Tillet said, without stopping work, "**We put it back where it came from.**"

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"**Then you have got two things in a season that are in a form in a building and one of them is us, and ours is not in a building, and I would like it noted that that is the whole of what I have got to say about it and I am not going to say it again.**"

At about half past eleven Ada Rusk came down the bank with a lantern and stood at the top of the ladder and did not come down, and she said one thing.

"**You are going to ask me at the end of the week whether you have been in the way this season and I am going to tell you that the answer is yes on Thursdays and no on Wednesdays, and I have had it ready since the second of the month.**"

"I have not been in the way on a Wednesday in my life," said Owen Rusk.

"**You have been in the way on a Wednesday twice, in the month of rain, and both times you were right, and I have written down the dates, and one of them is the fourth and one of them is the eleventh of the month of rain, and I am not going to have an argument with you about the difference between being right and being in the way, because you have worked that out and it took you a season and I have known it since I was nineteen.**"

Cobb Rennie was at the ladder with a bar he had brought himself and had not been asked for either, and he said, over the noise of nine people in a drain:

"**Four months.**"

"Yes."

"**It is four months and a bit.** It was four months on the twenty-third of the month before last and it is four months and a bit now and I have written the day and the hour in the book as well, in my own hand, under Bray's, and she did not ask me to and she has not told me to take it out."

"Why did you."

"Because in about four years somebody is going to ask how long," said Cobb Rennie, "and there is no column, and I am the sort of man who writes a number down."

---

They came out of the channel at about ten past two on the Wednesday morning in the middle of the third month and it was two degrees and the fog came off the flats and made a lantern into nothing about thirty feet out, and Owen Rusk put a spade back against a wall and had frost-numbed hands and could not get them warm for about ten minutes, and the two of them who go up to the pump house at four were already going.

Bram Tillet is forty-one and is one of the four names the word is not written beside, and he does not talk in the channel and does not talk at the pump either, and what he says at the pump is *hold that* and *let go*, and it is the only work in this county that nobody has ever found a column for.

**The two are Bram Tillet and Ivy Sarr.**

Ivy Sarr is twelve. She is the household that the fourth day of the fourth month of last year was about, and she is one of the six persons in this ward whom it cannot ask, and she is on a rota for a standpipe at four in the morning because the rota is what the ward has instead of a person who can be asked, and **there is no person in this ward who keeps anything for anybody, and the word for that is not written beside anybody's name and is not going to be.**

She is about four foot ten and she is in a coat belonging to somebody about twice her size and she can lift the bucket of priming water on her own, which took her about a year to manage and which she does not mention.

The pump house is nine feet by seven and there is one lamp and the green door is off its top hinge, and at about ten to four Owen Rusk came down the lane on his own because he had not been asked and had not offered and there was nothing in that lane for him to do, and he stood outside the door in the dark and listened, and he stayed about an hour and ten minutes and did not go in, which was on purpose and was the fourth time he had done it in nine months.

The first stroke at about four minutes past four brought up the water that was standing in the pipe and it was brown, and the sound of a hand pump at four in the morning in a stone hut in a lane is a sound that does not vary and he has noticed that it does not vary and has decided that it is the only fact of his working life that has never once needed a form.

At about half past four they came out and Ivy Sarr had the bucket and it was full and she put it down on the trough and then sat on the step with it, in the cold, and did not say anything, and he said goodnight to her and she said goodnight, and then she said:

"**You stand out there a lot.**"

"I do."

"**Bram says you are not allowed to be in it and Bram is wrong and I am not going to say he is wrong out loud because he is forty-one.**"

"**You may tell him you said it and you may not tell him I heard it,**" said Owen Rusk, "and that is a rule and not a joke, and I will keep it."

"Okay," said Ivy Sarr, and stood up, and then, as though it were the ordinary end of an ordinary night, and it was: "**The bins are empty again and there is not going to be anything in them on Thursday either.**"

"I know."

"**That is all right,**" she said, "because the trays are not stopped," and she went up the lane with the empty bucket in one hand at a quarter to five in the morning, without a lamp, because the lane knows the way and she has been going up it in the dark since she was ten.

The marker goes up on the Thursday and on the Friday at seven forty. It is a post and a board and it is not a notice of anything, it is a board with the depth of the water on the flats written on it in chalk every week, and there is no form in this county for it and no office has ever heard of it and about nine people walk down the lane at half past seven to read it, and it is a figure about a place, written by a person who was at the place, and **it is the only figure about a place in this ward that anybody can check by looking at the thing it is a figure of.** Ada Rusk had said that sentence in this ward's granary on the Tuesday and in this ward's hall on the Monday before, and Owen Rusk had heard both of them and had not understood either until he was standing in front of a board on the Friday morning of the twenty-first with a bit of chalk in his hand and a girl of twelve coming down the lane behind him, and he put the chalk down and went up the hill, and he did not go down the lane again that week, and nobody noticed.
