# Chapter 63 — What Carries It

The form is a single sheet printed one side and it is item fourteen, and it asks four things.

**One. The objector. Name, and the premises at which the objector may be asked.**

**Two. The settlement. Name, and the year of the last reconciliation.**

**Three. The ground of the challenge. State it as a ground and not as a grievance.**

**Four. The figure you say should stand instead.**

He wrote it on the Sunday evening of the twentieth day of the first month of the Concord Year 488, at the table in two rooms in Fetter Lane, with the lamp out because the lamp made him think he was working, which was a thing he had started doing in the autumn and had not told anybody about.

He wrote the first line in and stopped for about a minute, because **there is no premises at which Owen Rusk may be asked** in the ordinary sense, and because a person may be asked at the premises of the district office, and because the officer of the record of the district returns has no premises at all, and a form that asks for a place to be asked at and cannot be given a place is a form telling you something about yourself before you have written anything.

He put *the office of the district returns, Docketford* and underlined it, and wrote under the underline: *I am the officer of the record of the district returns out of which this settlement is taken and I have no other premises.*

The second line: *Hark Holm. Struck from the count of Docketford district in the Concord Year 484. No reconciliation of the Concord Year 488 is proposed and none is asked for.*

And then he wrote four grounds under the third line, and he numbered them, and the numbering is the whole of what he did on that Sunday evening and it took him about two hours.

---

**Ground the first.** That the four premises of Harkholm should have remained upon the count of this district in the season of the Concord Year 488.

**He struck that one out before the ink was dry on it**, and he wrote underneath it, in the same hand and not smaller, *I do not argue this and I am not permitted to put it in a reason column and I would be ashamed of it.*

Because a person who cannot answer for themselves cannot consent, and a count correction is a statement about premises and not about people, and the premises were four of four, and he had read the schedule of premises attached to the correction and there is not one error in it, and if he stood in a room and argued that the four should have stayed on he would be arguing for a thing he did not believe, in order to win a smaller thing, and Ada Rusk would have found out inside a week.

**Ground the second.** That the correction of the Concord Year 484 was made without authority.

**He struck that one out too.** The correction was made by the Provincial Counting Office and attested by the Warden and it is dated and it has a schedule of premises attached to it and a reason in a box, and every part of it is exactly what it says it is, and it is four years old, and the only thing wrong with it is that it is four years old, and age is not a defect in an instrument; age is a fact about a season, and a season is a fact about a correction.

**Ground the third.** That the four households at Hark Holm in the Concord Year 484 ought to have been asked what they wished.

He did not strike this one out. He sat with it for about forty minutes.

He wrote under it: *I do not argue this. A person who cannot answer for themselves cannot be asked what they wish, and the method does not permit it, and if it were permitted then no notice in this county could ever be served on anybody at all, and the fourteen days would not exist. I do not argue it and I would like it recorded that I have thought about it for forty minutes and that I have put it down because a form that carries a ground a man will not argue is a better form than one that does not.*

And he wrote one more line under that, which he crossed out and then wrote again because it was the true one and he had been writing around it all evening:

**The four were not asked what they wished, and the eleven were not asked what they wished either, and neither fact is anybody's fault, and the only thing anybody can be asked about it in a room is a season.**

**Ground the fourth.** *That the count of the Concord Year 488 of Docketford district is being taken with an entry in the standing extract of places struck from the count of this district, entered in the season of 488, against Hark Holm, upon a count correction made in the season of 484; that a count correction has effect for the season in which it is made, by the words on the face of it, which are **struck from the count of this district for this season**; that no instrument in the count provides for the effect of a correction to be carried into a later season, and no office in this county has ever been asked to carry one, because there is nothing to ask; and that the entry made in the season of 488 is therefore made without authority, and that the practice of making it is ended in this district from the day of the finding.*

And then line four, which is the one the form was built for and which took him about nine minutes and which is the best thing he has ever written on a printed form:

*The figure you say should stand instead.*

*No figure of the Concord Year 488. The figure four is a figure of the Concord Year 484 and belongs to the Concord Year 484, and the entry that carries it has no season of its own, and there is nothing in this district to put in that box for the season of 488 except the figure four again, and I am asking that no figure be put.*

He read the whole of it back to himself twice. It was two sides of a sheet's worth of it in one side, and it was correct, and it was the narrowest thing he had ever written, and the narrowness was the entire point.

---

He lodged it at the Glass Stair at twenty past nine on the Monday morning of the twenty-first day of the month, at the count room on the ground floor at the back, at the counter with the rail round three sides and the public stand on it, and the counting clerk at the second counter read the four lines and then read the fourth line twice and put her hand flat on the sheet, which is not a thing anybody does to a document in a public room and which she did anyway.

"That is a figure box and you have put a season in it," she said.

"Yes."

"I am not going to argue with a man about a season." She turned the sheet round and stamped it, and the stamp is the only part of it that was not his. "There is a day on this. It is a hearing before an officer of the count having no district in the matter and there is one of those in this city this week, and she is the same one that examined your application, and it is **the twenty-fourth, at ten in the morning, in the long room**, and there is a seven-day period for objection and the twenty-fourth is inside it and the period does not have to be used."

The twenty-fourth of the month was a Thursday.

Owen Rusk stood at the counter of a public room in a building he had worked in for eight years and worked out three things in about ninety seconds, and the first of them was that the twenty-fourth was inside the seven-day period and he had four days.

The second of them took longer.

"That is the day," he said. "That is the day for Lower Lock. That is the day the warden dated on Friday for the third week."

"Yes."

"And what happens to a reconciliation in a district on a day a challenge to an instrument of the count for that district is before an officer of the count."

The counting clerk turned round and looked at a card on the wall with a number on it, and then she did not look at the card, because she knew it.

"**There is a recitals bar.**" She said it in the flat voice of a person reciting a thing that has been in the same book for a hundred and forty years. "While a challenge to an instrument of the count is before an officer of the count, no reconciliation shall be conducted in the district named in it. It is in the recitals of the counting rules and it has been there since the service was constituted and it is in there because of the year the figures were being changed under people who had not been told, and the office has never had to use it and has never had anybody ask what it means."

"And it is lifted when the challenge is determined."

"It is lifted when the officer determines it, and the officer can lift it earlier if she is asked to and she thinks fit, and nobody has ever asked her to." The counting clerk looked at him. "It will be lifted on the twenty-fourth when she is finished with you, and the day-book takes a line when the line is earned, and there is no line for the twenty-first, and there was never going to be one at ten o'clock this morning because at ten o'clock this morning I stamped a sheet that put it there, and I did not know I was doing that, and I am telling you I did not know."

---

He found Kade at the west gate at about half past ten with the case shut and the man of sixty reading a day-book entry, and he told her in about four sentences standing up in the cold because he had learned in a fortnight that she will stand in the cold indefinitely and will not sit down for you.

She did not interrupt him. When he had finished she looked at the field for about as long as it takes to count a thing twice, and then she said the first of the two things she was going to say, and both of them were about the danger in front of her, and she was right about both, and Owen Rusk wrote them down afterwards and did not lose either.

"**The first one is the danger to me and I am telling you about it because nobody else in this county will.** You have found an error. The error is mine. I attested a correction of the Concord Year 484 and I have had that file open in a season of the Concord Year 484 and in 486 and in 487 and I wrote the figure in the box in 484 and I have not looked at the year on the form since, because in four years the figure has never moved and a figure that does not move is not a thing anybody in this service re-examines, and I have asked twice in writing for a number I could not get and the answer came in eleven weeks both times and both times it was correct, and I have never once in four years read the year at the top of a document I attested.

"You have found the error. The office will agree with you in about four minutes and then the office will send me a settlement a season and I will do it, and I will do it correctly, and I will do nine more of them correctly, and one of the nine will be a place with a figure in it that does not move. And the thing you have done is real and it is the first time anybody has done it, and **it does not follow that the next one will be good.** A challenge is a thing anybody may enter. I cannot tell the difference between a good one and a bad one from inside the form. I have to take them both, and I take them both at the price of my own season, and you are not sorry for me and I would prefer you were not, because a warden who is sorry about it is a warden who is managing him."

"I am not going to argue that with you," said Owen Rusk. "I do not think I can and I do not want to."

"**The second one is the danger in front of me and it is arithmetic and you have done it yourself.**" She turned the ledger round on the trestle table and did not open it. "Your district is short by seven. It is reported short and a qualified count goes up to the province. You are now going to be before an officer of the count on Thursday morning in a room with high windows, and a man in a board is going to be told on Thursday afternoon that a form in this county has been set aside for the first time in its history, and it will be true, and it will be in four newspapers by the end of the week in Concord Hall, and **what happens to a quota after that is not in anybody's gift.** A warden with a quota and a case and a headline is a warden who reconciles faster, and a warden who reconciles faster is a warden who reconciles things that should not have been reconciled. I have done it for nineteen seasons with nobody watching and I have not once done it with a headline. I am telling you that your good work is going to make me worse at this for about a month and I am going to do it anyway."

"I know," said Owen Rusk.

"You cannot know, you are not me, but it is decent of you to say it and it is about the size of a lie, and I have had four years of somebody's decent about the size of a lie and I will take it."

She put her hand flat on the case for a second and did not take it off.

"Two more things and then I have to go and do Rush Halt, which has fourteen households and a notice that has been out since the eighth and a person in it who is a child. **One. Do not make that woman a party.** She is the only person alive who was one of the four, and if she stands in a room as a party then in nine years there will be a file in this county with a seventy-nine year old woman at the top of it and a count correction on the second page, and she will be the person who was on the correction, and there will be no room in that file for the eleven, and the eleven is the only thing anybody actually wants out of it. **Two. Do not read the extract in front of me.**"

"Which part of the extract."

"The part with Hark Holm in it." She said it flatly. "It is going to be on that board and I have known for two days that it is going to be on that board and I have not gone and read it, and I am not going to, and I would be obliged if you would not read it in front of me. **I do not want to watch me find out what I did.** I have known for four years and finding out is a different thing from knowing, and I would like the finding to be in a room where I am doing work."

---

He did not read it in front of her.

He read it on the Tuesday evening of the twenty-second of the month, alone, at the west gate of Cutter's Fields at about a quarter past six, on the second board at the foot of the frame, in a narrower type and a smaller frame, standing up, with a lamp brought out of the tent by the man of sixty, who did not ask and put it down at his feet and went back in.

**PLACES STRUCK FROM THE COUNT OF THIS DISTRICT. Place. Year of the correction. Premises struck. Reason.**

Four hundred and six rows, and it is the oldest public document in Docketford district, and it is a record and not a list, and it has been on that board every season for a hundred and forty years.

The Hark Holm row is the fourth from the bottom.

**HARK HOLM. 484. Four. Four.**

Two figures. Four premises struck, and a reason, and the reason is four, and the reason is a figure about the same four premises, and the year is a hundred and forty years of somebody's clerical habit and one row that should not be in it.

He read that row four times. He did not read the row above it. He did not read the row below it, and the row below it was the third from the bottom, and he had read the third row from the bottom once, on the twelfth day of this month, with the light going, and had stopped, and had said out loud to a field why, and the reason was a drawer under black cord on the ground floor of the River Desk.

He stood there for about a minute and a half with a lamp at his feet and worked out the difference between the two rows, and the difference was about nine lines of a printed form and eleven days of a river in a bad year, and it is the only difference there has ever been in his working life between a thing he may do and a thing he may not, and he could not have told anybody at the counter of the public room on the Monday morning which one was which without about ninety seconds and a pencil.

The row below was a ward that was officially closed in the Concord Year four hundred and sixty-one, struck out of the count with a figure against it in the same ink in the same ordinary hand, and it had been on that board for a hundred and forty years, and it was not a settlement and it had no premises and it was not on the schedule of settlements to be reconciled and it was not on any list of eleven, and **a fourth column on a board in a field and a drawer in a building are the same decision in two places**, and he had made that decision on a stair in the last month of last year and a man does not get to make it twice in a season.

The row above it was Hark Holm, and reading it was the fourth limb of a ground of challenge, and the ground of challenge was going to be read out in a room with high windows on the Thursday morning by an officer having no district in the matter, and it is the first time anybody in this city has challenged a count correction, and the person at the top of the frame is a woman of about fifty from Concord Hall who has read four hundred of these and has seen one of them filled in twice.

He put the lamp back on the trestle table at about ten past seven, and the day-book on that table had four lines on it and the quota of eleven at the head of it, and he read the four, and one of them was Nether Sill, and it said **struck**, and the figure against it was four, and the word against that was four.

Then he went up the hill and did the tray and was on it at about a quarter to seven, and there was a sheet of paper in his coat with a woman's name at the top of it and about nine lines under the name, and the boat was going up the reach on the Tuesday morning and again on the Wednesday, and he had left a note for the girl who pilots in a fold of a tide table on the trestle table at the head of the Wexford Landing slip, and it was five lines long and it said that there was a hearing at the Glass Stair on the Thursday at ten in the morning and that there was a boat on the Wednesday and that if a woman of seventy-nine wanted to come down to a room with high windows she could, and that she would not be asked to speak, and that if she came she would be the only person in the room who had been one of the four households and that this was not a party and there was no form on which it could be made one.

It was the only note he has ever written that he did not want to send.
