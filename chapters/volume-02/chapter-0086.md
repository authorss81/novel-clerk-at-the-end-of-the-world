# Chapter 86 — A Count of Nothing

He wrote it on the Saturday morning of the twenty-second day of the third month at the table in two rooms in Fetter Lane with the lamp out, because it is a thing he has written before and it goes better in the dark, and it took him about fifty minutes, and it is four sheets and it is correct, and it says so first, because everything else in it is about a man who did everything right and got nothing for it that anybody can use.

The form is a single sheet printed one side, item fourteen, and it asks four things: the objector and the premises at which he may be asked; the settlement and the instrument; the grounds, numbered; and the relief asked for. He wrote the first two in about four minutes, and he is the officer of the record of the district returns, so the premises at which he may be asked is the district office at Fetter Bend, which is a building, and it is the same building as the tenth row of a printed schedule, and he did not think about that for about nine seconds and then he stopped thinking about it, on purpose, because the moment he thinks about it he has a document about the schedule and the only permitted act with that is nothing.

Then the grounds, and there is one of them, and there is a preamble above the grounds, and the preamble is not a ground and does not get numbered.


**And the forty minutes were the whole of his growth this season and nobody in the world is ever going to know about them. The hardest four were these. He wrote out four sentences one after the other in the form of grounds, and then he read them back, and every one of the four was an argument about the schedule, or about a choice of places, or about the reason column, and he struck all four out, and then he wrote the same four sentences out again as a preamble instead of as grounds. The sentences did not change and their number did not and the box they went in did, and that is the whole of what a Continuance Examiner is, and it is not a technique and it is not clever and it is not what anybody would call progress, and somebody is going to read a determination of this county in four years that says *the applicant is right* and is going to have no idea at all that it took him a lamp and a table and forty minutes and a decision about which box he was allowed to write in.**

He wrote:

*Applicant is a Continuance Examiner of the Count of Docketford district and is an officer of the office in which the district returns of record are made, and the district return for the season of 489 was made in that office. He has an interest in the matter within the meaning of the form. He says so and does not conceal it.*

Then the preamble, and it is not a ground, and it is the sentence he learned in a corridor in another city in the first month of the year and did not know he had learned until the twenty-ninth of the second:

**I do not say the figures are wrong. I do not say the schedule was made wrongly. I do not say that any settlement was chosen, and I have nothing to say about the tenth row and I will not say it. I do not put a word in the reason column and there is no reason column in this document and I have checked. My ground is arithmetic and the currency of a paper, and the paper is the district return for this season, and it is my own office's return, and it is in my own office's record, and I have made it.**

And then the ground, and there was one, and here it is.

**That the district return for the season of the Concord Year 489 was received in the state in which it was made, figures carried forward, no verification of premises in this district in the season of 489, and that the ten rows of premises of record upon it are therefore not figures of the season of 489. That a count is a count of a season named on it. That where no person was in any of the ten settlements of record in this district in the season of 489, no figure upon that return is a figure of that season; and that an arithmetic of a certification which describes such figures as the figures counted in that season does not add up to a statement of what the count is a count of. That the applicant does not say the ten figures are wrong. He says they are not of this season, and he says the difference between the two claims is the whole of this document.**

And the relief, and it is four lines, and it is the part of the whole thing that took the fifty minutes:

**That the Office do one of two things, and which one is the Office's to choose, and both of them are true, and the applicant does not say which.**

**One. Produce the figures of premises of record counted for Docketford district in the season of the Concord Year 489.**

**Two. Print that it counted none.**

Then he signed it, and dated it the twenty-second, and read it back once, which is one time fewer than Marguerite Bray and it is not the same document.

---

He took it up the hill at about half past two on the Saturday afternoon and put it on the counter at the second one at the Glass Stair at about ten past three.

The counting clerk read the first page and the fourth and did not read the second and the third, which is what a person who has read about four hundred of these does.

"**One ground,**" she said.

"One ground."

"**And a preamble, which is not a ground, and which is the thing the woman with a slate is going to read out first and is going to be pleased about and is going to make a point of, and I want you to know that I have read it and that you did not write it for me and what it is worth is not a thing I am going to say.**"

"What it is worth."

"Nothing," said the counting clerk. "**That is what a preamble is.** It is the difference between a man who knows what he is not saying and a man who has not noticed, and nobody in this county has ever been able to put that difference in a column, and somebody is going to make a decision on one of these two one day and it is going to be made badly."

She squared it on the counter and then put her hand flat on it, which is not a thing anybody does to a document, and which she has now done twice in nine years.

"And the answer."

"You told me on Wednesday. You told me on the Wednesday and I have had three days to think about it and I worked it out on Wednesday in about ninety seconds before you said it and I will not insult you by pretending."

"Then I am not going to say it again," said the counting clerk, "and I am going to do the ordinary thing, which is to give you the date and the room, and the room is the long one and it is the one with the high windows and it is the one that holds about nine, and you have been in it once this year and the other time you were right and this time you will be right as well and it will not be the same right, and I would like you to hold on to that on Sunday morning and then let it go about eleven o'clock because it is not going to help you." 

She wrote **SUNDAY, TWENTY-THIRD, LONG ROOM, HALF PAST EIGHT** on the corner of a slip and gave him the slip.

"That is not a form and it is not a receipt and it is not a promise about the outcome," she said. "I do not know the outcome. I know the shape. Those are two different things and this county has spent a hundred and forty years pretending they are the same and that is the whole of what is wrong with it."

---

The long room at the Glass Stair holds about nine people and has four windows in the top half of the far wall and a rail down one side with a gap at it, and the gap is where the person whose matter it is stands, and Owen Rusk had stood in that gap in the first month of the year and been right about a document, and on the Sunday morning of the twenty-third day of the third month he stood in it again at half past eight with about nine people in the room, and the woman of about fifty with a slate and no coat was on the bench with her knees apart and a folder she did not open for the first twenty minutes.

"Read your preamble," she said.

He read the preamble.

Nobody said anything at it and that was correct, and then the officer read it out loud herself, in the ordinary voice, word for word, and it took her about fifteen seconds, and she said:

"**I am going to record that the applicant began by setting out four things he is not saying. I am going to record it because somebody is going to read this determination one day and is going to have to know what kind of man made it, and they are not going to be left to work it out from the ground.**"

"May I ask the officer to—" said Owen Rusk.

"No," said the officer of the count, "because I have said it. I have said it in a room with nine people in it and it is now on three sides of this room and you do not need a fourth."

She read the ground. She read the two reliefs.

Then she said, "**And I am going to ask you one question before I determine anything, and it is the standing question and you have heard it and you have answered it before, and the answer does not change and I would like the room to hear the answer rather than me.**"

"The figure," said Owen Rusk, "is the premises of record, and the figure is a figure of the premises of record of a settlement of this district, and the figure was put in the book of premises of record of this district office in a season before this one, and no person in the district office has been at any of the ten premises in the season of 489, and I am the officer of the record of that book, and I put the office's forward on it, and the office's forward is the note at the foot of the return, which is that the return is received in the state in which it was made."

"Yes," said the officer of the count. "And that is the whole of the matter and it has taken you four minutes, which is two minutes fewer than the last time, and I am going to say why it is two minutes fewer and then I am going to determine it and nobody is going to be thanked for either."

She wrote for about four minutes and then she read it out.

"**Sustained on the first ground, and on arithmetic, and I am recording that it is arithmetic, and I want that recorded in this determination and not only here: I have determined one matter on the third limb in eleven years and it was a season, and I will not determine two in one season in the same county, and in about four years somebody is going to write a short history of this district and the history is going to say that the currency limb was used twice in it, and it is going to be wrong, and there is no instrument on which I can correct it in about four years.**"

Somebody at the back said something. She did not look round.

"**The two reliefs stand as they were written. Both of them are true. The Office may choose. And I am going to say what I think it will choose, once, and then I am not going to say it again: it will print that it counted none, because the return is on your own file with my building's hand on it saying that no verification took place, and because a body asked to produce figures it has not got will produce them, and because the count of this district for this season is in fact a count of nothing, and that is not an absurdity. It is the first honest description of a season this county has printed, and it is going to be the most dangerous document a district office has ever certified, and neither of those two things is the officer of the count's business.**"

A man in the third seat said, "**Ma'am, if the district office produced no figures, then what is the count of.**"

"**That is exactly the right question and it is the applicant's question and I will not answer it in this room, because the answer is that the count of this district for the season of 489 is a count of nothing, and that sentence is going to be printed on Friday with a warden's hand at the foot of it, and it is not this room's sentence and it is not yours.**" She closed the folder about halfway, which is a thing a person does when they have said the part they came for. "I am not asked to prefer either of the two reliefs and I do not prefer either. I have said once what I think and I am now going to stop."

---

He came out of the long room at about ten past nine on the Sunday morning of the twenty-third day of the third month and Junia Pell was on the bottom step of the Glass Stair, which she was not by arrangement, and she had a folded sheet in her hand which was the register extract and which she folded again as soon as he was in front of her.

"Well?"

"Sustained on the first ground, on arithmetic, and the two reliefs stand and the Office may choose, and she told me in the room what she thinks it will choose, once, and then told the room she was going to stop."

"Then it is done," said Junia Pell, "and I am going to say four things and I am going to say them quickly and I am not going to be thanked for any of them."

"Say them."

"One. **Nobody is struck.** No household comes off anything. No draw goes back to a main. Nobody loses water. No person in this county is fourteen days further from a supported living restoration than they were on Friday, because of anything you did in that room." She put the sheet in her bag. "Two. **The only cost of that win falls on your own office, and your own office made the figures, and it is the correct cost and nobody in Concord Hall is going to be asked to pay it and nobody is going to be hurt by it.** Three. **It is the first thing you have won since the first day of the month of rain that has cost nobody outside the room, and I am saying that once, out loud, in daylight, and once is all of it there is going to be this season, and I would like you to sit with it for about ten seconds and not turn it into something.**"

Owen Rusk stood on the bottom step of the Glass Stair and did what she had told him to do for about ten seconds.

"Four," said Junia Pell. "**Somebody is going to read that determination one day and it is going to say that a Continuance Examiner of the Count challenged the return of his own office on the ground that his own office had not gone out, and won, and then made the paper current four weeks later. And if you make that paper current, that is the whole of the rest of your life, and I have just told you in the first week of this month that you worked out how to stop the most and sent the answer up the stairs yourself. So: do not make it current.**"

"I have not made it current."

"You have written ten dates on a sheet of foolscap and put it in the back of a book," said Junia Pell, "and I have not been in that room and I have never asked to be, and I know what is in the back of that book, and I am not going to say one word about it, and **that is me not saying it and that is the last time either of us raises it.**"

He said, out loud, on the step, in the ordinary voice, once, and then never again:

"It has cost nobody outside this room. That is the first one in a season and there is not going to be a speech about it."

"Good," said Junia Pell. "Now go and do the tray."

He did not go and do the tray. He sat down on the bottom step of the Glass Stair for about a quarter of an hour with the determination not yet in his hands, because a determination takes a day to come down and he was not going to go and do the tray on the strength of a thing that had not been written yet.

What he did on the step was to go back over the four minutes on the Saturday morning, and to ask himself the standing question that he had been asked in a room with high windows in the first month of the year — *state the figure, state what the figure is a figure of, and state who put it there* — and to answer it a fourth time, on a step, to nobody.

The figure is premises of record. The figure is a figure of the premises of record of a settlement in this district. It was put in a book in this building by a district office in a season before this one, and the district office is the office of the record of it, and he is the officer of the record of the district returns that the book is the book of, and the note at the foot of the return is the receiving office's own, and the receiving office is the same building.

**All three parts of the standing question, and the third one is the office, and the office is his, and it is going to be his in four years' time when somebody asks the district of Docketford for the reasoning behind three documents and a list, and the answer to the third part of the standing question is a man in this city, and he is going to be stood eight feet off that frame while somebody else says it.**

---

On the Monday morning of the twenty-fourth day of the third month the determination came down at the second desk in a folded sheet with a number on the outside of it, and he read it standing up, in about four minutes, and it is the document in the file and it is a page and a quarter and he has it:

**DETERMINATION. In the matter of a challenge by a Continuance Examiner of the Count, Docketford district, against the district return for the season of the Concord Year 489, heard at the count room at the Glass Stair on the twenty-third day of the third month of the Concord Year 488 before an officer of the count having no district in the matter. The applicant is a person at whom this office has no question and the office raises none. The applicant's interest in the matter is declared on the form and is his office's return and there is no form in this county for a person to be both the officer of the record of a return and a person challenging it, and the office accepts the declaration and proceeds. Four things which the applicant does not say are recorded. The ground is sustained on arithmetic. The two reliefs asked for stand. Either the Office produce the figures of premises of record counted for Docketford district in the season of the Concord Year 489, or the Office print that it counted none. The Office may choose and its choice is not subject to review. The challenge is sustained and it is sustained in full and the applicant is entitled to nothing by it except the two things he asked for and one of them is a decision and one of them is a sentence, and a decision and a sentence are the only two things this county gives anybody, and the office notes that the applicant's own return is the return in question, and that this office has no advice to give him about his own conduct in the matter and has not given any.**

It is the truth of every word of it. He put it in the district file, which is where a determination goes, and the file already had three documents in it with his name at the foot of them, and on the Monday afternoon at about four he stood at the end of the second desk and found that he had nothing in his hands, and that was new, and that in a season in which he had written a statement for a hall, put a ward's own paper in an office's record, entered a challenge, been sustained on arithmetic and taken a real thing off a man's season's work, the first week in which he had come out of a room with his hands empty and something the right shape in them was the week in which nobody's water moved.

**Four days to the certification. The count of the season of 489 for Docketford district is certified on the twenty-eighth day of this month at about ten in the morning at the west gate of Cutter's Fields, in the cold, in front of about twenty people, because that is where it has been certified in this county for as long as anybody can remember, and a Warden of the Census Guard certifies a count, and there is not one other person in this county who may do it.**
