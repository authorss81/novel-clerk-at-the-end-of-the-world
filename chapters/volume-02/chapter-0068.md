# Chapter 68 — A Work Not Maintained

He went up the reach on the Monday morning of the fifth of the second month with the line already in his head, because it had come off the tray on the Friday in a quarterly works return from the second desk in his own building, in a hand that is not his hand and is not anybody's hand on that tray, and it is correct, and there is no person in the county who can say that it is not correct.

**NETHER SILL. MILL. WORKS MAINTAINED: NO. GROUND: no wheel turned in the quarter. RETURNED BY THE FACTOR. DATE: the second day of the month.**

A work not maintained is one of the three things this count is. It is not a correction. It has no schedule of premises attached to it and no reason in a box the size of a hand and no date that anybody chose and no fourteen days and no appeal route, because there is nothing to appeal. A correction says *this place is struck from the count*, and a person may be angry about that. A work not maintained says *this wheel did not turn*.

Prue Hallam took the tiller and did not ask, and about an hour up the reach he said, to the bow, without turning round, the way a person says a thing they have been carrying since Friday.

"They will have heard."

"Yes."

"The girl at the head of the slip told two people on the Saturday and one of them was the woman at the mill and one of them was a man at a kiln who owes money to nobody and everybody." Prue Hallam held the tiller. "They have not heard about the wheel."

"No."

"Then do not lead with the wheel," she said. "Go up and look at the water and come to it after, because they have been watching the water since the twelfth and nobody has come up and said one word about it, and that is worse than the wheel."

---

The wheel was the first thing. Not the worst thing. The first.

The bend is two hours up and it is a working mile, and the mill is at the top of it on a stone sill with a house against it, and the cut runs away north under four feet of silt in a dry summer and carries a head when there is one. Owen Rusk had been up it three times since the first of the month and had not looked at the wheel, because on the first two times he had been looking at a count sheet, and on the third he had been carrying a piece of paper with a woman's name at the top of it and had said almost nothing for six hours.

**The wheel is a wheel.** Nine feet, elm, iron straps, a breast-shot race two feet across, standing in about two inches of water, and it has been standing in about two inches of water since the twentieth day of the month, which is a fortnight and a day, which is a long time for elm.

Neve Searle was on the mill office step in a shawl with her sleeves pushed up. She had not been off the floor in nine days and she said so before he was to the bottom of the steps.

"I know what you have come about," she said, "because the girl at the slip said a man from the district office was going up on Monday, and I have had twenty-three days to work out what I would say to a man from the district office and I have not got one that is true, so I am going to say the true ones and you are going to have to sit down."

He sat down on the step, which is the lowest thing in a mill yard and the only place in those four rooms where a person can sit down without turning round.

---

"**The water went back to the district's main on the twelfth,**" said Neve Searle, "and nobody told us and nobody had to, because the form says it goes back. And on the nineteenth the man from the water gate came up on a Saturday on his own in a cart, and he was very quick about it, and he said he was sorry, and he said he could not do anything else, and he said it about four times. I have had sixteen days to decide whether to be angry at him and I cannot, because he is a man with a gate and a form, and the rule does not have a place in it for what the mill is."

"What did the gate do to the head."

"**The gate at the top of the cut has been a hand's width open every day since the nineteenth,**" said Neve Searle, "and it has to be, because there is a main on the other side of it that has got the water of nine premises in it now, and if you do not pass that water it stands in the main and the main comes up the lane at Fetter Bend. So the gate is open. And above that gate there is a cut with a sluice in it, and the sluice is the only thing keeping the main from standing in that lane, and the wheel is on the tail of it."

"Hanna opens it."

"Hanna opens it twice a day and she has opened it twice a day for twenty-six years," said Neve Searle, "and she does not open it for the mill. **She has never opened it for the mill. She opens it because the fields below have to be emptied or they go under, and since the twelfth what she empties it into belongs to the district.** And every time she opens it the wheel loses what it had that morning, and she knows it to the inch, and she has never once said so out loud, and neither have I, and you have been in this yard nine minutes and you are the first person to look at the wheel at all."

---

He had not. That was true, and it took him about four seconds to work out why, and the reason was that there is a thing a count is for and a wheel is not one of the four columns, and he has spent a month looking at columns, and a yard is not a column.

Ivor Searle came out of the mill at about half past nine. He is fifty-one and he said nothing for two days in the second week of the month and has said almost nothing since, and on this Monday he said four things and three of them were about bearings.

"The upper bearing wants oil and it has wanted oil since the spring," he said. "That is not the wheel. I want that said. I am not letting the wheel be the bearing."

Nobody said anything.

"The wheel is the water and the water is the count and the count is the twelfth of this month," said Ivor Searle. "I have been in this mill since I was nine. I want to say one thing to a man from the district office and then I am going back inside, and it is not about the mill."

"Say it."

"There was a room at the Glass Stair with high windows in it on the twenty-fourth of the month," said Ivor Searle. "There is a notice of it on the door of this mill and it is in the paper, and the girl at the slip read it out to about nine people and then took the notice down again, which she should not have done, and I am not going to say that to her. **So the one that is not on the count got a room.** Hark Holm. Four households off the count in the year four hundred and eighty-four, and a warden made it, and a woman of seventy-nine came down the river in a boat to stand in the room, and I am told she asked for a figure and got it."

"That is right."

"And we are on the count," said Ivor Searle. "We are on the count and nobody has ever come up this reach to ask me anything, and I have been on a count sheet since the year four hundred and eighty-six. I am not saying that to be hard. I am saying it because a man in a field has worked out that the column everybody has been reading is the wrong column, and if the column everybody is reading is the wrong column, then the whole of this season is about water. And the water is the only thing that has ever come off this bend, and it came off on the twelfth of the month."

He went back inside and shut the door at a speed that was not meant to be unfriendly.

---

Hanna Roe was at the head of the cut at about eleven. She is fifty-four and she is the only person on that bend that anybody in this county has ever heard of, and she has been offered a place on a return twice and has taken neither.

She was standing in two inches of water on the sill of a stone structure with a plank across it and a handle on a spindle, in a coat mended in four places, doing what she has done twice a day since she was twenty-eight.

"You will want to ask me a thing," she said, before he had got to the top of the cut. "I have had three people from a count on this bend in nine years and two of them were the same man and the third was a girl with a slate, and every one of them asked me a question, and you have been here nine minutes and you have not asked me anything."

"I have not."

"Then I will tell you what you would have asked, because you are not going to get it now and I would rather you had it than had it in a week in a building. **You would have asked whether I want to be on the count.** And I want to be on the count. I have wanted to be on the count since I was twenty-eight, and the answer is yes, and you may write it down, and it is worth nothing at all, and you knew that before you came up the river, and I knew it before the boat turned at the slip, and that is the reason you have not asked me, and you did not not ask me out of manners."

"Then why did I not ask you."

"Because I have answered that question once already," said Hanna Roe, "and it was in a hall with nine people in it, and two of them were the right ones, and the answer went on a piece of paper, and the paper had a column on the other side of it, and in nine years that column has cost me nothing because there is no name in it. **If you ask me here, and I say yes, and you write it down, then my name is on a thing, and I will be the reason a settlement stands or falls, and I am fifty-four, and there is a man in that mill who cannot tell anybody that he is in it.** And I would rather the file said nobody was keeping anything for him. That is the whole of it. I have said it twice and I am not saying it a third."

"I am not going to ask you."

"I know you are not," said Hanna Roe. "I have been watching you not ask me since you got off the boat, and it is the first good thing anybody from a building has done on this bend in my lifetime, and it does not help me, and you knew that when you got off the boat. I am going to say the rest of it anyway, because you are here, and you are the first one who has ever come up here twice and come back."

---

The rest of it took about four minutes and it was this.

**The thirtieth day from the suspension was the twentieth day of the first month**, and it went past fifteen days ago while the wheel was already down, and nobody came and told her, and nobody was required to.

"You have known that since you were on the water on the nineteenth of the month," said Hanna Roe, "and you did not come and tell me, and I want to say that I would rather you had not. **There is nothing anybody can do with the thirtieth day. It is not a door. It is a wall, and everybody in this county has known what it was for and has gone on living behind it, and if you had come up on the twentieth and told me I would have sat on that step and waited for something to arrive, and nothing arrives, and I would have been one day less able to open a sluice at six in the morning.** So you did the right thing, and nobody is going to thank you for it, and I am telling you now so that nobody has to tell you later."

She put the handle back on the spindle and wiped her hands on her coat.

"Now the mill. You are going to tell me that a work not maintained is one of the three things this count is. I know that. Hanna Roe has known it since she was twenty-eight. I want you to say it out loud and say it looking at me, and then I want you to go away, and I want you not to write my name on anything."

He said it out loud. He said it looking at her. She nodded once, which was the whole of it and was not cold.

"And then go and look at the bearing, because Ivor will not say a word about it to anybody, and he said it to you, and a man of fifty-one who says three things out of four to a stranger is telling you something he cannot put in a return."

---

He looked at the bearing. It wanted oil and it had wanted oil since the spring, and he said so out loud in the yard so that Ivor Searle could hear it through a shut door, and Ivor Searle did not open the door and said *that is all right* through it, which is the first sentence the two brothers have exchanged in nineteen days.

The man of sixty-eight was in the chair by the window with a blanket over his knees, four questions a day and two answers, and the window is the one that sees the cut. Owen Rusk had been in that room twice and had looked at him twice and had not asked about him, because there is no column for a man in a chair, and the only person entitled to write one is his daughter, and she has entered **none** in capitals and would like it on a public file more than she would like the water.

The man was looking at the water in the cut and Owen Rusk was the only other person in the room looking at the water in the cut, and the man said one word.

"Down," he said.

That was all he said, and it was correct, and Owen Rusk put his hand in his coat pocket and wrote it on the back of his own hand in pencil because there was nothing to write it on, and then did not know what to do with a piece of hand, and put the hand back.

---

The factor came up at four o'clock in the afternoon to take the mill's paper away. He is **Emmet Traill** and he is fifty-eight and he has ground at that mill since the Concord Year 476 and at four other mills since, and he is a small careful man with a book of weights in it, and he is not going to be a villain in this or in anything else.

He gave Owen Rusk the paper. The paper is a mill's quarterly return. It is current, and it is dated in the month before, and it is the paper any reconciliation of this settlement rests upon so far as anybody in this county can establish, and Owen Rusk asked about it because he is permitted to ask about it and because it was the only thing left that his card would let him do.

"Then the paper is right," he said.

"The paper is entirely right," said Emmet Traill. "The paper is the only entirely correct document in this valley and it has cost me the mill."

"You told the district office there was no wheel."

"I told the district office there is no wheel because there is no wheel," said the factor, "and I would have said it whatever the water had done, and the water has nothing to do with it. I said it because a mill that is not turning is a mill that is not turning, and there is no use in this valley in a person writing that the wheel is stopped for a reason that stopped in the water. **I am not a man who writes reasons. I write weights.**"

"Would you say that in a room."

"I will say anything you like in a room," said Emmet Traill, "and I have said it in nine of them this year, and I am the ninth reason in every one, and nobody has ever printed a ninth reason anywhere."

Then Owen Rusk asked the question he had been carrying up the river for a fortnight, which was a bad question, and he asked it because he tells people the true version before the comfortable one and has been telling them that since the second week of the month of rain.

"The four tons of meal you put into the granary at Lower Lock in the first week of this month, against a paper that is not money," he said. "That was this mill."

Emmet Traill looked at the river for a while.

"**Yes,**" he said. "And I have been telling the people in that mill about it for nine years, which is a different thing from telling the people at Fetter Lane, and I have never asked them for anything and I am not going to start, and I would like it understood that I put four tons in because a girl of about twenty-four came to this mill on a cart and shouted at me for nine minutes. Not because anybody in Fetter Lane asked me to."

"Who was on the cart."

"Her," said the factor. "I would have given you her name but I have a factor's habit and I do not name a person in a yard, and she would not want it. She is the only person in this county who has ever shouted at me for nine minutes and got four tons out of me, and that is the whole of what a factor is for."

Neve Searle walked him down to the boards and said one thing at the gate of the yard, and she said it flatly, and it was not unkind.

"You have been here three hours and you have not asked us one question and I want you to know what that is worth. **It is worth exactly nothing, and it is worth it every week, and there is no form in the county that will let anybody pay you for it, and in about four years somebody in Concord Hall is going to look at what a Continuance Examiner did this season and it is not going to be this.** You do not need me to tell you that. I am telling you because you are going up on Wednesday and there is no reason on this earth that you should."

"Then I will come on a Wednesday."

"**You will not come on a Wednesday,**" said Neve Searle, "because I did not ask you, and a man who is not asked and comes is a man who has made a duty out of somebody's Wednesday, and I have a man in a chair and I have not got room for a man from a building being faithful at me. Go and do your tray."

---

He did the tray on the Monday evening and he was on it at ten past seven and it took him until eight.

On the Tuesday morning of the sixth, the second desk sent a sheet up to him with a docket on it and a line for his hand, and the line was the fourth column of the quarterly works return, and the fourth column is headed **PERSON RESPONSIBLE FOR THE WORK**, and it may not be left blank, and beneath it, in the ordinary form's voice, *if there is no such person, enter none.*

**NETHER SILL. MILL. WORKS MAINTAINED: NO. PERSON RESPONSIBLE: —**

He read it and read it again and then went and got the return of premises of Lower Lock out of the record of the district returns and read the eighth line of the ward's answer to the Count, which is in Ada Rusk's capitals and which he has read about nine times this season and has never once thought about as a form.

*There is no person in this district who keeps anything for anybody, the word for that is not written beside any name in this hall, nobody is to be asked, and the cost of it is five hours a week and that figure is a figure and not a person.*

Nobody had thought of that line in this building as a form. It had been read as a ward's answer, twice, by two people, including him, and it is a form, and it is the answer, and the office has had it in its own record for nine weeks.

**He wrote NONE in capitals and signed it, and it took him about four seconds, and then he sat at the end of the second desk and found that he had done a thing he cannot undo, and worked out what it was in about nine minutes.**

It is this. The district has a form, and the form has a column, and the column has been filled in by the district's own officer of record with *none* in capitals on a mill two hours up a river, and it is dated today, and it is on a file. In about four years somebody in a building is going to ask the district office for the reasoning behind a form, and the district is going to produce a line in the district's own hand that says there is no person, and it is going to be the district's own document, and not a ward's, and the ward's eighth line will be a ward's opinion.

**He has just given the district the ward's answer, in the district's hand, with his name at the bottom of it, and he did it in four seconds because he knew the answer, and he knew the answer because a woman of fifty-four said it out loud in a hall in April in front of thirty people and he was in the room.** There is a difference between a ward that answers a form and a district that answers a form, and the difference is that the district's answer binds the district, and in about four years somebody is going to want to know how the district of Docketford fills in a column that wants a name, and the answer is going to be in his hand.

He did not put a note on it. He thought about putting a note on it for about two minutes, and a note would have said *this is the entry of the district and not an act of the district*, and a note on a quarterly works return is a document about a document, and there are forty applicants to this office and nobody has ever read a note.

---

He went up the reach on the Wednesday morning of the seventh because he said on Saturday that he would not and he is a man who says what kind of question he is being asked after he has answered it, and this one he got right.

**He was on the sill of the sluice at ten to six in the morning on the seventh of the second month of the Concord Year 488, in the dark, in a coat, in two inches of water, and Hanna Roe came round the corner of the cut at six o'clock with a lamp and a stick and stopped about four feet off him, and neither of them said anything at all for about a minute and a half.**

"You have not been up here at six in the morning in your life," she said.

"No."

"It is nothing at six," said Hanna Roe. "That is what I want to say to you and I have got about four minutes before this is work. It is nothing at six. **I have done this twice a day for twenty-six years and there is nothing at six and there is nothing about it that anybody would want to see, and I would like that said out loud by somebody from a building, once, in the only county where a person can be sent to a place by a form.**"

Then she opened it, and he stood on the sill for an hour and ten minutes and watched the main fill and the wheel stand in two inches of water, and it did not turn, and nothing happened, and nothing was going to happen on the seventh of the month or on any morning in that month, and at about eight o'clock she shut it and wiped the spindle and said:

"Right."

"Right," said Owen Rusk.

She looked at the lamp and then at him.

"**Tuesday,**" she said. "I open it at ten to six and again at half past two. Tuesdays and Fridays, and the other days when the fields are under. If you come on a Tuesday I will not say anything about it, and if you come on a Tuesday for a whole season I will not say anything about that either, and in about four years you will stop, and that is the correct way round and I have watched men stop at things for twenty-six years.**"

**It is Tuesdays and Fridays,** she said, and so it is Tuesdays and Fridays, beginning with the Friday, and it is five hours a week with a boat, and the boat costs, and the crew's wage is four months in arrears and is a written condition of a roster that nobody funds, and there is a woman in Fetter Lane who told a hall in April that five hours a week is five hours a week and not five hours on a Thursday, and that the hours of the person who was there do not stop.

**It is the first thing he has done in this season that nobody asked for. It is not a duty, it is not a hold, it is not on a card, and it is the only one of the three that he can afford.**

Prue Hallam brought him down at about eleven on the Wednesday and put him on the boards at the Fetter Lane slip at half past and said one thing on the way, which was that at the head of the Wexford Landing slip a woman of seventy-nine had been asking at a post whether anybody from the count had been up the reach, and had been told, twice, no.

He got back to Fetter Lane at about one and did the tray, and was on it at a quarter past six, and there was nothing on it from the reach and nothing on it from the Water Office, because the only thing coming for him in this county had been entered on the Friday of the second and stamped on the Friday of the second and is to be delivered on the sixteenth, and the season is certified on the ninth, and the schedule for the season of 489 is printed on the twenty-eighth of next month, and the eleven households of a silted bend are not going to be on it, and nobody up that reach is anybody's matter, and he is going to be there on Tuesday morning at ten to six for about four minutes of it.
