# Chapter 4 — Arbour Kitchen

Arbour Kitchen had been a bakery that had failed and then had been something else, and the something else was a front room with four tables, a stove, a kettle going all day, and a rule that anybody could sit down if they were hungry and anybody could speak if they wanted a witness. On a Thursday afternoon in the second week of the rain season there were nine people in it, and three of them had come upriver from Lower Lock in the last two days and were sitting with their coats still on, and Owen knew them all.

Nell Arbour came out of the back with a ledger under her arm and her left hand held slightly away from her body in the way she had held it for six years, since a redaction case in Docketford had left the nerves in it unreliable and a bad operation had left the feeling worse. She was thirty-five. She had been an examiner in the building Owen worked in, four desks below his own, and she had left it two years ago, and the last words either of them had spoken were spoken in a hearing room, and they had both spent a certain amount of the intervening time being extremely busy.

"You've come about the annex," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you'll want to sit down, and you'll want to not do what you're about to do, and I'd rather tell you the second thing first so that the first one lands better." She put the ledger on the table. "You're here to tell me to stop representing Lower Lock."

"Yes."

"I won't."

"Three families—"

"Are in my kitchen because they walked to the annex on Tuesday morning and sat in a queue for six hours on the strength of a letter nobody had explained to them, and the counter clerk told them the ward's claims were being consolidated, and one of them asked what that meant, and the clerk said it meant they should come back after the next review with their papers." Nell sat down across the table from him. "I did not go and get them. They came here. And the Tuesday letter is eleven days old, and I have had somebody at this table every day since the morning it came in, and I did not need the Registry's permission to sit down with a person who is frightened."

Owen put the oiled paper on the table.

"Then tell me what to do with this."

She did not touch it. She looked at the corner of it and then she said: "Is that both leaves?"

"Yes."

"Bring the other one out."

He had the second leaf in his coat and he brought it out and laid the two of them side by side on a table in a room with nine people in it, which is not where he would have put them if he had been thinking, and which turned out to be the only place they were ever going to do any work.

Nell looked at the first leaf. She read it fast and completely, the way people read things they already know. Then she moved her hand to the second leaf and slowed down a great deal.

"Which of these have you been arguing about for a week?"

"The dates on the first one. It says the crossing moved on the ninth. The receipt was stamped on the eleventh. Two days before anybody could have handed it in."

"That's right. That's your two days." She did not look up. "Now Rusk. Read me the heading of that second leaf."

He had read it, twice, in the small hours, in a front room, standing up. "It says no local record at the named place."

"It says *at the named place*." She turned the leaf so that it faced him, and put her finger on the line under the heading, where there was a single named place, and the place was the crossing. "Not at the ward. Not in the district. At the Ninth Weir. Thirteen people, one bridge, and the Office is asserting as a positive, witnessed, sealed, received fact that there is no record of any of them at that bridge."

"And we can produce a record—"

"You can produce a record of the ward, and a roll, and a habitation, and a woman with a good eye who will stand up in a room and say the name out loud. None of it is the document. None of it answers it." She sat back. "Rusk, you have spent a week trying to prove that thirteen people existed. And they exist. Kray would tell you they exist, and the six that can't answer would be counted by somebody tonight. Existing is not the question and you cannot win it, because the Office has already decided there is nobody at the place to be counted, and a ward that answers a claim about a place by saying *we live here* has just agreed that the place is empty."

The room was very quiet. Owen looked at the second leaf and understood, with the specific helplessness of a man watching his own case turn in a direction he had not permitted it to, that she was right, and that she was right about the document and not about anything else, and that both of those things were true at once.

"So what," he said. "What is the claim? Strip it down. What is the Office actually saying?"

"That there is no supported district at the crossing." She said it flatly. "That is the whole instrument, underneath the transfer, underneath both leaves. It is not saying the ward has gone. It is not saying the people are dead or moved or never were. It is saying that at a certain piece of river masonry, no one is performing any duty, and that therefore the duty moves to a return that does perform it. And they are right, Rusk. They are right on the narrow claim, and that is the whole difficulty, and you will not find a document in this Office that says anything different because there isn't one."

"Then the ward can answer the narrow claim—"

"The ward can perform the duty." She said it before he finished, which told him she had been waiting two hours for somebody to say anything else. "That is the only answer to a claim about an act. Not a counter-claim. An act. The ward maintains the crossing — here is the marker crew, here is the date the marker was set, here are the two people who stood in the rain and watched it get set — and we say so out loud, and then somebody who is not us stands up and says *I saw that*, and then we do it again in seven days, and then somebody says it again in seven days after that."

She stopped, and then she went on, and Owen understood that the two hours had not started yet.

"That is what a return is. It is not a claim. A claim is a thing you say about the past and it goes stale. A return is a thing you do this week, in front of two people who do not get anything out of it, so that the thing you did this week is still standing next week. Do you understand the difference? Because the Office understands the difference, and that is why it is frightened of the second one."

---

Somewhere in the middle of it the room filled up and emptied and filled up again, and Owen sat at the fourth table with his coat on and did not say a word for two hours, and at some point a man he knew sat down opposite him and told him about a slate roof and then left, and at some point a child came through the back and was fed, and at no point did anybody look at the two leaves on his table again except Nell.

"Three times," she was saying. "I have seen consolidation three times before. Once on the Upland fringe, where two terraces folded their seed stores into one and the larger terrace spent the first winter being extremely glad about it. Once along the Claim Market, where a broker sold me, for four shillings, the right to say that a person he had never met was represented. And once in a case not like that, where it was honestly the only thing that would have kept a clinic open."

"And every time it was lawful."

"Every time it was lawful. That is the part you keep missing." She put her tea down. "It is not a trick. It is a tool, and it is a good tool, and it is used well and it is used badly, and the Registry is not the villain in this, it is a machine that has one way of joining two places together and it has been running that way since before either of us was at a desk. What it has never once had to do is ask a ward what it wanted, because a ward is not a counter, and you cannot put a ward on a form."

"Mill Ward got the crossing," Owen said. "Mill Ward's return has no crew and no funds and no way to reach the weir. I have read their entry. There are four men on the whole district works list and two of them are over sixty and one of those two is the district's own clerk."

"Then it is a bad tool used badly, and I have been telling you what the tool is, and you have been hearing me describe a villain." She looked at him steadily. "What do you want me to do with these families on Tuesday?"

"Stop. You can't represent them. You've never met the ward. You haven't been asked."

"I know."

"You've been telling them what the transfer means."

"I have been telling them what the *letter* means, which is a different thing, and I have been telling them what their options are, and I have been telling them the truth, which is that no one in that hall is going to come to them. If you want me to stop, I will stop today and I will give you the three families and you will do what you are going to do with them, and Rusk—" She stopped. "—no. Not that. I'm not doing that either. I'm not going to be your witness."

"Nell—"

"Listen to the reason, because the reason is the only interesting part." She put both hands flat on the table, and the left one lay there like something that had been put down and abandoned. "An advocate who takes one ward's case is that ward's advocate. After that I am a person other people cannot use. There are people in this room from four different places and two of them are here because the woman who went to the annex last month was told, kindly, that she was not the sort of person who could be represented by somebody. And you know exactly what I mean, because you were the copy clerk of record when you wrote down what I was, and you did it in about twenty minutes, and you did it correctly."

The room kept going. The kettle. A chair. Someone laughing two tables away.

"You want somebody to lean on," Nell said. "You've had a week of this Office telling you everything is fine and you have come here to be told you are right, and you will not be told you are right, and when you don't get it you will look for a person who will say it. I am not going to be that person. I have my own cases, and my own refusals, and a woman in Coal Hill I am losing because I cannot afford to be a person who says what she wants to hear."

"I never asked you to say it."

"You asked me to be a witness. That is asking me to say it. A witness is somebody whose word is put in a document, and if my name is on Lower Lock's file then next year when a clerk reads it he will read a claim advocate and not a witness, and that is how advocates stop being usable, and it happens one small reasonable favour at a time and never feels like the day it happened." She picked her tea up and drank it. "Also I have been in this room for a day and a half waiting to see whether you would come and what you would want. You came and you wanted me to stop. So the answer is no, and I am not cross, and you may sit down again whenever you like."

He did not apologise. He had the apology right there, assembled, the whole sentence of it, and he did not say it, and they both knew why: because an apology is the cheapest thing he owned and it costs her nothing and it would have been a way of making himself easier to be around.

He sat down.

"You have not asked me one question about the ward," she said, after a while. "In two hours. You have sat in my kitchen and asked me to stop and to stop and to be a witness and you have not asked me one question about the ward."

"I came with an answer. I found out on the way here that I did not have a question."

"That's a first." She refilled his cup without asking, which was the first kind thing she did all evening and which she ruined by doing it in silence. "Ask the real one. You have about a cup of time before Kray comes in and starts on the roof again."

---

"What would the ward have to have," Owen said, "in order to file at all?"

Nell put her cup down.

"Say that again, and mean it."

"What would the ward have to have in order to file at all. Not to win. To file."

"That," she said, "is the first question anybody has asked me about a consolidation in six years, and I am going to answer it properly, and then I am going to tell you the part that will make your week difficult."

And she answered it for the better part of an hour, in the plain voice she used when she was not advocating for anybody and simply telling him what the law was, and he wrote it all down in the notebook on his knee because he could not help himself, and by the end of it he had a thing he had not had at seven o'clock that morning.

"First thing," she said. "A return is not a complaint. It has to say what it is, narrowly, so narrowly that nobody can hide inside it."

"How narrowly."

"Narrowly enough that you would be ashamed if it turned out to be more than you meant." She held up a finger for each thing after that, and the kettle went and nobody looked. "An act that was performed, and the date of it, and two living people who saw the act and are not carrying the return themselves. How often it comes back for renewal. Where a person goes to complain about it. And every adult in it is in it separately, and can come out of it separately, and nobody's coming out can break the rest."

"And the subject line?"

"Don't ask me about the subject line. You will ask me about the subject line every day for a fortnight and I have already had that argument with six people who are not you."

"That last one," he said. "If one household comes out, what happens?"

"Then the return is one household smaller and it goes on." She said it without emphasis. "That is the entire point of it, and it is the reason nobody has ever been able to do this to a ward on purpose, because you cannot get at a ward through one of its households. If you build it so that one household can pull it down, then the person who wants to leave a bad thing cannot leave, and you have just built a ward where the refusal is the thing that hurts most, and you will have done the Office's work for it."

Owen wrote that down too, in the middle of the page, with a star against it, and did not for a moment notice that he had also written down that he did not have a name to give a person in his own ward who could not be reached by anybody.

He stayed until nine. Kray came in at half past and talked about slate for a quarter of an hour. The three families from Lower Lock were still there, and one of them was asleep sitting up, and Nell had put a blanket over her without making a thing of it, and Owen got up and put his coat on and stood in the doorway for a second looking at the two leaves lying side by side on the table, the dated one and the other one, which had between them taken three duties out of a ward and put thirteen names against a bridge, and which had cost him a week of the only kind of fight he knew how to have.

He had come downriver to be told he was right. He had been told, instead, in considerable detail, what it would take to be a different kind of right, and he had a page of it, and the page was not his.

He went upriver in the rain with the page in his notebook and one question left, and it was not a question for an advocate, because an advocate answers what people ought to do and he needed the shape of what had been *done to* them, eleven months back, in the ordinary way.

The transfer had been entered as a receipt. Receipts arrive. Somebody had sent something to the Mill Ward annex counter, and the thing they had sent had carried a signature and two witnesses and a seal, and the Office had received it and stamped it, and eleven months later the crossing belonged to a district that could not reach it.

Somebody had sent it. Which meant somebody had a copy, and had decided, and had chosen the date, and had been willing to be the kind of person who chose the date.

The only person in Docketford he had ever met who kept a hand record of what actually arrived in this ward was sixty-one years old, had a brass weight on her ledger, and had never once in his life been asked what was in it.

Owen walked faster.
