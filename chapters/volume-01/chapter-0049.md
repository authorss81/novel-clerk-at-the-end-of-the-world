# Chapter 49 — Marta Rusk

The Redaction Register is two floors down and it is a register of the sittings and not of the things, and Owen Rusk had been down to look at it twice in seven weeks and had learned one thing about it that is worth the trip, which is that it is kept in an ordinary book with a cloth binding and that a person can sit in front of it for a whole afternoon and be the only person in the room and not once be interrupted.

He went down on the Saturday morning of the sixty-eighth day of the month with a request, and the request was in the correct form, and he had written it out longhand and read it back to himself twice on the way because a request is a thing and being a line in a request register is a cost, and he had two of them already.

*A batch may be produced to a person named in one of its entries, alone, once, with a clerk at the door, and a production note is kept.*

That is the rule. It is not a favour and it is not a discretion and it is not a favour with a discretion in it. A batch is struck in a sitting by one officer, and the entries are a person's whole life reduced to a queue, and the procedure is that a person named in an entry may come and see the batch and nobody else may, and only once, and a clerk stands at the door the whole time, and a production note is written, and the note is kept.

Owen Rusk is the only living person named in Redaction Batch 471/Q4/19. Entry nine. He has known that since the fourth week of the month and he has told nobody in this city, and he told the clerk at the desk on the ground floor on the Saturday morning at about ten past ten, and the clerk read the request, and looked at him, and said:

"You will be in the production room. It is the small one at the end. I will be at the door the whole time and I will not be in the room, and that is the procedure, and it is not a courtesy to you, it is the procedure because in the Concord Year 4 a man's wife was made to stand outside a room while her own entry was read to a stranger."

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"The batch will come up from the strong room. It will be the whole batch and not an extract, because an extract of a batch is not the batch and anybody who has been given one knows it. It is forty-one entries. They are in the order the officer went down the queue and not in the order of the numbers, and the schedule is numbered one to forty-one by line and not by entry, and if you have been told that entry nine is the first row that is because it is the first row and it is not because it is the first."

---

The production room is eight feet by ten and has a table in it and no window, and the batch came up in a grey board folder with a label on the spine, and a clerk stood in the doorway for the whole of it, and she stood there for about an hour and ten minutes and she did not read anything and she did not go out.

Owen Rusk did not open the folder for about two minutes.

He had spent seven weeks carrying the number of it in his head — 471/Q4/19, struck on the twelfth of the fourth quarter of the Concord Year 471, one sitting, one officer, initials H.L.P., forty-one entries, ground *revision of residence status, flood quarter arrears* — and he had a line in his own register with the whole of it, and he had three of the other entry numbers in his own hand from off route lists inside nine years, and he had worked out in the second week that he was a mechanism in somebody else's deliberate act.

He opened it.

Entry nine.

*Residence status, Fetter Lane, Lower Lock. Revision on flood-quarter arrears, sitting of the twelfth. Pending: yes. Interval: eleven days. Priority medicine: delayed. Presenter: O. Rusk, of Fetter Lane; relationship: son; claimant informed.*

*Claimant informed.* Owen Rusk had been in a hall in the third week of the month ask what that meant, and Pell had told him, and it means that a boy of sixteen was present when the form was presented, and that the form said he was present, and that nobody asked him anything.

He read it four times. He did not touch it. He wrote nothing on a sheet of paper, because the instruction on the wall of that room is a single line in a clerk's hand and it says: *Nothing taken out. Nothing written in this room. Nothing copied out of this room except by the clerk and only for a production note.*

And then he said the thing he had come down here to find out whether he could say, which was the question of what an amendment is.

"May I ask the office for an amendment to the interval and to the consequence."

"You may ask. You cannot have it. There is no form on which a person may have it, and I am not being clever, that is simply true." The clerk said it from the doorway. "What an amendment is, and I will tell you because you have come a long way and you are entitled to know what you have come for. An amendment is a new entry, made from a stated time forward. **It erases nothing.** The original entry stays. The batch stays. The schedule stays. The initials stay. The date stays. Everything anybody ever did stays, and the amendment is added on top of it, and the addition is the only part that is new, and the addition is made now, and it says now, and that is the whole of what now is available to anybody in this city for a delay of fifteen years."

"I know what an amendment is."

"You know what an amendment is in a case you have read about. That is not the same, and I am not being unkind, I have watched forty of these and I have never once had anybody arrive knowing what an amendment is." She turned a page of her own book. "What do you want the amendment to say."

Owen Rusk had known what he wanted the amendment to say for about a month and he had never written it down, because writing it down would have been describing it, and describing it is the thing he has been caught at twice.

"I want a sentence that says that a woman who could not get upriver in the wet was not at an address for eleven days, and that her priority medicine was delayed, and that her burial was disputed for eight months."

"That is four sentences."

"Three," said Owen Rusk. "And a fourth, which is that no part of the eleven days is returned and no part of the eight months is returned, and that nothing in it says otherwise."

The clerk wrote for about a minute and a half.

"That is an amendment that will be made in about four minutes and that nobody will contest, and I want you to understand both of those things at once, because you are the first person who has ever understood them at once and I have been doing this for twenty-two years." She put the pen down. "The batch has never been disputed. The initials have never been disputed. The date has never been disputed. Nobody in this office has ever said that the interval on row nine was anything other than what it is. And the amendment will not be contested either, and it will not be contested because **conceding a delay costs nothing once the delay is over.** That is not kindness. It is arithmetic. There is nobody left alive to be harmed by it and no sum to be paid and no case to be brought, and the office has been waiting fifteen years for somebody to ask, and nobody has ever asked, and that is not a conspiracy either, it is just what happens to a queue when nobody comes."

"And the delay did not cause the fever."

"No. It was not intended to and the interval was the sitting and not her row and she was one of forty-one and every one of the forty-one was on the same interval, and that is the discovery, and you have had it for a month." The clerk did not soften it. "Your mother died of a fever. The interval delayed her priority medicine. The interval is why her burial was disputed for eight months, which is a longer figure and a different figure and the two are not the same thing and they are not swapped. Nothing comes back. There is no medicine. There is no time. There is no woman at Fetter Lane. The amendment will say what happened and it will not give you one hour of it and it was never going to, and the only thing in it that is worth anything to you is that it will be true, and on a page, and it will name the office, and it will name the batch."

---

They made it at about half past twelve.

The entry is short. It is a new line in a column, with a day and an hour and a form of amendment and a reference to the batch and the entry, and it reads:

*Amended this day. In Redaction Batch 471/Q4/19, entry nine, the residence status of Marta Rusk, of Fetter Lane, in this district, was pending revision for eleven days in the fourth quarter of the Concord Year 471. While it was pending she was a person not at an address. The consequence was that her priority medicine was delayed. Her burial was disputed for eight months. This amendment is made in the Concord Year 487, at the hour stated below, by the district office named as the office which held the pending entry, and it names the batch in which the pending entry was struck. No part of the eleven days is returned. No part of the eight months is returned. Nothing in this amendment returns, restores, or claims to return any part of either.*

And then the last line, which is the one Owen Rusk spent about ninety seconds on a Saturday morning in a room eight feet by ten with no window in it, and which is the only line in that whole column that he asked for.

"The record of this residence status is corrected now. It was not corrected then."

The clerk stopped writing.

"That is not customary."

"Is it untrue."

She looked at him for about four seconds. "No."

"Then put it in."

"I want to say why I am hesitating, because you have been honest with me for ninety minutes and I am not going to be dishonest at the end of it. It is not customary because a sentence of that shape is a statement about this office, and a statement about this office on a form of amendment is read by whoever holds the file next, and whoever holds the file next will be in about four years, and they will read *the record is corrected now and it was not corrected then* and they will read it as a complaint by a party." She held the pen. "That is the whole of my hesitation. It is not that it is untrue."

"Then it is a complaint by a party, and the party is the person named in the entry, and the person named in the entry is me, and the complaint is about the interval, which is the matter of the amendment in any case."

The clerk wrote it in, in the ordinary hand, and it went into the column under the other six lines, and it is the last line of the amendment of the residence status of Marta Rusk of Fetter Lane, made in the Concord Year 487, and it is in a clerk's hand and not in his.

---

He did not sleep much on the Saturday night and he did not go and look at anything, and on the Sunday morning he did the one piece of arithmetic that had been sitting on him since the second week of the month and that he had deliberately not done.

Forty-one entries. One sitting. One officer.

Three of the other forty numbers are numbers he handled inside nine years, off Fourth River Desk route lists: two quarterly roll notices going to a public counter, and one form 12-B carried against the same entry number to a sub-table counter in the week of the eleventh of the Concord Year 471, which is not one of the forty-one and is not about a residence status.

**And the other forty people in that batch have not been asked.**

Not one of them has been asked and not one of them can be made to ask, because the procedure is a person named in an entry, and a person who does not know that they are named in an entry cannot apply to see it, and how many of the forty are alive is not on the schedule and is not in the batch and is not in the Redaction Register, because a residence status is a record of where a person is and not of whether they are, and this city has never once had to answer the second question in the same register as the first.

Three of the forty he knows, and the three are numbers he handled inside nine years off Fourth River Desk route lists, and the rest of the forty is a column of figures in a grey board folder in a strong room two floors below where he is standing, and there is no column in this city for the rest of them and there never has been.

And the Registry built all of that honestly, on purpose, with reasons, and the reasons are sound, and the result is that a queue of forty-one people was cleared in an afternoon in the fourth quarter of the Concord Year 471 by a careful man with a form and a pen, and it will stay cleared, and there is no instrument in this city that opens it again.

And his own name is on the production note, where it has been since Saturday, and it is the prominence of the production note, and a production note records who was produced to and when and by which clerk. It is not a small thing and it cannot be un-named, and it is going to be in a register in this building for as long as the building stands, and there is no instrument that removes a name from a production note.

And his name is on row nine of the batch, and it is on row nine because the officer went down the queue in that order, and that is the whole of it, and there is no more to it than that, and it is the single most comforting thing he found in the record room on the Sunday morning and it is not a comfort at all.

---

They answered the four pages in the ward hall on the Sunday evening.

It was not a meeting. There was a stove and about fourteen people and the delivery book open on the long table, and Marguerite Bray had the pencil, and Ada Rusk had the slate and was at the end of the table and did not sit down once.

Mr Tillet's was already written. That was the first one, and Bray read it back in her own voice, the refusal and the reason and the date, and about nine people in the room said nothing at all while she did it, and she ruled it and the room accepted it and nobody was glad.

Holm Ferris wrote his at about seven o'clock.

He is fifty-eight and keeps the night watch in this ward and he asked the correct question in a hall in the third week of the month — *what is the duty* — and Ada Rusk told him in front of twenty-two people that it was the part she had not got, and on Friday she had given the ward six named people with one thing each that one of them could do alone, and he had sat at a wall and said *that is the duty then, that is what the four of us would have to say*, and she had said yes.

His page said:

*I will take the copy. Wil Pomfret has carried that board up that path in the wet for six weeks and nobody has ever asked him to and I have watched him do it from a window every Thursday. I will do the copy. One person, at the weir path, on the day the count is written up, hung where anybody coming down can read it, and brought in again in the wet. It is one thing and I can do it alone and I will complain in pencil in Mrs Bray's book if it cannot be done.*

"That is a yes," said Ada Rusk, "to a duty, and not to a branch, and a branch is a different thing and it is still not ready and I have not asked the office to name anybody."

"Rule it exactly," said Holm Ferris, and Marguerite Bray ruled it, and read it back, and put the pencil down, and nobody congratulated him.

Ada Rusk wrote hers at about half past seven, and hers was not a yes and not a no, and it is the shortest of the four.

*I was one of the four who voted no on the seventh day of the month and I voted no because we were going to lose. I have read the bundle. I write on this page for two reasons. The first is that a page with my name on it and nothing on it has been in a document going to a Council four times and I would rather there was a sentence on it. The second is that I do not consent to a separate branch and I record that the district's paper does not claim a seed stall, and a woman of seventy-one has lost forty-one years of one, and I will not have this ward's paper stretched over it to make me feel better, and if anybody tries I will read this page out in whatever room it turns up in.*

Nobody said anything for about half a minute, and then Wenna Sarr said, from the wall:

"That is the second sentence of yours I have had to correct in a month and I am not tired of it yet."

And Wenna Sarr's page was still blank, and she had said why in a hall on the Friday in one sentence out loud, and the bundle went down to the mill in the morning for the fifth time with the same four pages in the same order — one refusal in a cook's hand, one page carrying a duty in a watchman's hand, one page carrying a sentence in a bridge mechanic's capitals that was the opposite of a pardon, and one page blank on purpose, whose owner was standing in the room with her basket at her feet and had said so out loud and had not been chased and was not going to be.

---

He got in at about eleven and he did not do the arithmetic, and he had a very good reason for not doing the arithmetic, which was that he had got the thing.

He had got it. It was on a form of amendment in a column in a book in a building in this city, made at about half past twelve on the Saturday, in a clerk's hand, with his mother's name in it, and the eleven days and the eight months in it, and the office named, and the batch named, and the last line in it, and the sentence at the end about what is not returned.

He sat at his own table with the plate on it and the lamp on and found that he was not crying and was not glad and was not anything, and that what he had wanted for a month was not this.

What he had wanted for a month — and he worked it out standing up, at about a quarter past eleven at night on the sixty-ninth day of the month of a rain season in the Concord Year 487, because standing up is how he thinks now — was a sentence saying that a woman who could not get upriver in the wet was not at an address for eleven days.

And the sentence exists. He has it. It is in a column in a book two floors down and it is the truest thing anybody has written about his mother in fifteen years, and it is on a page, and it names the office, and it names the batch.

And it is a sentence about a delay that has already happened.

She is still dead. She has been dead since the Concord Year 471 and she was thirty-one and the fever was not the delay and the delay was not the fever, and there is no version of this in which a sentence in a column brings back eleven days or eight months or a woman, and the amendment says so itself, in the fourth line, in the plainest clause anybody in this city has ever written, and it says it because a clerk at a door insisted and he asked her whether it was untrue.

Being right in a room about a woman who has been dead for fifteen years is not a consolation and it was never going to be one, and there is nobody to tell that to, and the register in his inside pocket has a page with **Marta Rusk** written at the bottom of it in his own hand for the first time in his life, and there is still nothing under it, and there is going to be nothing under it for the rest of his life, and that is not a wound and it is not a sentence about a document.

It is a woman who could not get upriver in the wet.
