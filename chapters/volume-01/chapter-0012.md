# Chapter 12 — The Answer Is a Date

On the Wednesday morning Owen tried the ordinary ways, one after another, in the order a man tries things when he is competent and has four days.

The first was the countersignature desk, where he took the docket stub and asked whether the return could be put in front of somebody before the quarter closed. The clerk there was about thirty and had a good hand and no interest in him whatsoever, and she looked at the stub, and then at the tray card, and then she said, "That is reviewed by the district office."

"I know it is reviewed by the district office. I'd like to know who reviews it."

"Her."

"Who is her?"

"The Deputy Registrar," said the clerk, "or a person he puts in front of it, and he will not put himself in front of it, and if you want to know why, the reason is that he is the man who will be asked about it in public, and nobody in that building wants to be the man who was asked about a document he had not read." She put a stamp down on a pad and picked it up again, which is what clerks do when they have finished talking. "It goes up. It is read. It comes back or it does not. Nobody here can hurry it and nobody here is punished for it not happening, which is the same as saying it will not happen."

The second was the Glass Stair, where Owen went to look at the docket of his own petition and found, in the ordinary way, that a district return does not appear on a docket at all, because a petition is a request and a return is a thing somebody has, and the two live in different books and are kept by different people who do not speak to each other at lunch.

The third was Nell. He did not go, because he had promised himself on Saturday night at a counter that he would not use her as a route into anything, and because a promise made to yourself at a counter in the rain is the only kind that has ever held for him.

The fourth was Pell, and it took eleven minutes, and it was the answer.

She was on the second desk at twenty past nine with the tray in front of her and the ink drying, and he put the docket stub down on the corner of it, and she read it, and then she read it again, and then she said the thing in a completely flat voice, the way a woman reads out a rule she has never had cause to argue with.

"Rusk. A district return does not go to the Council. Everybody thinks it does. It goes to the district office. The district office is the Office. The Deputy Registrar's office is the one body in Docketford that can act on it, and it is also the one body that has already received the transfer that caused the entire business, and it signed the load restriction, and it is going to be the body that somebody stands up in a room and asks why a ward has been frozen for a fortnight." She put the pen in its cup. "You have filed a return at the only office in this city that has an interest in what it says. And it is not a corrupt interest and it is not even a dishonest one. It is an interested one. Which is the worst kind, because you cannot appeal it."

"There is no alternative."

"There is the Council, and the Council does not take district returns, and the Council has spent the last four days closing the door on the quarter, so that is not an alternative either, it is the same door with a sign on it." She turned the stub around and squared it to the edge of the tray. "You understand what has happened to you. You filed the thing in the right place, which is the only place, and the right place is a room that will be asked about it. Every route you try from here is a route into the same room, and there are more doors into that room than there are out of it, and that is not a conspiracy, it is a building."

Owen sat down on the end of the tray, which he was not supposed to do, and which he did anyway.

"Then what is the answer doing about my petition?"

"Two things, and you will want the second one and you must not have the second one yet." She held up a finger. "First: the district office has a form for putting a document somewhere without deciding anything about it. It is called a receipt, and there are two kinds. A receipt that says the document has been received, and a receipt that says the document has been received and examined. The first kind is free, takes a minute, and commits nobody. The second kind is a thing a person signs."

"And the first kind has been in the file since Tuesday," said Owen.

"The first kind has been in the file since Tuesday, and it is the reason your return cannot be hurried and the reason it cannot be refused, and both of those are the same clerk's discretion exercised in your favour for no reason at all." She put the finger down. "Second thing. I am going to tell you this because you will find it out on Saturday and I would rather you had it tonight and could not do anything with it, which is a phrase I have been using a lot this month and I am aware of it."

"Say it."

"The answer to the petition is being drafted from a template. There is a standard answer for a ward petition that asks a district office to explain a public work, and it is four paragraphs, and it is correct, and it is written by nobody, and the man filling it in is a district clerk who has four of them in front of him this week and a good hand and no view." She looked at him levelly. "And a docket clerk at the Glass Stair may refuse a template as inadequate. It is the correct form. It is free to file. It costs the Office a day and it costs you nothing, and the answer that comes down afterwards is a fresh answer and not that one."

Owen wrote it down. He wrote it down the way he wrote everything down, in a small square hand, in the margin, and then he read back what he had written and understood that he had no possible use for any of it, and said so.

"You have no use for it tonight," Pell agreed. "You will have one use for it on Friday morning, and it will not be the use you want, and I am not going to help you find the use you want, because I have watched you find the use you want twice this month and once of those was a notice with a pointer in it."

---

The ward that night had a job on, and the job was the channel clearance, and the channel clearance is done at night because the mill puts its silt down in the afternoon and lets it settle.

That is the whole reason, and it is the reason a person who has never kept a river learns it in one evening: the crew waits until the water stops bringing new mud, and then somebody goes down into the spillway channel with a pole and a hook and pulls the bed of it back to where the bed of it was, and if they do not do it on a Tuesday they cannot do it at all, because by Friday the water is in the top of the channel and by Saturday it is over the coping.

It had not stopped raining. It had been raining for twenty days in the way that rain stops being weather and becomes a condition of the world, and the river was up two feet on the lower stair, and Owen stood at the top of the weir path with a lamp in his left hand and his right hand inside a woollen glove with a cut under it, and Ada Rusk allocated him, without discussion, to the job of standing on the coping and calling people by name and saying out loud what he could see, which is the job he had done in the dark on the tenth day and which nobody in the ward had ever thought to give him again because it had worked.

That was the part of the evening he had not been ready for. He had been ready for the water and the cold and being frightened, and he had not been ready for being handed a place.

The crew was eight. Ada, Ferris on the bank with the log, Dory Anstruther with his collarbone still strapped and a face that had gone the colour of candle wax and who did not go in the water and did not complain about it and was extremely good at the top path, holding a coil. Ferrow in the channel with the hook. Tillet at the head of the stair with the chalk and the levels. Ilse Mern with the second lamp. And two of the nine who were not on the ward's return at all and had come because the channel was the channel.

Owen called them out. He called the moment Ferrow's boots went under and the moment they came out, and the moment Ada had the pole in and the moment she had the bed of the channel, and he called Dory's name every time he turned, because Dory was standing on a wet path with a bad chest holding a rope he was not allowed to be useful with, and being called was the only thing in Owen's gift that a person on a bank can be given.

They were in for an hour and ten minutes. And at about fifty minutes in, Ferris said a word, and Ada said a word back, and Ferrow's voice came up out of the channel complaining that there was a bar of something across it that had not been there on Friday, and Owen wrote *bar of silt and rubbish, nine feet, across the channel, new since the ninth* in the log because Ferris was watching him write it and because that is the only reason a log exists.

At eight o'clock they came up, and the crew stood about in the rain eating bread out of a sack, and the pump went at four the next morning and would go again on Friday and Saturday, and Owen walked up to the hall and found that nobody had asked him to do anything at all, and that this was the first evening in a month that had gone like that.

---

On the Thursday he asked for the district works inspection roll to be read.

That is a real thing and it is a strange thing to ask for, and the way it is done is that you may not take it away, because it is a public document and the Front Desk holds it in a drawer that anybody may open at any hour the counter is open, and the reading of it is not an act you perform but a thing that happens in a room with other people in it.

He wrote the request in the ordinary form at the counter at half past ten in the morning, and the form has a box on it that says *purpose of request*, and he filled the box in with a sentence, and the sentence was: *to be read against the acts recorded in District Return 487-LL, in public, by any clerk who cares to attend.*

The counter clerk read the box twice.

"Any clerk who cares to attend," she said.

"Yes."

"That is a strange thing to write in that box."

"It is the box," said Owen.

"It is the box," she agreed, and stamped it, and said the reading would be at eleven, and that the roll was a public document and always had been and was in the second drawer and anybody could have read it any time in nine years.

He did not attend.

He went up to the copying floor and sat at the end of a bench on the second desk with a batch that was not his and read a page of somebody else's continuance for an hour and a quarter, and he did not go down, and the reason he did not go down was the reason he gave Pell and the reason was true: the moment he stood in that room the return stopped being a thing nine people had done and became a thing Owen Rusk wanted, and every single person who came to look at the roll afterwards would be looking at a roll that a Registry clerk had asked to be read, and a clerk who has asked to be seen has stopped being a witness and started being an interest.

At half past twelve Pell came up and put a sheet of paper on the corner of the bench and said, "Four lines," and went away again without waiting.

He read them four times.

*31st. Ninth Weir public crossing. District works engineer. Cracked abutment, west side, wire and mark renewed, offset five inches. Weather bad. Work seen in progress on the bank: marker freshly painted, clearance of the channel apparent. No district crew present; two private persons.*

*1st. Fetter Lane pump. District works engineer. Pump out of service. Hand-worked during the quarter; not district work. Noted for the record.*

*4th. Ward granary, Lower Lock. District works engineer. Weights checked against the district schedule. Count consistent. Signatures of two private persons in the margin, not district.*

*9th. Ninth Weir public crossing. District works engineer. Re-measured. Offset five inches, wire intact. No district crew present. Two private persons, one in the water.*

Owen read the last line four times and then had to put the paper face down on the bench, because the Office's own public record, in the Office's own hand, signed with an engineer's name, contains four entries describing four acts performed by the ward in three weeks — and contains the phrase *not district work* in two of them, and *no district crew present* in two of them, and *two private persons* in two of them, and the district engineer wrote those words in a book that has been in an open drawer in the ground floor of this building for nine years.

The ninth of the tenth is in there. The night the river came over the coping, the crossing was in the roll, the wire was on, the measurement was the measurement that Pike's suspension rests on, and beside it a district officer of the Registry of Continuant Works wrote *two private persons* and went home.

He did not go down and thank anybody. He sat on a bench on the second desk with a page of somebody else's return under his hand and a cut on his finger under a woollen glove and understood that he had spent a fortnight learning what a filing was from a woman who had told him a filing was a thing a person does, and that the district engineer had written the same four lines in the same four weeks, and that nobody had ever read them aloud.

At six he found Pell at her tray and said thank you, and she said, without looking up, "The roll is a public document and always was, and it has been in the second drawer since the year my mother died, and I read it because you asked me to and not because it is interesting."

"You wrote the four lines down and gave them to me and you did not say one word about what they mean."

"No," said Pell.

---

He asked the second question of the day at the Front Desk, at about four, and got the answer by being told it, and then watched it happen.

"What happens to a return like mine after the quarter closes?"

"Nothing happens to it. It sits." The counter clerk turned the drawer a half-turn with her wrist. "It will sit until somebody has time, and if nobody has time before the end of the quarter, then the quarter closes, and after it closes the district office does not examine community filings, and it returns them unexamined to the filer, and the filer may lodge an objection, and the objection goes into the next quarter, and the next quarter is a quarter long." She shut the drawer. "That is the whole of the machinery and there is nobody in it to be angry at."

"And is it a refusal?"

"No. It is a receipt that says the document has been received." She looked at him. "You have one of those already. You will get a second one when they send it back, and it will look exactly like the first one, and it will mean the opposite, and if you keep both in the same drawer you will be able to tell them apart for about a year."

Then a man came up to the counter with a bundle and Owen, who had been about to leave, stood still and did not leave, because a thing happens in a room once and you have to be in the room.

The bundle was a household return. Havelock Reach, four households, a return with nine pages in it like his, except that it was not nine people and it was not a district, and the man at the counter had come in with the faces of two people who had been up all night in a hall with a stove in it.

The clerk read the bundle and then went through it again, slowly, and then she said one sentence out loud, and then she wrote it on a form, and then she gave the form to the man, and the whole thing took about four minutes.

The sentence was: *The return is returned for further particularisation. Please name the person holding the fourth branch, whose act is recorded at the third entry.*

There was a person. The man said so. He said, "It's my aunt. She's at the infirmary at Fetter Bend and she's been there since the flood and she can answer, she can answer you fine, she's just not been able to get to a counter since the eleventh."

"Then name her," said the clerk.

"I have named her. She's the fourth branch, it's her page—"

"Then you have named a branch, sir, and I have asked for a person." She put the form down and squared it. "A branch is a thing a return carries. A person is a thing a person can answer for. If you go back and write her name against the third entry as a person who performed an act on a named day, then it is a claim about a person and it will be examined as one, and it will go up, and somebody will look at it. If you leave it as it is, then it is a claim about a place, and a place cannot be examined, and I will keep it in this drawer until the quarter closes and then I will send it back to you unexamined and you may lodge an objection in the next quarter."

The man looked at the form for a while.

"You are telling me that if I write her name down properly, it gets looked at?"

"I am telling you that a person gets looked at and a place does not. I am not telling you which of those is better for your aunt."

He wrote the name. He wrote it slowly. Owen watched a man in a wet coat choose to be examined on behalf of a woman at the infirmary, and understood, standing four feet away at a public counter on a Thursday afternoon, that the entire machinery of the thing worked, that every part of it worked, that the parts that were cruel were not the parts that were broken, and that the question of which of them you ought to do was a question that this building had deliberately arranged never to answer, because answering it would have required somebody in it to be responsible for the answer.

The man went. The clerk squared the counter.

"That is the fourth one this quarter," she said, to nobody. "There is a rack behind you."

Owen looked at the rack. There were eleven bundles in it, and he counted, because counting is cheaper than guessing, and there were eleven returns in a building that kept a drawer in which the district engineer wrote *two private persons*.

---

He walked upriver at six with his hand throbbing inside the glove and got to the hall at half past.

Nessa Kray was on her own pitch with the shutters half up and the lamp lit and the seed trays out on the boards, which at half past six in the third week of the rain season is not a thing anybody does, and she was doing it, and she was alone, and she was sixty-nine in the fiction and seventy-one in fact and she had been doing it for forty-one years and she was the reason three streets of Lower Lock could tell you their own names.

"Copyist," she said, without turning round. "You've come about the new paper."

"I've come about the new paper."

"Then you'll want to know what they told me at the annex on Tuesday, and I want to say it to you before you tell me anything, because you are a man who comes in with a thing he has already decided and I am not going to be a person you did that to."

He stopped.

"They told me a return is a thing a district office reviews," said Kray. "I went in there to ask whether a person could take themselves out of one. I have signed a page and I have read my own page, because I can read my own page, and my page says I perform the maintenance of the Ninth Weir crossing weekly before two witnesses, and I have not been within thirty yards of that bridge at any hour of any day since my husband died, and I signed it because at that meeting we were all signing and I wanted the ward to have a name. And the man at the counter said no, a return is reviewed by the district office, and there is no provision for a person to take themselves out of it, and if I wanted to be out of it I should come back at the next quarter and say so at a review."

She turned round. She had a hand full of turnip seed and she set it down.

"Now," she said. "You can tell me whether that is true, and I will believe you either way, and I have put the seed down."
