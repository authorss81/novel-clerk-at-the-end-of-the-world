# Chapter 8 — Load Restriction

The thing came up from the District Deputy Registrar's office at noon on the tenth day, went onto the seal stair at half past one, and arrived at the Fourth River Desk at ten past two with a docket number on the corner and a line at the foot reading *copy and enter at Fourth Desk, per instruction*, and Owen read it standing up because a formal instrument in a building that has a seal stair is a thing you read where you stand.

**LOAD-RESTRICTION SUSPECTION**
*Ninth Weir public crossing, Docketford.*
*On the finding of the district works engineer, dated this day, that the abutment crack at the west abutment has advanced five inches since the inspection of the spring, the named crossing is closed to loaded traffic and to vehicles above four tons, with immediate effect, pending structural assessment.*

That was the whole of it. Half a sheet. It carried the engineer's finding as an exhibit and the finding was four lines long and Owen read it four times and it was correct. It was the most correct document he had ever handled. The crack was there; the engineer had put a wire across it in the spring and a mark beside it and had come back in the wet on the ninth with a rule and a lamp and had measured the offset and had written down the number, and the number was five inches, and the number was the entire case.

*No person of this Office will certify that the Ninth Weir is open to loaded traffic, and none can.*

It was not a threat and it was not an obstruction. It was a competent man declining, in the proper form, on a real measurement, to say something he could not stand behind. Owen had spent a week wishing somebody in that building would be caught out in a lie. It had never occurred to him, and it occurred to him now with a small cold shock, that the worst thing that could happen to him that week was for the Office to turn out to be right.

The notice required one more thing before it could be served on the ward, and the one more thing was the reason he was standing in the River Desk at ten past two with his coat already on.

*The condition of the named work shall be observed and recorded by a district officer, or by such witness as the ward may produce, at the hour of service.*

He read it twice. Then he went to the stair and asked the runner for the ward, and the runner said the ward was not the Registry's business, and Owen said that the serving of a suspension on a ward required somebody to go and look at the crossing, and the runner said that was the ward's business too, and Owen said it was written at the foot of the notice in the Deputy Registrar's own hand, and the runner said, "Then it will be somebody's business, sir, and I should not choose the somebody," and went away.

---

Pell was on the second desk at two o'clock with her sleeves rolled and the top of her ink dried with a scatter of sand over it, which is what the Hall did on wet days, and Owen stood at the end of it and did not sit down.

"I'm going out to the weir tonight to serve this."

"Then the ward will produce a witness."

"The ward cannot produce a witness. The ward is the ward." He put the notice on the corner of her desk. "Read the foot of it. *Such witness as the ward may produce.* Any person at all. I have been round that ward for two days asking better people than Dory Anstruther to put their names in a book and every one of them has said no for a good reason. If I go out there tonight on my own authority as a Registry clerk, I am a district officer observing a district work, and the observation is worth exactly nothing to the ward, because the notice is *served on the ward* and the ward has to be seen observing it."

Pell read it. She read it the whole way down, twice, which was not her habit.

"You want me to come out."

"I want two living observers with separable interests at a structure at dusk, and I have got no way of producing either of them out of this building without asking, and if I ask, the answer will be no, and if I don't ask then I am a man in a good coat writing down what he saw by lamplight." He stopped. "I would rather you came and it was your decision than that I arranged it without asking you."

"You have arranged it without asking me."

"Yes."

"Right." She capped her pen. "Then here is what I have decided, and I would like it written in my own words, because you will write it in yours and yours will be better prose and worse fact." She turned a clean sheet over and wrote on it, and then tore it off and gave it to him, and it said:

*I will observe the service of a load-restriction suspension on the Ninth Weir crossing on the night of this day, at the ward's hour and not the Office's. I am an officer of the Registry of Continuant Works. I observe. I have no authority to observe on the ward's behalf and I am not doing it on the ward's behalf. Whatever I see will be mine to describe and the ward's to use. If I am asked to certify that the crossing is open, I will not.*

"There," she said. "Now you can carry that in a coat pocket and it is my sentence and not yours."

---

The river was up two feet on the lower stair and had been coming up since noon.

They went out at dusk with a lamp and an oilcloth and a rule, and Wend Voss was already at the top of the weir path with a lantern, because he had come down on his own time again and had not said that he was coming, 
The party was nine. Ada with a lamp and the folded drawing. Ferris on the bank with the log book. Dory Anstruther, who had volunteered and who was the fastest of the nine in water and the second strongest. Two of the crew with a line. Ilse Mern, who had come to see, and who was told she could not go down, and who stayed. And Owen, with the notice in an oilskin and no boots worth the name.

"Down to the gauge," Ada said. "The suspension's served on the work, not on the rail, and the wire's on the west abutment, so somebody has to be at the abutment and somebody has to be at the gauge, and the gauge is in the spillway channel."

"The channel's running," said Ferris.

"Yes."

"Then nobody goes in the channel."

"Someone has to read the gauge, Thomas."

"I'll read it from the ladder."

"You can't read a number from a ladder in that water, and if you could, nobody would be able to tell you what it said afterwards, and the whole point of standing in front of a wire at night is that two people can see the same thing." She turned round. "Dory. You and Ferrow, in the channel, and the line round the bollard, and I'll hold the other end, and Rusk here is going to stand on the step and call out who is standing where and what he is doing, and if he says I can't see you then you are not there. Understood?"

"Understood," said Dory, and went down.

The channel took the water at the knee and then at the thigh, and Owen stood on the second step of the west stair with the lamp above his head and did the only thing he was any use for, which is to keep a record that a person could stand behind later.

He called them out by name. He called the moment each of them put a hand on the abutment and the moment each of them let go, and he called Dory's name every ten seconds for four minutes so that the log had a sequence in it and not merely a claim, and when Ferrow said the water was taking his boots off he wrote that down, and when Ada said the wire was at the third mark and the offset was at the fifth he wrote that down, and when Dory called out that he could see the mark and the offset both he wrote that down too, and then he made him say it again, because the first time it had been shouted over the water and the second time it was twenty words at a normal volume in the cold with his name on them.

"Again."

"I can see the spring mark and I can see the offset. Five inches, near enough, and the wire's still on."

"Whose wire?"

"Ours. The ward put it on. The engineer's is the brass one on the coping."

"And you saw the brass one?"

"No."

"Then you have not seen the brass one," Owen said, "and the brass one is the one in the finding, and I am going to write that you observed the ward's wire and the offset and did not observe the engineer's mark. That is what you will have to stand behind, so say it now if it is wrong."

"It's right," said Dory.

"Say it to her."

"It's right," said Dory, to Ada, and got out of the water, and put the rule on the step, and then went back down, because there was one more reading to take and it was at the upstream side where the crack had opened last.

He went in. The river was still coming up. The spillway at that hour was running hard enough to make a conversation impossible, and Ada had the line round the bollard with both hands, and Ferris had the other end, and Dory was a good boy in water and he was also, Owen understood, exactly the wrong age to be the one who goes back in.

"Owen," Ada said. "I want the mark again. The brass one. Ask him."

He called down. Dory said he couldn't see it, the water was over the coping.

"Then come out."

"I'm coming."

"Dory."

"I'm *coming*—"

And the river came over the coping.

It did not throw him. Owen would think about that for a long time afterward, and the thing he kept returning to was how un-dramatic it was: there was no crack of a voice, no cry, no moment. The water simply rose four inches in about two seconds, took the coping, and took a nineteen-year-old off his feet, and put him twelve feet down the spillway channel into a stone box with a closed gate at the bottom.

The line went tight. Ada did not fall over, which Owen thought at the time was the least likely fact in the world, and afterwards was the fact he could not argue with. She braced both heels in the stair and came back hand over hand on the line, and Ferris had the other end round a bollard, and Ilse Mern had got to the rope without being told, and a man Owen did not know came down the path at a run because Voss's lantern had been visible from the road.

They got him out at the third attempt. It took six minutes and it took two men Owen had never met and a boat-hook, and it took longer than Owen's memory has any room for. When it was done the boy was on the weir path on his side with his arms moving and Ada Rusk's hands on his ribs counting, and Voss was holding a blanket, and the lamp was lying on its side in the water at the bottom of the stair, and the ward's finished return was dry in a coat on the bank, nine feet and a stone wall away from where the boy had been.

Owen sat down on the coping of the weir path with his hands shaking and a sheet of oiled paper in his pocket and found that the part of him which was still working was the part that was glad nobody had asked him to sign anything.

---

The boy was alive. He was alive and he was in the infirmary at Fetter Bend until the second day of the morning with a broken collarbone and a lung full of river and no particular reason to be dead, and the ward got him out and paid for the boat-hook, and the ward had done all of that within forty minutes while Owen stood on a stair and did arithmetic.

Then, at about two in the morning, on the step, with the lamp relit and Pell sitting on the dry side with her coat up over her knees, Owen took out a sheet of paper.

He wrote it on the knee of the bollard in about four minutes. It was a district works continuity statement, which is the form engineers use to record that a structure remains in the condition last certified between inspections, and it is a form that exists because most structures are not inspected every week and something has to say that nobody has come and found the crack worse.

*Continuity statement, Ninth Weir public crossing. The named work is maintained by the district performing the maintenance duty. The condition of the named work is supported as maintained. The work is not certified open. No load certification is given.*

It read well. It was four sentences and it was not a lie in any of its words, and it was a forgery in the only way that matters, because *supported as maintained* is a status claim, and no one on the weir path had any standing to make it, and the statement had been composed by a man standing on a wet step who had watched a boy nearly drown under a bridge and had wanted, with a completeness that frightened him, to be the man who wrote the bridge down as all right.

He read it again. Then he copied it out, word for word, into the notebook he kept for himself, in the middle of a page about a pump and a granary and the word *withdrawn* three times, because a man who copies a thing down is a man who can be asked what it was, and he had decided in the four seconds it took him to copy it that he would rather be asked.

Then he tore it up.

He tore it into pieces the size of a playing card and he did it in front of Junia Pell, on a step, in the rain, and while he did it he said, out loud, at a volume she could not have missed if she had wanted to.

"I wrote that. That was the wording. That is what a false statement would have said if I had wanted to be the man who carried it, and I have written it in my own book, and I have just destroyed it, and I am telling you that I wrote it because I want it recorded that I am the sort of clerk who would have, and because in six months somebody is going to be looking for a hand that put *supported as maintained* on this crossing and there is one, and it is mine, and it is not on any paper in this building tonight."

Pell looked at him.

She said nothing. That was the whole of what she did, and it was more than he had any right to expect, and it was more than he could have survived an answer to. She did not say *it doesn't count* and she did not say *you'd never* and she did not look at him with anything at all, and after a while she got up and went down the weir path to see to the blanket.



---

The ward did the honest version, on the second day of the morning, at the hall, in about forty minutes, and Ada Rusk dictated most of it.

Not a claim that the crossing is open. Not a claim that it is safe. A claim that this ward performs the maintenance of it, and that the crossing's *last supported status* is preserved, and a repair duty that is not ours, and a date.

"Read it back," she said. "Read the effect."

Owen read the effect.

*This district performs the maintenance of the Ninth Weir public crossing, a named work at the named place, and preserves the crossing's last supported legal status, to wit: that of a public crossing at the Ninth Weir maintained by a district, and not that of an open crossing. This return claims nothing as to the safety, condition, or use of the crossing, which no witness in this return is competent to speak to. The crossing is closed to loaded traffic and to vehicles above four tons under a load-restriction suspension of this date, which is not challenged by this return and is not superseded by it. This district does not perform, and does not claim to perform, the repair of the west abutment. It renews, in the week ending, the marker, the channel clearance, and the pump prime, and it will renew them at the same interval while this return stands. Any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it. This return comes back for renewal every seven days from the day it is entered, and not before.*

Nobody argued about it. They had arrived, by the particular route of people who had to keep a pump running, at the only claim anybody could actually make.

"Read the last line again," Ada said.

"*Any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it.*"

"I want a second copy of that in bigger letters on the notice we put on the weir path," she said. "In case somebody from the Council comes."

She said the other thing before anybody asked her to, standing at the end of the long table with her arms folded, in front of nine people and a margin with two witnesses' names on it.

"Now listen. There's no money in that. No fund, no crew coming, nobody from the Council, and the district office is going to read this and see nine people who have volunteered to keep a bridge they are not paid for and cannot afford, and what they will do about it is nothing, for as long as they possibly can. So I want it said in the hall, in front of the two witnesses, that from today the nine of us are the people who keep that crossing up, and that when the water comes up in the winter it is us who go down there with a line. I have a young one who nearly went into the channel tonight, and I am saying it here so that it is written down somewhere that we said it." She looked round the table. "Is that fair?"

"That is fair," said Marguerite Bray, "and it is the first sensible thing said in this hall in a month, and I will write it in the book."

---

The suspension notice came back down to the Fourth River Desk on the second day at nine in the morning, routed by the district office for copying and entry, and Owen copied it, and entered it in the docket, and initialled nothing, because the box that says the copy agrees with the original is the Deputy Registrar's and not his, and has been since the building opened.

He copied it perfectly. That was the thing he could not get out of his hands afterwards: a document that closed a crossing, suspended a status, and gave a ward a real appeal window, which the spring notice never carried, and he copied it so exactly that not one clerk in Docketford could afterwards point at a page and say the Registry had made an error — and the accuracy of it was why a nineteen-year-old could not get a bridge back by writing a letter.

Nobody asked him to sign anything. Nobody has ever asked him to sign anything in this whole business, and that was the arrangement, and he understood it while he was doing it, and he understood it perfectly, and that was the moment he began to be frightened in a way that he did not have words for until much later.

Pike had learned what Owen's hand was worth. A nuisance on the Fourth River Desk had become a component of the machinery, and the machinery did not need to threaten him or even notice him, and he sat at his desk with a clean run and a shut docket and a ward that could not pay for a crossing, and he was proud of his copy, and the pride sat in him like something swallowed.

He wrote a list that afternoon. He did not know who it was for any more.
