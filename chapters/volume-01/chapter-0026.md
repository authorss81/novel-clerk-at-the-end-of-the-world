# Chapter 26 — The Schedule of Refusals

The counters open on a Saturday for matters with a date on them, and the copying floor does not run, and Owen Rusk went down to the glass at twenty past nine on the morning of the thirty-third day of the month with a sheet of paper in his coat that he had written at five in the morning and had not shown to anybody, including the woman who had watched him write it, who was a very bad sign.

The ward had renewed on the Friday. That was done. It was in a drawer in a room in the district office with a date on the interval in it, and the interval had started again on the thirty-second and the next one was the thirty-ninth, and the disposition was *renewed for review* for the third time in two weeks, on the same terms, with the two observers re-separated in writing, in their own hands, once for this renewal, and nobody had been suspended and nothing had been restored and the marker had been set at seven forty on the Friday morning by three people on the top path, and the channel had been clear, and the pump had taken, and there had been no stranger in the ward at all, because a stranger in the ward is a thing a ward writes and asks for itself, and this ward had not yet learned how.

The return was renewed. The order was on a docket with no hearing date on it. The pump grant was suspended. The crossing was closed. The abutment was four thousand one hundred and sixty and unfunded. The sixteen were still sixteen.

And in an hour and a half Owen was going to do the only thing he had ever done in this building that could not be taken back, and he was not sure it was right, and he had gone and done it anyway, which was a sentence he had said out loud about somebody else a fortnight ago.

---

The Glass Stair counter clerk on the Saturday was a young man and he had the docket open and he had a good memory, and when Owen put 487-GS-19 on the counter the clerk said, "Co-petitioner. You were in here on the twelfth of the month with a form about a shape."

"Yes."

"That was the best thing anybody has done at this counter in a month," said the clerk, "and I have been told by a runner to tell you that the improvement in the answer is *not* to be attributed to you, which is fair, and I have also been told to keep saying that, which is what I am doing." He pulled the docket. "What do you want."

"I want to inspect the documents produced in the matter."

The clerk stopped with his hand on the volume.

"That is a thing," he said. "Nobody has ever asked me for that. There is a form—"

"I know there is a form. I want to inspect the documents produced in a matter in which I am a named party, and I am not asking to have any of them copied, and I am not asking for anything about the matter. I am asking what the office put in it."

The clerk read the rule on the board above the counter, which Owen had read four times in a month and had never seen read, and then he went and got a second man from the back and the two of them were gone for six minutes, and came back with a tied bundle of about forty sheets and a slip, and the slip was the standard slip, and it said what a slip says.

"Thirty-nine sheets and the covering sheet. You may read them here and you may not take them off the desk. If you want a copy of any one of them you may ask and it is a separate thing and it costs nothing and it goes on the record."

Owen read the thirty-nine sheets standing up at the end of the counter over about three quarters of an hour, and the queue behind him did not go away, because a queue outside a counter on a Saturday morning is a queue of people who are waiting and a man reading a bundle at the end of the counter is a thing that happens, and about nine of them read over his shoulder at some point, which he was aware of and permitted and had counted on.

Thirty-nine sheets. A *received* acknowledgement, a copy of the return as filed, the countersignature slip, the load-restriction suspension, and then a run of fourteen sheets that were the district office's internal file of the matter, and then — at the back, and bound separately, and with a different tie — four sheets headed:

*Extract from the refusal column of this office for the present quarter, produced in answer to the matters raised.*

Owen read the four sheets four times.

A **refusal column** is not a book of grievances. It is the dullest document the Office produces, and it is produced twice a quarter, and it is a numbered column with a quarter at the top and an officer's initials against each line and a disposition against each line, and the dispositions run in standard forms because a form costs nothing and a sentence costs money, and the whole of the Office's public memory of what it has said no to is about ten printed words wide.

**One hundred and nine entries** in the Docketford quarter.

One hundred and nine lines, one hundred and nine numbers, one hundred and nine dates, one hundred and nine sets of initials, and one hundred and nine dispositions in about ten forms, and it is correct on its face on every single line, and a man can read the whole of it in twenty minutes and learn that this office has declined to do a great many things this quarter for a great many reasons, most of which are about form, and none of which is anybody's fault.

And forty-one of the dispositions are the same four words.

Owen Rusk stood at the end of the Glass Stair counter on the thirty-third day of the month and read the same four words forty-one times, and they were:

*No district office competent.*

He read them and then he found the section of the column in which the fall was and read them again there, and they were the same, and he copied nothing and wrote nothing and did the thing that he had learned to do in a month, which was to go and look at the physical act — and the physical act was a bundle of thirty-nine sheets tied with two ties, one of which is a county tie, and the second tie is this Office's, and the second tie is on because the bundle has been out and back.

---

"Mr Rusk." The clerk had come round the end of the counter. "You have been standing at my counter for six minutes with a face on and I would like you to move because there is a queue and you are in it."

"Sorry."

"Do not be sorry, be useful. If you want a copy of one of those four sheets, say so now and I will do it in ninety seconds and it will go on the record and you will have it in a week by post, and everybody who comes looking for you afterwards will find that you asked for it properly and in public." He put his hand on the tie. "Or you can put it down and go away, and you will be able to say that you saw it, and nobody will be able to check, and you will have got exactly nothing out of it."

Owen Rusk looked at the four sheets for about two seconds.

"A copy of all four," he said. "Please. In the matter, in my name, today, with the date and the time on the slip, and the county tie off and the Registry tie on."

The clerk did it in about ninety seconds and it was the first thing Owen Rusk had ever seen anybody do quickly in this building that was also correct.

---

He made the comparison sheet at the copying floor between one and three in the afternoon on a Saturday, in an empty hall, with the press cold and the lamps off, and it took him an hour and a half and he made it four times.

The first one was a document. It had a heading, a docket reference, a list of sources, and a conclusion, and it read like a memorandum, and it was a memorandum, and a memorandum is a thing that goes in a file and a file is a thing in a drawer, and he threw it in the waste basket himself and it went down with a noise he enjoyed.

The second one was a petition, and it was eight paragraphs, and it was good, and it was a filing, and he had spent a month learning what filings cost, and he tore it up at the bottom rather than the top because a man who tears a thing at the top is performing.

The third one he took to Pell, who had come in on the Saturday because there was a dated matter, and who read it standing at the end of the second desk and said, "This is a poster."

"It is a comparison of four forms in a column against a return and against a grant file."

"It is a poster with numbers on it and a heading and no conclusion," said Pell, "and I want to tell you three things about it and then I am going back to the tray."

"Go on."

"One. As a document it is worthless. There is no office it goes to. Nobody is obliged to answer it, nobody is obliged to keep it, and in nine years there has not been one in this building because a document is a request and this is not a request." She squared it. "Two. As a *thing in a public room* it is not worthless, and you have worked that out, and I have watched you work it out in about ninety seconds which is faster than I did, and I am not going to be bitter about it. Anything on a table in a public room with a heading on it is a thing that has been said, and it has been said in a room with people in it, and the reason the Office cannot reverse a thing is not procedure, it is that there is no procedure for un-saying a thing in front of the people who heard it."

"And three."

"Three is that you will make enemies who are not enemies yet," said Junia Pell, "and that is the part I want in your book, because you will not believe it in a week. Not the Deputy Registrar. The Deputy Registrar has a Friday and a signature and a column of sixteen and he does not care what a sheet of paper says in a waiting hall. What you are about to do is noticed by the people who draft the standard forms. Because that is where the four words live. Those words are not an officer's invention, they are a *form*, and a form belongs to whoever maintains the forms, and that person is a clerk on a schedule of duties in this building, and they have never been anybody's enemy in their life, and you are about to make them one by printing their own sentence in a public room and asking a queue what it means."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It is not a threat, it is a *description of what you are doing*," said Pell, "and you are going to go and do it anyway, and I am not going to stop you, and I am not going to be in the room when you do, and I want that recorded in the sense that I am going to remember having said it."

---

He did it at ten past three on the Saturday afternoon in the waiting hall of the Glass Stair, on the public table by the door, in the middle of a queue of about twenty people, with a card propped against it that said what it was and where the four sheets could be found and on what date and at what hour, and he did not speak to anybody, and there was no speech, and the sheet said this and said no more:

*In the matter of petition 487-GS-19, Lower Lock ward.*
*The four words above are the disposition in this office's refusal column for the present quarter, against one hundred and nine entries. Forty-one entries carry them.*
*They are also the disposition against the three requests by T. Sarr of this ward, refused at the Fetter Bend sub-table, of which the office has said nothing since the autumn, and of which one is a matter of record in this docket.*
*District Return 487-LL, entered the fifteenth, of which the ward's steward is a petitioner in this docket, is before this office for review, and was renewed for review on the thirty-second, and claims a duty and not a status.*
*No conclusion is offered. The column may be inspected at this counter on any day the counter is open. The four sheets are produced in this docket and may be inspected here.*

Underneath, in his square hand, at the bottom, where a man who has spent a month learning to file puts the one thing he is actually saying:

*The phrase names a thing about this office and not about any of the forty-one people. That is why it is the same forty-one times.*

He read it over once. Then he put his hand flat on the table for a second, which he had picked up in four weeks from two different people in two different rooms, and then he took his hand off it, because it was not his table.

And then he went and stood at the end of the queue and let twenty people read a sheet he had written, and about fourteen of them read it, and one man read it twice and asked the clerk at the counter a question about it, and the clerk answered it, because it is the clerk's own document and the clerk had produced it.

Owen Rusk stood against a wall in a waiting hall in Docketford for about an hour and a half and watched his own handwriting be read by strangers and felt absolutely nothing, in the way he had felt nothing at the bottom of a stair with a cut hand, and understood that this was the correct feeling and that the correct feeling was the one he was going to have for the rest of his life on the days when the work was right.

---

Pell found him at the door at half past five with her coat on.

"You are not proud of it," she said.

"No."

"And you are not sorry for it either."

"No."

"Say both of them out loud, because you have never said either out loud in the four weeks I have known you and if you are going to be a man who prints things in waiting halls then you are going to have to be able to say the two sentences out loud to another person while they are looking at you."

Owen Rusk stood in the doorway of the Glass Stair at half past five on the thirty-third day of the month and said them out loud, both of them, to a woman of twenty-two who was already late for something.

"I am not proud of that sheet, because it is a count of a queue of people in a district that will not fund a pump, and the forty-one words in it are the most efficient sentence anybody in this building has ever written, and whoever wrote it did it to save a clerk four minutes, and that is not a villain, that is a person doing a good job badly. And I am not sorry for it either, because the third request on that sheet was refused three times by a man who had walked two miles upriver in the wet and it was refused correctly and the office has never once said so out loud, and it is going to be said out loud now, and the only reason is that I made a table with a heading on it and stood next to it."

"Right," said Pell.

"That is genuinely all of it. I have spent a month in an argument about whether this Office is careless and I have proved, twice, that it is not, and the only thing I have ever got anybody to do about it is a form being printed in a hall."

"Which is the whole of what the record is for," said Junia Pell. "Not the argument. The printing. Every good thing anybody has ever done in this building has been somebody writing something down where a person who was not in the room could come and read it, and nobody ever puts that in a speech, and I have been in this building three months and I have watched about forty people find that out and I have watched about nine of them find it out from a waiter."

She went out into the wet and he stood in the doorway.

The sheet was on the table by the door. It would be on the table by the door tomorrow when the counter opened, and on Monday, and on Tuesday, and it would be on the table by the door until somebody came and took it down, and the only person entitled to take it down was the person who put it up, or an officer of the office in a written order, and there is no form on which an officer can order a sheet off a public table without putting his own name and reason on the order and leaving the order on the table as well.

He had spent a month learning to file. He had never once learned to print.

And on the walk upriver in the rain he did the arithmetic that he had started doing on the twenty-sixth, standing at the bottom of a lane, and it came out different. He had a return renewed for review on a Friday with a date on it. He had a ward that was going to be read on a Thursday by a stranger and a condition in a delivery book that is not funded. He had three of the forty numbers in his own writing from a route list, and a batch of forty-one that would be read at some point by somebody who had not been told any of this, and a name he had said out loud at a counter on Monday that he could not un-say. And he had, on a table in a public hall, a sentence of four words printed forty-one times in a quarter in a standard form by a person who was good at their job.

Which made him, at thirty-two years of age, on the last wet Monday in the fourth week of the rain season, in a good coat, with a cut finger that had not healed — the person who counts things for a living. He had not wanted that. He had wanted, for four weeks, to be a man who was right about one bridge. He was going to be, instead, a man who had made a list, and a list is a thing other people have to argue with, for ever, and there would be a copy of four sheets in his coat inside a week by the ordinary post, and the Office was going to have to answer, in a room with a queue in it, about forty-one dispositions.

At the bottom of the hill the standpipe was running and there was a light in the pump house, and the granary book said three weeks and a bit, and the sixteen were still sixteen, and the order was still on a docket with no hearing date on it.
