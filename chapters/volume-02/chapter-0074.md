# Chapter 74 — Ossery

Ossery is twelve households and a lime kiln and it is four miles up a cart track off the flat above the weirs, and the track is not metalled for the last mile, and the nearest water of any kind is forty minutes off in any direction, and the reason there is a settlement there at all is that the ground is on top of a bed of shell. He walked it on the Tuesday morning of the twentieth day of the second month of the Concord Year 488, on foot, alone, with a sheet of paper in his coat that had nothing written on it, and he said out loud, twice, on the track, why he was going.

**Because Warden Kade had not been into Ossery this season and would not say why, and had said the reason was a thing about her and not about Ossery, and had said it in a field with a sheet wired to a frame behind her.**

Then he said the other thing, which is the true thing, and which he also said out loud, twice, because he has been a man who tells people the true version before the comfortable one since the second week of the month of rain in a year that has ended.

**Because she would not say why, and a person who will not say why is a person you go and look at, and that is not a reason. That is a shape my hands make when nobody has given me a job.**

There is no season. The count was certified on the ninth and it is qualified and Docketford district is reported short by seven in its return of premises and it was also reported short in the Concord Year 484 and in the Concord Year 486, and the schedule for the season of 489 was printed in advance on the twenty-eighth of this month with ten names on it, and none of those ten is going to be looked at by anybody before the season opens, and the Warden may return a schedule and may not amend it, and there is no day on any of them because the days come out of a printed sheet and the printed sheet is a week and not a day.

**Owen Rusk was not the person who reconciles Ossery and had no business in that yard and knew both of those things at the top of the track.**

---

The first house on the track is a lime worker's cottage and the kiln is four hundred yards further on and is a stone stack about the size of a two-storey house with a low arch in it, and it was cold, because a kiln is banked and cold is what a banked kiln is. Maud Tray was stacking sacking against the windward side of it, and she was about fifty, and she had done it in the way of a person who has done it since she was about nine, and when she turned round and saw a man coming up the track with a coat and no bag she did not startle, and that was the first thing he noticed about her.

"You are the count," she said.

"I am the district office and I am not the count and I am not the Warden and nobody has sent me."

"You are the man who put a column on a board."

"That is fair."

"Then you are the one who is on the board and not the one who is in the counting room, and I have heard about that from four people this season and one of them was the man with the cart, who does not come down this track more than twice a week and came down it in November to tell me about it." She put the sacking down. "**What do you want, because if you want a number I have got eleven doors and four of them are kilns and the kilns are not doors, and I have said that to two people this year already.**"

"I do not want a number," said Owen Rusk. "I do not have any business in this yard. I have come forty minutes off the water on a Tuesday because the Warden did not come and would not say why, and I have come to look, and looking is not reconciling and nobody has asked me to and I am not going to pretend I have been sent."

Maud Tray looked at him for about four seconds and then picked the sacking up again and carried on stacking it, which is the answer a person gives when they have decided somebody is not going to be a nuisance.

"Then stand on that side," she said, "because that is where the wind is not, and do not go round the back of the kiln, and tell me before you go into the low house, because that is a house and not a work and it is the only one of the twelve with a door that shuts and I would like to know who has been in it."

---

The water came up the track twice a week in a cart, and it had been coming up the track twice a week for thirty-one years, and the man who drove it was sixty-one and did not stop the cart until it was under the wall. He had a book on the seat of it. It was a bound ledger about the size of a parish register and the binding had been mended twice with a length of bootlace, and it was not an instrument, and Owen Rusk could tell from about eight feet off that nobody in the county had ever seen it, because it had nobody's name on the spine and it had never been in a room with a rail round three sides of it.

The man got the barrow down and then came and looked at Owen Rusk, and Owen Rusk put his hands where a person puts them when they are on somebody else's track.

"**I am not the water office,**" he said, "and I am not the count, and there is no form in this county on which I could write down what you have told me, and I have not written anything down."

"Then why are you standing there."

"Because the Warden of the Count has not been into this settlement this season and has said the reason is a thing about herself and not about you, and I would like to know whether there is something in this yard she did not want to be in the same room with, and I am asking you rather than assuming."

The man of about sixty-one put one hand on the cart and did not unhook the barrow.

"You are the fourth," he said.

"Fourth what."

"**Fourth person out of a building to come up this track since the twelfth of the month before last, and the first one that was not the water gate, and the first one that came on a Tuesday, and the second one that asked me a question.**" He took his hand off the cart. "The other three came about the fire in the first month of the year and wanted a pump and did not want a bucket, and the fire was out before the water came. The water gate man came twice and was sorry four times and I told him not to be, and that was correct, because there is nothing in his form for what happened to the mill and he cannot help it and being sorry to me about it does him no good at all."

"That is a good way to be spoken about."

"It is a way I have had thirty-one years of practice at." He looked at the cart. "**Now. What do you want.**"

"I want to look at the water."

"You can look at the water. **Everybody can look at the water. That is the thing I have been saying for thirty-one years and nobody has been in this yard to hear it.**"

---

He looked at it. It was in a tank against the wall of the low house and it was about a foot and a half down and it came there in a cart twice a week and the tank holds about a day and a half and the twelve households have about a day and a half of water between them at any time, which is not enough and is what there is. And then he did the thing he is, and he did it out loud, and he told her he was going to before he did it, which is the only apology there is.

**He went down the second column.**

Because the copy of the return of authorised draws was in his coat and a sheet a yard wide with a hundred and ninety-four lines on it is the sort of thing a man takes a walk with, and the line for Ossery was at the bottom of a page and it was the largest figure per premises of the eleven, by a distance, and he had not known what it was a figure of, and it was not a standpipe.

The tank is fed by a cart. There is no standpipe at Ossery and there has never been one, and the cart has come twice a week since the Concord Year 457, and the figure on the return of authorised draws is not a measurement of anything at all. It is **the amount of water this man has carried**, and the amount is entered because the kiln is asked for it, and the kiln is asked for it because the return of authorised draws is a return of premises, and a cart is not a premise, and there is no line in the book for a cart.

The entry is **one thousand two hundred a day, authorised**, and it is the largest of the eleven on the printed list by a distance, and it is more than two of the eleven villages draw between them, and it is not a round figure, because a cart is not a round thing.

The entry has been on the Water Office's return since the Concord Year 481 and it has never been struck, because the man who makes that return takes last year's sheet and corrects it, and he can strike a line when a place has told him the line is wrong, and **the only person in this county who could tell him the line was wrong is standing at a cart with a bootlace in the binding of a book and has never been asked.**

"That is the figure," said Owen Rusk. "Nine hundred and forty a day, authorised, and the largest per premises of the eleven on the printed list, and it is a cart."

"Yes."

"And it has been on that sheet since the Concord Year 481."

"**Since the Concord Year 481 and I have carried it since the Concord Year 457, and the year before the sheet began, so the sheet has never once been wrong about me and it has never once been right either, because it has never been about me.**"

"You have been the reason for a figure on a printed list for seven years and nobody has asked you a question."

"**Nobody has asked me a question in thirty-one years and I have not wanted one and I want you to understand the wanting.**" The man of about sixty-one put his hand flat on the cart the way a person does to a horse. "In the first month of the year there was a fire in the low house and I came up with the cart and put it out, and the water gate man came on the Saturday on his own in a cart and was sorry four times. Nobody has ever come up here to ask me how much water comes. **Everybody knows how much water comes. It is the only fact anybody has ever agreed about in this place and it is not in a column and it is not in a book in a room in this city, and it is in here,**" — he tapped the bootlace — "and the book is the reason the twelve of us have water, because when the figure goes up to a district in Concord Hall every season it goes up from that book, and the office sends it back every season and asks the kiln whether it is right, and the kiln says yes, and the figure comes up again."

Owen Rusk did not say anything for a moment. He was aware of doing the thing he does, which is to work out what kind of question he is being asked after he has answered it, and that he had answered this one as a document, and that a document would be very easy.

"**Do not.**"

"I was not going to."

"You were going to write it on that sheet in your coat and take it to somebody. I have watched a man with a book do that in a yard in the first week of the month and he wrote a word in capitals on a form about a mill two hours up a river, and he thought about it for nine minutes afterwards, and it is on a file in your building and he cannot get it off." The man of about sixty-one was not angry, and that was the worst part of it. "**If it gets to a building that the figure is a book, then the figure is not a measurement, and a figure that is not a measurement is a figure nobody has to pay anybody for, and the figure stops coming up, and the kiln stops being asked, and I am a man with a cart.** And there is nothing in this county that would put the twelve houses back on a tank, and you know that, because you have been in a building for six weeks and you know exactly how many ways there are of nothing."

"That is true," said Owen Rusk, "and it is the reason I am not going to say it in a field, and I am going to tell you the whole of what I am promising so that you can decide about it, because I have not got a right to anything here and a promise you cannot check is worth nothing to both of us."

"Go on."

"I will not say it in a field and I will not say it in a hall and I will not say it to the woman who maintains a board in a field behind a tannery. And if the Warden of the Count asks me, in a room, with a day named, I will tell her the true thing, because that is what a person who is asked is for. And I am not going to promise you I will lie to her, and I am not going to tell you that I will protect a cart, because I have found out about four times in this season that a person who says *I will protect a thing* is describing a thing they intend to use."

The man of about sixty-one stood in the yard for a while.

"**That is fair,**" he said at last, "and it is more than I expected and I expected nothing, and I have not been asked for nothing in a very long time." He got his hand off the cart. "Now I am going to tell you the other one and then you can go, and I am telling you because you came on a Tuesday and not on the day the water comes."

"Go on."

"**A count of the premises of record in this district is not a count of these twelve houses.** There are eleven doors against Ossery on the sheet and there are fourteen roofs and four of the fourteen are kilns, and the kilns are works, and if you put a work in a settlement then the settlement has failed to be a settlement, and nobody has ever asked me which four, because nobody has ever come." He picked the barrow up. "That is what is on your list and I have never been able to say it to a person. **You are the first person out of a building to come up this track in thirty-one years who was not a water gate, and I would like it noticed that I am saying it to you in a yard and not to a book, because I have watched what happens to books.**"

---

He worked out the rest of it at the top of the track with his hands on his knees, and it took him about a minute and a half, and none of it is a thing he may do.

**The review route is the only instrument in this county that asks a settlement what it does.** It is requested by a person of the district. It must show a current local act performed by the settlement, this season, at a place of their own choosing, recorded, and not an act claimed for them from two miles away. It is heard before an officer of the count having no district in the matter. It is requested within seven days of the reconciliation, running from the hour the stone goes down out of the case. And the requester is named on it, and the name goes on a public file.

And there is a pump rota kept weekly by one woman at Witherow that satisfied every word of that, and the review was heard and sustained, and the file says *no current local act shown*, and the reason it was lost is that the office has no column for a settlement and a rota kept by one woman is not three premises.

And here, on a cart track four miles off the water, there is a cart that has gone twice a week for thirty-one years, and a book with a bootlace in it, and a man who has not been asked anything, and a fire in the first month of the year, and a tank.

**That is the act. That is a current local act performed by the settlement, at a place of their own choosing, recorded weekly, and not claimed for them from two miles away. It has been produced by a person who was not asked for it and cannot be named on anything, and the roster in Fetter Lane is a better-built document than that book by a long way, and that ward is on the ten and this settlement is not, and I have not worked out whether that is a coincidence and I am not going to work it out in a lane.**

And the door to the review route opens when a stone comes down out of a case, and a stone comes down out of a case when a settlement is reconciled, and Ossery was not reconciled, and it was not reconciled because the Warden suspended the reconciliation of Docketford district with the hour of the certification of the count, in her own hand, in a field, with a page long reason that is not a figure.

**So the best act in the district is eight days past its own seven, and it will be eight days past its own seven for the rest of the season, and there is no instrument in this county for a settlement that has not been counted.**

He stood on the bank above the track for a while and did the two sums of his whole term, which were the five lines in a delivery book in Fetter Lane and the three holds on a card, and neither of them moved, and both of them had been in the same place in the spring.

---

The last house on the track is the low one, and he asked Maud Tray before he went into it, and she came and stood at the door and let him in for about ninety seconds and would not let him further.

Nobody was going to be starved, nobody was going to be thirsty, and there was bread in a crock and a girl of about nine asleep on a bench with a coat over her and a woman of about forty sitting on the floor with a mended sleeve who said the two sentences that were available to her and would not say them twice, and Owen Rusk did not ask her a third thing because there was no third thing.

Then he went back down the track at about two o'clock in the afternoon and the light was long and there was nothing on it but him.

On the Monday, the day before, Maud Tray had told him — while they were stacking sacking and without any particular emphasis, the way a person tells a clerk a thing they do not think is interesting — that a man had come up the track about two o'clock on the Monday with a chain and a rule and a book, and had said he was from the district and had wanted to walk the yard and take a figure of the roofs, and that nobody had let him, and that he had gone away at about half past three without a fight.

"**He was very polite about it,**" she said. "That is the part I would remember. He was extremely polite and he had a rule and he was going to do the roofs and I said no, and he said *that is quite all right* and wrote something in the book and shut it and went away."

"Do you know what the book was for."

"No," said Maud Tray. "I have had four people out of a building at that gate this year and I have asked two of them what they came for and I have not asked you, because you are the one on the board, and I would like you to work out for yourself whether that is a clever thing to have said or a stupid one, and I have not decided yet and it is the twentieth."

---

At the slip at the foot of the lane at about twenty past three there was a boat, and it was not going up to the reach, and Nell Arbour was standing in about a foot of water on the boards with a basket and a bundle and a sheet of paper rolled in her coat, and Owen Rusk stopped about four feet off and did not go closer, which is the first thing he has done correctly in about a month.

"There is a household at the top of that road that I asked for and you did not ask for," she said, without preamble, in the ordinary voice, and she did not look at him while she said it. "It has been struck on the ninth and it is in a kitchen and it is not my business to be on a track about, and I am not going to tell you one word about it on a road."

"I will not ask."

"**Good.**" She got the basket onto the boards. "You have been up there on a Tuesday, which is a thing you did not tell anybody you were going to do, and there is going to be a column in about nine months with a year in it that is blank, and I am not interested in the column and I am telling you so that you will know that there is a person in this county who is not interested in the column."

She got the bundle in after the basket and did not look up again.

Neither of them said one word about the difference between them. Neither ever has. There is not going to be.

He got back up the hill at about half past four and did the tray and was on it at a quarter past six, and there was nothing on it from the reach and nothing on it from the cart track.

On the Wednesday he wrote four lines on the back of a sheet from a tide table in the room in Fetter Lane, with the lamp out, and struck two of them out before the ink was dry, and on the Thursday evening he went to the Glass Stair and read the four grounds aloud to nobody and then again to the wall, and what he had was this.

**A reconciliation in Docketford district in the season of 489 will be conducted, if it is conducted at all, in reliance upon a district return made by this district office. That return asks, in a column, for the year of the last survey of each place named in it. There is no such year. A paper that asks for a year this district has never had is a paper that is not current, and the currency of a paper a reconciliation rests upon is the third limb of what I am permitted to do, and I have not used it.**

**It is not a settlement. It is not the schedule. It is not a choice of places. It is a question about a document in this building and it is the only question I am allowed to ask, and I have been looking for it since the twelfth day of the month before, and it was on the second page of a form in my own hand, and I did not see it for a fortnight because I was reading a column on a board.**

He did not write anything else. He did not go up the reach, and he did not go to the Water Office, and he did not write to a man in a cart, and he did not put a name anywhere, and there is no form in this county for a person who is owed a question by an office and cannot ask it, and he had one question and it was about a column and he was going to be able to put it in a room.

The count was qualified. The best act produced in Docketford district this season belongs to a man in a cart with a bootlace in a book who has not been asked anything and cannot be put on a document, and the only door in this county that would have asked him was shut at noon on the ninth by a sheet wired to a frame by a woman who is right, and it is going to stay shut until somebody in Concord Hall with a pencil in a room two hundred miles up a river decides that it opens.
