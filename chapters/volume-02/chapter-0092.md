# Chapter 92 — What a Room Can Ask

The question was not what did you want. Owen Rusk had a form in his coat for what a person wanted and it was not the right one, and he found that out standing in a doorway at half past four in the afternoon of the Thursday of the fourth month of the Concord Year 488 with about fourteen people in a room eight feet by eleven with a stove in the corner of it and no table in it but a long one against the wall with a ledger on it.

"So I am going to ask it a different way," he said. "**I am going to ask each of you what you want, and I am going to write down the answers as they are given and not as they ought to have been given, and not one of them goes in a column, because there is no column in this county for it and I will not make one.**"

"And where do they go," said Tobin Ragg.

"In a book I keep," said Owen Rusk, "and the book does not leave this room unless I carry it, and it has not been shown to anybody and it stays shut, and that is the whole of the promise I can make in a room, and I have made it before, to people who believed it."

Somebody at the back said, "How many rooms."

"Twenty-two households was the figure on Monday and I did not count them and nobody counted them for me."

"Twenty-two and about forty rooms and nine outbuildings," said Prud Naylor, who had the tank book on the long table and did not open it, "and the nine outbuildings are the pump house and the cart house and seven others, and one of the seven is a byre with a roof on it and nobody has ever called a byre a dwelling and I am not the one who starts, and the chapel is not one of the nine because the chapel is a chapel."

---

**He asked fourteen people in that room over about two hours and he got five answers and no two of the five were the same, and the fifth one came from a man of nineteen who had been sitting on his hands the whole time and who had not been asked anything by anybody in that room before Owen Rusk got to him.**

One: Jos Harker, forty-six, at the sluice wheel. "**Somebody to come and look at that frame. That is all. I am not asking for the sluice to be made better than it is. I am asking for a person with a trade to stand under it once and say a thing about it out loud, and then I will know what I have got, and I have been putting my shoulder against it for nine years and I do not know what I have got.**"

Two: a man of about sixty named only as Caws, who had been at Slackwater forty-one years. "**Nothing. I would like nothing. I have never had nothing from this county and I have not noticed it until about a year ago, and that is the whole of my grievance and it is against myself.**"

Three: the widow of a man who cut at the eleven in the Concord Year 481, who asked whether anybody would come if she asked, and then said that she was not asking, she was asking whether it made a difference, and that she had worked out in about a minute that it did and she would like to be left alone.

Four: Tobin Ragg, thirty-four, with a girl of nine asleep on his coat. "**A roof on the sluice before the winter and no questions. And I will say the second thing because I have been in this room an hour and I am going to go home and I would rather say it here than think about it on the way. I do not want to be a line in a ledger. I have been a line in a ledger once and it cost my mother a roof over her head in 474 and I am not going to be one twice in one life.**"

And five, from a man of nineteen at the end of the table with his hands flat on the wood, who had a daughter of eleven in the next house and no premises of record of his own anywhere in this county, and who said:

"**Ask us what we want. That is what a room is for and you have had it an hour. And I will tell you what I want, which is that somebody writes down that there are twenty-two of us here, in our own words, in a book that is ours, and that I have asked for it and that somebody in a building read it out in a field once and that is all I want. Not the tank. The tank is a tank. I want the writing down.**"

Nobody answered him and he did not appear to want anybody to.

---

**And here is the thing that went wrong in that room, and it went wrong in the ordinary way, and it is the way it has gone wrong in this county for a hundred and forty years.**

There were twenty-two households. There were about fourteen people in that room and four more in two rooms, and one woman of eighty-four who was in none of them and was in the yard if she was anywhere, and there was a child of nine asleep on a coat and a child of eleven in the next house and a man of nineteen in front of him.

"And who signs for her," said Owen Rusk, and he said it to the room and not to anybody.

Nobody answered for about four seconds.

"**You do not,**" said Prud Naylor. "**There is no form in this county that lets anybody sign for a person who cannot answer, and there is no person in this room who can answer for the woman at the end house, and she has a mind and she has said things this week and none of them are about this, and if you write down that she wants a thing she will find out in a year that she wanted it, and that is how a place like this one gets its water taken off it.**"

"That is correct," said Owen Rusk, "and I am not going to do it, and I want it written in the book in the same hand as the answers and not as a note, because a note is a thing somebody can lose."

He wrote it. He wrote, in the back of the tide-table sheet after the head and after the order in which he had asked a question: *No person in this room may answer for a person who cannot answer. The person at the end house has not been asked and is not counted here.*

"That is the first thing I have written in that book," he said, "and it is the only thing in it that is mine."

---

**He worked the five parts out on the long table at about six that evening with Prud Naylor's tank book shut in front of him and a sheet from a tide table and a pencil, and he wrote them out at the bottom of the sheet, and it is the only part of that day that anybody could check.**

The five parts are enumerated once in this county and they were enumerated in a hall in Lower Lock in the month of rain and a person in a building wrote them out afterwards and has never forgotten them. Two unrelated living witnesses. A current care act. A named local appeal route. A named place at which the thing is kept. A named person who keeps it.

Slackwater had two of the five, and then it had three, and by the time he had been in the yard three times it had four, and he wrote them down in that order because the order is the order of how hard they are to get.

Two unrelated living witnesses: **the tank book**, which is in five hands going back to the Concord Year 469, and which is a current local act because the tank is worked twice a day and a person in each household turns the wheel and the book has the day's number against it. That is a repeatable act performed in public at a door, and there are two families in it who have never spoken about anything, and the two of them are the witnesses.

A current care act: **the cart on the Tuesday and the Friday**, which takes the woman of eighty-four to and from the tank and the water, and which is a care act with a day on it and a place and two people.

A named place at which the thing is kept: **the tank house, and it is a building, and it is not a dwelling, and it has one window.**

A named person who keeps it: **Prud Naylor, and she has kept the book for nineteen years and she is sixty-one and she has said that she is not going to do it at seventy.**

And the missing one, and it is the one he had gone up the track to find and it is the one he did not find: **a named local appeal route.** A person or a room or a counter where a household in this yard could take something and be answered. There is a form for it in this county. There is a form for it in this county for Lower Lock and Lower Lock has not got one either, and the reason is that the route is a Registry function and the Registry does not pay for it and never has, and the difference between a settlement that can be appealed for and a settlement that cannot is about four pounds a year in a schedule somewhere in Concord Hall, and the two words *custody* and *route* are the same two words in the two different places this county cannot afford them.

"You have written four," said Prud Naylor, over his shoulder. "**And you are going to go away and the fifth one is the only one we cannot do, and I have known that since the eighth of October and so had you before you sat down.**"

"Yes."

"**Then why did you come up the track.**"

"Because the other four are the only four that exist," said Owen Rusk, "and if I do not come up the track then there is nobody in this county who has ever heard of four of the five, and I am the officer of the record of the district returns and there is going to be a return made in that building for the season of 490, and the fifth column in it has to be completable."

"**The fifth column,**" she said, "**is in a building two miles down the hill and I have not seen it and I will not, and I do not know what is in it. You will tell me or you will not, and either way there is nothing I can do about it, and you have come up a mile of the worst track in this county to tell a woman of sixty-one that her tank is on a list. It is on a list. I have known it was on a list since the eighth of October. What I did not know is what the list was called.**"

"It is not called anything," said Owen Rusk. "**It is a head in a ledger, and the head is the name of your tank, and it is eleven years old, and it was written by a man who was not asked anything, and it is the only thing about this place in this county that anybody ever wrote down, and it is a line in an arithmetic.**"

Prud Naylor looked at the tank book on the table for a while.

"**Then we are on a line,**" she said, "**and I would like to be told that it is a line and not a door, because a door can be shut and a line cannot. I have had eleven years of it being a line and it has been the best thing that has ever happened to this yard, and I will not pretend I have not noticed that, and I will not stand in a room and call it a good thing either, and I would like somebody to write down both of those sentences in whatever it is you are writing in.**"

He wrote down both of them, in that order, in his own hand, and the day and the hour.

---

**On the Thursday morning of the fourth day he went down the track and up the hill, because the marker goes up on the Thursday and the Friday at seven forty, and because the ward reads its week out in public at ten past six in the evening and it is the only day of the week on which anybody in Lower Lock can be shown to have done anything at all, and it is not his day and he does not stand at the front of it.**

The pump had been primed at four in the morning by Bram Tillet and the household in the two rooms in Fetter Lane, eleven minutes, and the first stroke had brought up the water that was standing in the pipe and it was brown, and the girl in that household had carried the second bucket up the lane without a lamp because the lane knows the way. The channel had been done on the Wednesday night of the third and had not been done by anybody who was paid anything this year.

He stood at the back of the ward hall at ten past six in the evening on the Thursday with his hands behind his back and Ruth Mapp read the roster out in the ordinary voice, and about nine doors were read, and the ninth was the eleventh door on a schedule attached to a certification and was not counted, and the crew's wage is four months and a bit in arrears and is the crew's wage and may not be migrated to the district office.

Ada Rusk was at the front because Ada Rusk is at the front, and she did not look at him once in about eleven minutes, and after it she said one thing and it was about the pump and not about him.

"**The Fetter Lane pump is going to fail before the first frost, not because it is old but because there is no grant attached to it and a thing with no grant behind it gets no parts, and I have written to the district office about it, and the district office has answered, and the answer is a form.**"

"What is on the form."

"**That a district may survey its own book at any time it likes and nothing prevents it,**" she said. "**I have read that answer to about fourteen people and every one of them has said the same sentence, which is that the pump house is not a book.**"

---

**He was back up the track at about four in the afternoon on the Friday and there was a letter on the long table in the room with eight feet by eleven of it, and it had been folded twice and it was wet at one corner, and it was not signed by Owen Rusk and it was not written by him.**

"There is one of those on the Saturday," said Prud Naylor. "**I have not read it and I will not, and I would like it understood that I did not read it. It is not addressed to you. It is addressed to a Warden of the Census Guard, and there is no settlement here and there never has been one, and a letter is not a settlement and it does not make one, and I have had it eleven hours and I have not sent it and I am not going to.**"

"Who wrote it."

"Jos Harker wrote it and a man of about sixty named Caws has put his name under his and I have made him, and I have read the address twice and I am telling you what it says and not what is in it, because it is not mine and I gave my word an hour ago and I do not break it in the same hour."

"To the Warden at the count room," said Owen Rusk.

"To the Warden, at the Glass Stair, in care of the count room," said Prud Naylor, "**and it is a request, it is not a claim, and I made him write the word request and not the word claim, and I made him strike out a sentence about the tank, and he did not want to and he struck it out.**"

"Why did you make him strike that out."

"**Because the Warden is the only person in this county who can put a person under a suspension for fourteen days and protect them,**" said Prud Naylor, "**and a person who asks for that is asking to be counted as somebody who cannot answer for themselves, and there is no column anywhere in this county that says what happens to a person in that position afterwards, and I will not have my name under a request that has that in it, and neither has he, and he is forty-six and he has been at that wheel since he was thirty-seven.**"

---

**The letter went down the track on the Saturday morning of the sixth day of the fourth month, in the boot of a man of nineteen, on a bus that does not run to the Glass Stair, and then on his legs, and it took all day, and it asked for the emergency suspension of fourteen days to be entered for six persons at a settlement which is not a settlement of record, and it named the six, and against each of the six it named a care act, and the six were four who cannot be asked and two who can be and asked.**

And Owen Rusk did not write it, did not read it, did not tell anybody in a building that it existed, and did not go and find a person with a slate to say that a document was coming up the road, because a document is a thing a building receives and a person who tells a building what is coming has decided what it will be before it arrives.

**He went down the track instead, at about four, and he was stopped about two hundred yards short of the stile by a man with a chain and a rule and a book, who was extremely polite, and who said that he was not asking, and that he had a round, and that a round is a round.**

"I am not asking," Owen Rusk said.

"**I know you are not, sir, and I have not asked either, and I will say that this is the second time this year I have said that to a person from that building and both times it was true, and I have a book and it is in Concord Hall and it is not copied and nobody in this county will ever see it, and I am not saying that for a reason. I am saying it because you have not asked and I would like the not-asking to be a thing that happened.**"

He wrote it down at the top of the track on the back of a sheet from a tide table, because the book was full of the other four days, and the day and the hour, and the words, and the fact that the man had said it. It is in a longhand book on a shelf in a room he has the key to, and it is not in a district file, and he has never found a reason to move it from one to the other and has not looked for one.

**The man of nineteen came back up the track at about seven on the Saturday evening with the letter gone, and he came up it to say one thing and then he went straight down again, and it took him about forty seconds.**

"He asked me three things at the counter," he said. "**Whether I was from the place, whether I was one of the six, and whether anybody had put the six in a book. And I said yes, yes, and no. And then he read it and he wrote on it and he gave it back and he said it would go up to the Warden in the morning.**"

"Do you know what he wrote on it."

"**No,**" said the man of nineteen. "**And I did not ask, and I want that written down as a thing I did not do, because I know what it is like when a building puts a line through your name and you find out about it in a year. He wrote something in the middle of a page and he turned it round and showed me nothing and gave it back.**"

Somebody inside said "**Caws**" and the man of nineteen said "**He is sixty-one and he can read and he came with me to the road and he stood there while I said the three things and he did not say anything and he is the one whose name is second on it.**"

"And the sixth," said Owen Rusk.

"**The sixth is the woman of eighty-four, and Jos wrote her down as nobody may answer, because that is what she is, and I told him to change it, and he would not, and he said that if he wrote her a name then in about four years a person in a building would be able to say that she had been asked, and she has not been asked, and that is the whole of what I came up here to say.**"
