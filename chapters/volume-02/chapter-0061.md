# Chapter 61 — The Week, and Not the Day

The reconciliation of a settlement on a published schedule is not given a day.

Owen Rusk worked that out on the Sunday morning of the thirteenth day of the first month of the Concord Year 488, standing in a ward hall in Fetter Lane with a slate in his hand and about nine people in the room, and it took him about two minutes, and then he had to stand there for another four with nothing to do with his face.

A schedule of settlements to be reconciled names eleven places in this district and gives against each of them a year and a figure, and in a fifth column that was printed before anybody surveyed anything, **the week of the season in which it falls to be reconciled.** A day-book is posted for the season and is added to on every day of the season, and the day-book has a line for each settlement reconciled **with the day**, and the day is written on the line at the moment the stone comes down out of the case. There is no third document. There is no letter. There is a printed sheet that says *third week* against the fourth of the eleven, and a board in a field with a gap in it, and a woman with a slate who writes the day down when it happens.

**So a ward on a schedule is given a week, and not a day, and it finds out which day on the day.**

He had known this in the ordinary way, in the way a man knows that a parcel comes when it comes. He had not known it as a thing that had been designed, and the reason he had not known it was that he had spent the last four days reading a column of reasons off a board and had not once read the printing at the head of the sheet, which is the printing that tells you when a place is coming.

He put the slate down on the long table and said it out loud, in the ordinary voice, without the day's news in it first, because the day had been in the room already and he was not going to be the man who brought it in small.

---

Ada Rusk had the terms written out before he got there.

Not on the ward's paper. On the back of the return of premises that had come back marked *incomplete* in the second week of the season and had never been answered, and she had ruled four lines across it in her own capitals, and she put it on the table face down and did not turn it over.

"You are going to want to read it before I say anything," she said. "Do not. I have had it since Friday and I have read it to four people and one of them was Marguerite and one of them was the mill's factor and one of them was a man of nineteen who said it was the best paper he had ever read and he is wrong, because it is four lines and it is the same four lines every time."

"It is turned over," said Owen Rusk.

"It is turned over because you have a card and a card makes you careful," said Ada Rusk. "You will do it in a minute. Turn it over and read it and then sit down."

He turned it over and read it and sat down.

**ONE. This district will answer at the table at which it answered the first count, in the same hand and to the same four columns, and it will produce what it produced and no more, and it will not be asked to produce anything else this season.**

**TWO. No person of the Registry speaks for this district. Not the officer of the record of the district returns. Not the examiner. Not a clerk at a counter. If a person of the Registry is asked a question in that room, the answer is that the person asked is the officer of the record of the district returns and is not speaking for this district.**

**THREE. The figure that goes in the reason box goes on the public board, with this district's name against it, whatever it is, and whether we like it or not, and before we see it.**

**FOUR. We will be ready on any day in the week the count gives us. We will not be told which day. Nobody in this district is to go up to that field to ask which day.**

Nobody said anything for a few seconds. There is a stove in that hall and it ticked.

"The third one is new," said Ada Rusk. "The first three weeks of the season I have been the sister who brings a paper. I have decided I would like to be the district that asks for a number about itself before it is written, because there is a fourth column on that board now and it went up on the seventh of this month and it took an afternoon and it cost the county nothing, and it is the first thing anybody in this city has put on a public board in my lifetime and I am not going to be the district that complains about it afterwards."

"You want to be a row on a board," said Owen Rusk.

"I want to be a row on a board that somebody had to write," said Ada Rusk. "There is a difference and you know what it is, because you are the man who wrote it, and if you say I am being clever I will put the slate down."

"I was not going to say that."

"You were going to say something worse and you have decided not to." She turned the sheet round. "Now the second one. That is the expensive one and I want it in your own hand and not mine, and I am going to tell you why and then I am going to let you say no to it, because a term I have not explained is a trick and I do not do tricks to my own brother in a hall in front of nine people."

---

She had it worked out and it took her about four minutes and she did it in the flat voice she uses for a roster.

"Everyone in this county now knows your name and nobody in this county knew it on the seventh of this month," said Ada Rusk. "You put a column on a board that a man from this ward's own boiler-room stood in front of on the Friday, and Cobb Rennie is a man of fifty-eight and he stood in front of it and asked what the eleven was, and he is going to be asked that again by people who are not him. And you are the officer of the record of the district returns of this district. That is not a thing anybody voted for. It is a thing you are.

"So on the twenty-first of this month there is going to be a room with a stone in it and about a dozen people in it, and there is going to be a slate and a man of sixty from the count, and there is going to be a woman from Concord Hall or there is not going to be anybody from Concord Hall, and either way the room is a room in which the officer of the record of this district's returns is standing up, holding the only book anybody in it can check anybody else's book against.

"And the moment you open your mouth in that room about this ward, this ward has stopped being a district answering a count and become a district with an officer. And in about nine years there will be a form in a drawer and a person will read the file and find a Registry man's words in it, and the words will be correct, and the words will be this ward's words, and there will be no way anybody in this county can ever separate them again, because you and me and this hall are the same family and that is not an argument, it is a fact about the Fetter Lane name."

Owen Rusk sat at the end of the long table and did not say anything at all for about as long as it takes a person to understand a thing that is going to cost him something later.

Marguerite Bray read it back twice, as is the third clause of the rule they work by, and then she wrote on the back of her own copy in her inward column, in her own hand, and read that out too:

**Ruled the thirteenth day of the first month, in the hall, with nine persons present, that the second of these four terms is a term and not a request, and that it is the only one of the four that costs anybody in this room anything, and that the person it costs is not in this district.**

Nobody looked at Owen Rusk, which was the correct way of doing it.

"I am not being asked to do it," said Ada Rusk. "I am telling you that if you do not do it, this district will be a thing with an officer in it by Christmas. You can say no to it and I will read your no back into the book and rule it, and it will be your no and not my asking. I have thought about it for two days and I have made a list of the reasons and there are four and two of them are the same reason."

"Which two are the same reason."

"That you are good at it," said Ada Rusk, "and that it works. Those are one reason and it is the reason we have had eleven weeks of."

---

It went up to the Glass Stair on the Tuesday morning of the fifteenth in the ward's bag from the hall door, with a copy, and Owen Rusk walked down with it because he had to be at the count room anyway, and the boat was not going and he got it there by ten.

The count room at the Glass Stair is on the ground floor at the back and it is open from the first working hour to the last and anybody may go into it, and on the Tuesday morning of the fifteenth of the month there were four people in it and one of them was the woman with the slate, who was not there for the count and never was, and who had a slate and four figures on it and no name on the board.

"Lower Lock," said Owen Rusk. "The fourth row. I have a document from the district in its own hand with four terms on it and I am not the person who may speak in the room."

"Terms," said the woman with the slate, and looked at it, and read it, and gave it back. "That is the first document in this season that anybody has handed me which is not asking for something."

"Two of them are."

"Two of them are," she agreed, "and three is not asking for anything, and I have been looking at that board every Wednesday for two weeks and I will tell you what I think three is. Three is a district saying *put me in the public column before I am in the public column.* I have been doing that board nine years and in nine years nobody has asked to be on it before they were on it. They have all been on it for about four hours before they found out they were on it, and then they have come and asked me questions, and every one of them has asked me a question about the number and not one of them has asked me a question about the reason, and there is no reason in the column." She put the pencil behind her ear. "That is a district that has read the column. Where did it learn to read columns."

"It has a sister who cannot leave a pump."

"Ah," said the woman with the slate.

---

The form for a challenge is a single sheet printed one side and it is item fourteen, and it asks four things, and Owen Rusk read it at the end of the second desk on the Tuesday afternoon of the fifteenth and put it face down and did not fill it in, and the reason he did not fill it in is that on that day he did not have the ground.

He had the instrument. He had a place eleven miles up a river that was struck out of the count of this district in the Concord Year 484 with a correction made by the Provincial Counting Office and attested by a warden, with a schedule of premises attached to it, four of them, a date, and a reason, and the reason was a figure, and the figure was four.

He had the qualification. A Continuance Examiner of the Count for Docketford district, entered on the fourth day of this month, may examine a reconciliation, and may challenge the arithmetic, the procedure, and **the currency of any paper the reconciliation rests upon**, and may not examine the schedule, may not examine a choice of places, and may not put a word in the reason column.

And the currency of the paper was the third limb, and the third limb was the only one of the three he had never used, and the woman of about fifty from Concord Hall had told him on the third day of the month, in a room with high windows, that he would spend his whole term finding errors in arithmetic and that every one of them would be about a figure and not one of them about a person's water.

He had a third limb and a settlement that was four years old.

He did not open the notebook that does not go on a table. He wrote one line on the back of the blank challenge form instead, in pencil, and put the form in the drawer, and the line is the whole of what he had at six o'clock on the fifteenth of the month:

*The correction of 484 is a correction of 484. Ask what carries it into 488. Nobody has asked.*

---

The schedule was wired up on the Wednesday morning, and it was the eighth day of the month, a Wednesday, and the woman who maintains the board had a ladder out and there were about nine people at the frame, and Owen Rusk was one of them, and he read the fifth column, which is the printing shop's hand, and which was the printing that tells you when a place is coming, and it took him about four seconds.

**Third week. Lower Lock.**

The third week of the season is the week of the fifteenth of the month, and the day-book, which had been up since the first working day, had four lines on it and would not have five until somebody did something in a room.

**Owen Rusk stood at a board in a field behind the tanneries for about two minutes and did not say anything, and Ada Rusk came down the hill on the Friday morning to look at it and read it in about the time it takes to read a menu, and said one word.**

"Twenty-first," she said.

"Yes."

"Monday," said Ada Rusk. "It is a Monday. I have known it was a Monday since I got off the boat, because I have been doing the rota for eleven weeks and every Monday in that week is a channel night or a bin night, and there is a crew on that rota and the crew can be in two places at nine o'clock in the morning and one of them is here."

She looked at the board for a while.

"You will be in it," she said.

"Yes. The recitals say a reconciliation is conducted in the presence of the officer of the record of the district returns out of which it is taken. I am the officer of the record of the district returns of this district."

"You will be in it and you will not speak," said Ada Rusk, "and in four minutes somebody will ask you a question and the answer will be that you are the officer of the record of the district returns and you are not speaking for this district, and I want you to practise it on the way up because I have watched you explain yourself in rooms and you are about four words too long."

"How long is the answer."

"That is the answer," said Ada Rusk. "You have said it wrong twice. Say it shorter."

Owen Rusk said it shorter.

He walked up Fetter Lane at about ten past ten in the morning on the eighteenth of the month with four words in his mouth and the eleven-day notice running out behind him, because the season's notice had been out since the first and there is nothing on any form in this county that says when it stops, and the day-book is a week and not a day, and the fourth row was eleven doors and a figure, and he was going to be in a room on the twenty-first of the month with a stone in it and he was not going to be permitted to speak.

At the top of the lane there were two notices on a post. The first was four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and the words *any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it*, and it had been up in the weather since the spring, and the second was the counting season's notice with eleven names on it, and neither of them had changed, and he read the first one because he always read the first one, and did not read the second one at all, because he has read it nine times and could say it.

There is no seventh renewal of anything in this ward. There is no second column anywhere in this county in which a place is given a reason in words. And on the Monday of the twenty-first of the month he is going to be in a room he is not allowed to speak in, and a man of sixty is going to put a stone on a table and take it out again, and a woman of about thirty with a slate is going to write down the day.

He went down to the field after his tray, because he had four things left on it and the field was on the way, and at about a quarter to six in the evening he read the fourth column for the tenth time since the ninth of the month, and the ten reasons were still the premises column written twice, and the eleventh was still a figure of four doors of nine, and underneath them all the standing extract was still four hundred and six rows long with a hundred and forty years of struck places in it and the year four hundred and sixty-one in the third row from the bottom.

He read the heading of the extract and one row, and stopped, and went up the hill, and did the tray, and was on it at about ten past seven, and was not late.
