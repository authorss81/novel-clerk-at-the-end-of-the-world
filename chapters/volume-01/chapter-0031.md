# Chapter 31 — The Office Answers the Column

The hall was empty at half past eight on the Saturday morning of the fortieth day of the month, and the sheet was on the table by the door, and it had been on the table by the door for seven days, and it had gone from a thing people read to a thing people stood near.

That was the whole of the change and he had watched it happen from about nine feet away, four mornings out of five, without ever once being in the room when it happened. On the first day a man read it standing up and went away. On the third day two people read it together and one of them said something about the word *competent*. By the seventh there were people in the queue who had clearly never intended to read it and were standing at the table because the queue was at the table, and Owen had understood, somewhere in the middle of that week, that a thing on a public table is not a document with readers. It is furniture, and furniture gets used.

He had come down at half past eight for one reason, and the reason was arithmetic rather than principle. An office answers a petition on a working day and posts it on the next day the counter is open, and a Saturday counter is open for matters with a date on them, and this matter had no date on it. So it would come on a Saturday, and it would come down out of a room he had never been inside, and it would be put on a table at some hour between ten and eleven, and if he was standing at the table when it was put there then a room full of people would watch a piece of paper arrive.

He could not shape a word of it. He had understood that on the Friday, lying awake, and had gone over it in the dark in the way he went over everything, and the understanding was this: the Office had been writing that answer since the Wednesday, because the Office had been told about the sheet on the Monday by somebody who had read it, and the Office's practice with a document that has been printed in a public room is to answer it, because answering is free and correcting is not, and a free answer that is correct costs nothing and cannot be withdrawn.

All he could do was be in the room when it landed.

---

He went up first, because he had two things to try and he wanted to be rid of them before ten.

The copying floor on a Saturday morning is the loneliest room he has ever been paid to stand in. The press was cold. Sixteen hundred sheets of clean copying paper were drying on the racks in bars of grey light off the high windows, and the smell was cold ink and hot dust, and he was the only person in it.

The first thing was a **request for a hearing on the comparison**.

He wrote it out in longhand on a sheet of the copying kind, in the correct form, with the docket number at the top and the day and the hour under it, because a request without a day and an hour is a thing nobody can file, and because he wanted, this once, to be unarguable.

*In the matter of petition 487-GS-19, and of a comparison posted at the counter of this hall on the thirty-third day of the month, the petitioner requests a hearing upon the comparison so posted.*

Then he read it back, and it took him about ninety seconds to work out that he had just handed the Office the only thing it wanted.

A hearing is a date. A date is chosen by the person who grants it. And the subject of a hearing is set by the terms on which it is entered, and the terms on which a hearing is entered are the terms the person who asks for it supplies, unless somebody objects.

If Owen asked for a hearing *on the comparison*, then the comparison was the matter, and the matter was a table with a heading on it and one hundred and nine lines in it and forty-one of a particular disposition, and forty-one people who had been told nothing would be standing in a room in place of the forty-one people who had actually been refused something. And the Office would grant it, gladly, in a category of its own choosing, and Lower Lock's petition would be folded into the refusal column of a district office, which is precisely what the refusal column had done to Lower Lock's four requests in the intake year, when a question was answered by receiving a document and nobody in the room decided anything at all.

He read his own sentence again. *The petitioner requests a hearing upon the comparison so posted.*

It was a well-made request. It was in the correct form. It had the docket number and the day and the hour. And it was the transfer's own trick, one level up, and he was the man who had learned to see it standing at a table with two leaves on it.

He put it in the tray drawer, unsent, and wrote on the outside of it, in pencil, in his own hand, the date and the words *not sent — wrong instrument*, because in about four months he would want to be able to point at it and say that he had known.

The second thing was a point of law, and it took him about four minutes to kill it, and the killing took four minutes because he had to go and find out whether it was dead.

He found Junia Pell at the second desk at about a quarter to nine, standing at a tray with her coat on over her clothes, which is what a clerk does who has come in on a Saturday for a dated matter and is not going to be thanked for it.

"I am not going to put a second point of law in this matter," he said.

"Good."

"I want to hear why from you and not from myself."

"That is a new thing to want." She put the pen down. "Say what you were going to do."

"I was going to put a second point of law on the same words as the first — whether a district office may maintain a disposition without a reason — and I was going to put it in my own name in the same docket, and I was going to argue that the precedent's fourth ground is a ground and not a preference and therefore a column of dispositions without reasons does not satisfy it."

"And the answer you already have on the docket says what?"

"It says a sub-table is not a district office and a county register is not an office record and the sub-table is not subject to the precedent. It is six sentences. It is correct. It is in the file."

"Right," said Pell. "So you would be putting the same point in a second form in the same quarter in the same docket, in a matter where the office has already answered it once, in writing, correctly. And here is what will happen, and it is not a guess, it is how this building works. The second point will be answered. The answer will be a different paragraph, and it will be better than the first, because they will have had a day. And then the two paragraphs will be read side by side by anybody who comes after us, and what the two paragraphs will show is not that the office cannot answer. It will show that a man asked twice."

She picked the pen up and then put it down again.

"You have one good answer in a file and you were about to make it look like a campaign. Which office can answer. Which office can answer in four days, and will enjoy it, because there is a difference between an office that is asked a hard question once and an office that is asked the same question twice by the same man, and the difference is about you and not about them."

"That is the second time this month you have stopped me using a thing that works."

"It is the second time this month I have watched you get ready to use a thing that works." She looked at him properly. "Go and be in the room. Whatever they put on that table this morning, it will be correct, and it will be correct in about ninety seconds, and there is not one thing in it you can hold up, and it will say the thing it has to say, and by Tuesday it will be the answer to the question and by Christmas it will be the form."

---

He was at the table by the door at ten minutes to ten and there were four people in the hall.

The answer came down at four minutes to ten, and it came down in the hands of a runner from the district office, who put it on the table beside Owen's own sheet without a word, and squared it to the edge of the table with two fingers, and went away.

It was one sheet. It had a heading on it and five clauses under the heading and a signature line, and the signature line was a stamp, and the stamp was the district office's, and there was no name on it anywhere.

Owen read it twice standing up.

*Answer of the district office, in petition 487-GS-19, upon the matter of a comparison posted at the counter of this hall on the thirty-third day of the month.*

*That a disposition is a decision. That a decision is entered in this office's column with the officer's initials and the date of its making. That a disposition is not a reason and is not a record of a reason. That this office does not maintain a column of reasons and is not required by any instrument in force in this district to maintain one. That the disposition "No district office competent" is a statement of the reach of this office at the date of its making, and is not a finding upon any person, place or work.*

Then there was a sixth line under the fifth, in the same hand, and it was not part of the paragraph.

*The office notes that a comparison posted at a public counter is not a filing, and that the office has answered it as information.*

It was correct.

Owen Rusk stood in a public waiting hall in Docketford and went through it clause by clause, the way he had gone through two hundred and twenty-four words in this building three weeks ago, looking for the thing that was false, and he did not find it, and it took him about a minute and a half, and there were seven people in the hall by then and two of them were reading over his shoulder.

It was correct because a disposition genuinely is a decision. It was correct because a decision genuinely is entered with initials and a date. It was correct because this office genuinely does not keep a column of reasons, and genuinely is not required by anything in force in this district to keep one, and Owen had spent a fortnight in the record room two floors below the counters and had read the Schedule of Instruments in Force with his own eyes and there was nothing in it about reasons. And it was correct, and it was complete, and it was the whole of the Office's position, stated in five clauses, on one sheet, in ninety seconds of a careful man's morning.

And it restored nothing. It explained nothing. It did not say a word about a sub-table, or about a count, or about the eighth day of the month of the intake year, or about nine refusals against a named place, or about forty-one dispositions in a quarter that had been made by going down a column of a form.

It said, correctly, that the office does not keep reasons.

Which is a different sentence from *here are the reasons*, and it is the same sentence as *there is nothing to be had*, and it is a sentence about what the Office has rather than about what the Office did, and it is written in the passive voice on purpose, and a Registry of Continuant Works has been writing sentences in the passive voice on purpose since the Concord Year 1.

And it was going to be the answer to this question, and the answer to this question next quarter, and the answer to this question in every quarter for as long as the building stood, because it was not an answer. It was a position. And a position is a thing an office can keep in a drawer and bring out again.

---

He read it out.

That was the whole of what he did, and he did it in the ordinary way, and there was no speech in it and he had not written one out.

He stood at the side of the table and read the five clauses aloud at the pace of a man reading a menu, twice, and then he read the sixth line, and then he said: "It is on the table. It is the office's answer. It is on the same table as the sheet, and you may take a copy of it, and if you want the four sheets it answers they are at the counter and may be inspected by anybody in this room."

Nine people read it. He counted, because he could not help counting, and he counted twice and got the same number both times, and the ninth was a woman of about sixty with a basket who had been in the queue for something else entirely and who read the whole of it standing up.

Then he stopped, and put his hand on the edge of the table for a second — the thing he had picked up in four weeks from two different people in two different rooms and had taken his hand off the last time because it was not his table — and then he took it off again.

And he did not print anything else. That was the whole of the discipline of the morning and it cost him more than the argument would have: he had three sheets in his coat. He had drafted the second one on the Friday night, a comparison of the six disposition forms in the quarter, which would have gone on the same table on the same morning and which was *good*, and which would have been a second thing on a public table in the same week, and the only difference between the two sheets would have been that one of them was his and one of them was not, and a room that has seen a man print twice in a week is a room that has learned to expect it.

So he left it in his coat and took it home and put it in the tray drawer with the unrouteable correspondence, where it is, and where a thing goes that is not going anywhere.

---

Pell found him at the door at about half past ten with her coat still on.

"You read it out," she said.

"Yes."

"Not to the room. To whoever wanted to hear it."

"That is the only way there is."

"It is." She was quiet for a moment. "You understand that you are not going to be able to take any of it back."

"I have understood that since about a third past nine this morning and I have had two hundred sheets of copying to think about it in."

"Then here is the one thing I did not tell you on the thirty-third, and it is one thing and it is short." She looked back at the table, which had nine people around it and was going to have fifteen by noon. "There is a **schedule of duties** in the district office. It is on the board by the second counter. Anybody may read it, it is not a secret, and nobody reads it, because it is a list of what the building does to itself and a building is not interested in that. The person I told you about is on it, in a column, against a duty."

"And I am not going to go and read it."

"You are not going to go and read it, and I want you to hear the reason from me rather than work it out on your own, because you will work it out on your own and it will take you two days and cost you a night." Pell said it flatly. "A name in a schedule of duties is not a person in a case. It is a person who is going to walk into that building on Monday morning at the same hour she always does, and she has never in her life had anybody ask her a question, and there is not one instrument in this city that turns a man knowing a thing into a man who is owed something. The only thing it turns you into is a man with an enemy who has not been told she has one."

"Who has never heard of you," said Pell, "and who on Monday will come in and find that a sheet with a heading on it has been standing by a public door for seven days with four words from their own form on it, and that a queue has built up in front of it, and that the district office has had to answer it. And that person will not be angry with you, Rusk. That person will be frightened, in about the way that a good craftsman is frightened of a machine that does the same work better than he does, and frightened people in this building write better forms. So you will get what you wanted. And it will not be what you thought you wanted, and I am telling you that now on a Saturday so that you do not spend a month congratulating yourself."

She put her hood up.

"Also," she said, "you have made yourself a thing that happens in this hall on Saturdays. Do you understand what that is? You have made yourself a standing item. Whatever is on that table next Saturday, and the Saturday after, a room is going to look at the door to see whether there is anything on it from you. That is worth an enormous amount and it is not worth what you think it is worth, and what you have actually lost is that a room will now tell you what it thinks before it tells you what it knows."

Owen Rusk stood in the doorway of the Glass Stair at half past ten on the fortieth day of the month and understood two things at once, and understood that they were not the same size, and that he had spent a month learning that being right in a room and being useful in a room are different products, and that the second one requires somebody else to be holding the paper.

Being right had required him to stand at a table with a sheet on it and a card propped against it and a queue in front of it, and he had done that, and it had cost him a name in a schedule of duties that he was never going to be able to use.

Being useful was going to require somebody else to pick up a piece of paper in a room two miles upriver, and it was not going to be a Registry clerk with a good coat, and he had known that since the thirty-first of the month when a woman had said it to him in front of twenty-two people, and it had taken him nine days to be told it again by somebody who worked for the building.

---

He went up the hill at half past eleven in the rain, and there was no marker on the top path, because the marker goes in on a Thursday and a Friday and on nothing else in the week, and the pump had been last primed on Thursday and had held, and the standpipe at the top of Fetter Lane was running, and the hall was shut because a hall is shut on a Saturday unless somebody has business.

Ada Rusk was in the shed at the top of Fetter Lane with the lamp out and her hands in a bucket of tar, and she did not stop working when he came in, and she did not ask what he wanted, and he stood in the doorway of a corrugated lean-to with a wet coat on for about ninety seconds and worked out that he had come up a hill to ask a question and that the question he had come up with was the wrong one and had been the wrong one since Wednesday.

"I have come to ask whether the ward will hold a second sheet," he said.

Ada Rusk put both hands up out of the tar and wiped them on her thighs, which took longer than it should have because there was tar in the creases.

"No," she said.

"You have not heard what I was going to ask."

"You were going to ask whether we will put a paper of our own on a table in Docketford." She put the lid back on the bucket. "And the answer is no, and not because we are proud. The answer is that we have got one document and it took three weeks and it is in a hall on Fetter Lane and anybody can walk up and read it against our own book. The day that document is on a table in Docketford it is a ward document in a room where nobody in the room has ever carried water, and the two things are not the same size and everybody in this ward will know which one it is and so will you."

"Then what do you want me to ask."

"I have got a question and it has been sitting on me since the Friday." She turned round. "It is not a question for a hall in Docketford. It is a question about money and it is ours and it has been ours for a month, and it is written in Mrs Bray's book in her own hand in front of twenty-two people, and it says *and the wage*, and under it *wage not funded*, and she wrote the second line on purpose so that the room agreeing to the first one would have to read the second one."

"I know. I was there."

"Then here is what has happened to it in a month." Ada Rusk stood with her back against the bench. "It has become a line in a book. On Thursday a woman from a landing upriver came and read the roster aloud and wrote in her own hand that the wage is not funded and that she will not soften it, and that line is now in a stranger's own book as well as in ours, and that is the best thing that has happened to that wage in four months. And nothing has happened to the wage. It is not funded. It was not funded on the thirty-first and it is not funded tonight and it will not be funded on Friday, and the whole of what a month of the best work of your life has produced is that a wage which is not funded is now legible."

"Is that what you want to say to me."

"No," said Ada. "I want to say the next part, and I have been up since four and I want to say it to a person who is going to have to carry it. If you print a second sheet, and if the Registry answers it, and if the answer is correct — and it will be correct, it always is, that is the whole of what I have learned in six weeks — then we will have a document about the wage and a document about the refusal column and a document about a bridge, and the wage will be one of three things on a list. And a thing on a list is a thing somebody will look at when they have got to the end of the other two."

She picked the brush up and looked at it and put it down again.

"So the next thing is ours, and it is going to be a paper of our own, in our own words, with my name at the bottom of it, and it is going to say what this ward does on what days at what hours in front of whom, and it is going to say what it does not claim, and it is going to be in our book first and in the capital second. And when it goes into that building you are going to carry it up the stair because that is the job and the job is yours. And you are not going to write a word of it."

Owen Rusk stood in the doorway of a shed at the top of Fetter Lane with a good coat on and three printed sheets in his inside pocket and understood that he had come up a hill to be refused a thing and had been given the whole of the next month instead, and that the person refusing him had been doing it since before he got on the ferry, and that she had a roster in a book and a bin count coming down on Wednesday and a Thursday marker at seven forty and a hearing that did not have a date, and that he had come to the top of Fetter Lane to find out what the ward wanted and had been told, in nine sentences, by the only person in the ward who knew.

"I am not going to write a word of it," he said.

"I know you are not." She turned back to the bucket. "That is why I told you."

At the bottom of the lane the standpipe was running and there was a light in the pump house, and the granary book said two weeks and a bit and had said it on Thursday afternoon in front of a stranger from another district, and the sixteen were still sixteen, and the order was still on a docket with no hearing date on it, and in a public waiting hall two miles downriver there were nine people standing round a table reading five clauses that were entirely true.
