# Chapter 53 — Premises of Record

The form came down on the Thursday morning of the seventy-third day of the month in a bundle with eleven other bundles, because there is one form for every district in the county and the county has eleven districts and the form is the same form and it is a good form.

It was a single sheet, printed both sides, with a heading and a foot and a line for the district and a line for the day and then a table with four columns and eleven ruled lines under it and a space at the bottom of the sheet about two inches deep for anything the district wished to add, which in eleven counties in this season has been used for the word *none* and once for a poem.

**RETURN OF PREMISES FOR THE COUNT. To be made by every district in the county before the first day of the month. The Count is a count of draws, of premises of record, and of works maintained. This return is not the Count. It is the district's own statement of what it has, made in its own hand, and the Count is taken from it and from nothing else.**

And then the four columns, and Owen Rusk read them standing at the end of the second desk on the Thursday morning of the seventy-third day of the month and had to read one of them twice.

**One. The premises.** The name of the place, the street or lane it stands in, and the door.

**Two. The persons at it.** Every person whose name is entered at that premises in the district's roll, and no person who is not.

**Three. The water.** The draw at that premises, by what instrument, under whose maintenance, and the instrument by name.

**Four. For any person at a premises who cannot be asked.** The care act now performed for them, in one sentence, and the name of the person who performs it. *This line may not be left blank. If there is no such person, enter none.*

It is a good form. That was the trouble with it, and Owen Rusk said so out loud in a hall in Fetter Lane on the Sunday of the same week, to about fourteen people, and the sentence he used was: **somebody has spent a long time on this and I would like everybody in this room to notice that before anybody in this room decides to be angry about it.**

---

They could fill in three of the four columns on the Friday and most of the fourth by the Saturday night, and the reason they could is that this ward has been writing down what it does for seven weeks in a form nobody asked for, on a public file, readable at any counter, and the count form asks for four of the things that form already has.

Ada Rusk came down off the hill on the Friday morning boat with the slate and did the premises herself, because it is her crew's ground and nobody else knows which of the thirteen households stands in which building.

**Eleven premises of record for thirteen household references.** Two households are in the same building and have been for years. One of the thirteen has no premises.

That one is the seventh of Marguerite Bray's seven, and Bray has known it for years and has it in the delivery book as a house, and it is not a house. It is a **pitch** at the bottom of the slope at the bottom of Fetter Lane, on ground the Registry has never made into a street, and there has been a seed stall standing on it since before the Concord year was counted from anything else, and a woman of seventy-one keeps it, and her name is on a **household** reference in a steward's book and not on a **premises** reference anywhere in this county, because the pitch has no door.

Column one, line twelve: *the pitch, bottom of Fetter Lane, no door.*

And it went round that hall four times in a day and nobody could fill it in, and the reason nobody could fill it in is that there are only three things a person can put in that line and all three of them are lies.

You can put the address of a building and say the household is there, and the household is not there and everybody knows it and the person who wrote it would know it.

You can put the pitch and leave the door out, and a pitch is not premises of record and cannot be made one by a district writing it on a form, and if it could, the stall would be a premises and the pitch would be a premises and the ground under it would be a premises, and a ward that writes a place against a claim in a column is doing the thing that a dead man's receipt did to this ward in the ninth month of the Concord Year 486.

Or you leave it blank, and the form does not say you may leave it blank, and a blank in a return of premises is read by a counting clerk as a household the district has not accounted for, and the district office's answer to that is a column, and a column is not a repair.

Nobody filled it in. That was the first evening's work and it was the whole of the first evening's work and it took eleven people and a stove and about two hours, and at about nine o'clock on the Friday night, Marguerite Bray ruled a line in the delivery book, and it is in her own hand and it is the sentence this ward is going to send to a city:

*No premises is claimed at the pitch at the foot of this slip. The pitch is not this district's to give away. This district has never made an entry of a place against a claim and is not beginning in the eighth month of a Concord year because somebody has printed a form.*

Ada Rusk read it back, which is the third clause of the rule they work by, and added a second line herself, in capitals, on the slate:

**And the district's paper does not claim the seed stall. It said so in the first week of the month in a room in Docketford and it says it here.**

And then a woman of seventy-one who was in the hall, standing by the fire, said the thing that had been in the room since the Thursday and had not come out.

"Forty-one years I have had that pitch," said Nessa Kray. "And I would like it noticed that what has just been ruled in this hall is the first time anybody in this city has ever written down that it is mine and then declined to write it down anywhere. That is a strange thing to be given and I do not know what to do with it and I am not going to say thank you for it."

Nobody thanked her either.

---

Column two was the easy one and column three was the hard one, and column four was the one the ward could not do at all.

Column two took an hour. Thirteen household references, two hundred and forty people, and the district's roll has had the names in it for thirty-six years in a book in a family, and where the Registry's roll and Bray's book disagree, Bray's book wins and has always won, and there are eleven names in it that the county's roll has not carried since the fourth quarter of the Concord Year 471, and all eleven are in a batch struck in one afternoon by one officer with initials, and **none of them has ever been asked, because a person who does not know that they are named in an entry cannot apply to see it.**

Owen Rusk put the eleven in the second column of a return of premises in his own hand and did not tell the district office where he had got them, and there was no line on the form to put that in, and he had looked for one for about two minutes on the Friday and had not found it and had been, for about four minutes on the Friday, genuinely happy about that.

Column three is where the ward spent the Saturday.

*Water. The draw at that premises, by what instrument, under whose maintenance, and the instrument by name.*

Lower Lock's water is the Fetter Lane pump, and the pump's grant is suspended and has been since the fourteenth day of the month of rain and its own review is not named, and the pump is hand-primed at four in the morning on the nights it does not take, and it took on the second stroke on the Tuesday of the flood week and Sully Dace wrote it in the pump book with the day on it, and the instrument by name is a **pump**, and the grant that maintained it is **suspended**, and there is no column anywhere on this form for a grant that is suspended.

Then the standpipe, at the bottom of the lane, which is fed off the district main, and the district main is Docketford's, and there is a form at the Water Office for a draw, and the draw is by household, and the rota has five names on it: four households and Cobb Rennie, and one of those four households is a household of two people and one of them is twelve.

And the crossing, which is a work of record, and is closed to loads, and is not maintained by anybody, and the maintenance is a notice.

So Ada Rusk wrote column three out in the plainest English anybody in the room could produce, and it is four lines, and it is the truest thing the form ever received, and it says that the district's water is drawn by hand on the worst nights by nine people and one girl of twelve, that the instrument is a pump whose maintenance grant is suspended and has been since the fourteenth of the month of rain, that the standpipe rota is a sheet on a board and not an instrument of this county, and that a man of fifty-eight who is on that sheet is owed a pension that no column in this city will hold.

And the last line of the fourth column, and it is the one the ward argued about for two days.

---

*For any person at a premises who cannot be asked. The care act now performed for them, in one sentence, and the name of the person who performs it. This line may not be left blank. If there is no such person, enter none.*

There are six. Six persons of this district, named on a schedule held in the intake year 486, of whom three are children, who cannot be asked, and for whom this ward has performed a care act every day since the third week of the month in a form nobody asked for, on a public file, in a roster anybody may read at the weir path on any day without notice.

Three of the five things a protective return requires exist in this ward. Two unrelated living witnesses, and there are four people in this hall who could be one and none of them has been asked. A current care act, and there is one, every morning, at four. A named local appeal route, and it is a place and a name and a standing, and the name is a woman who keeps a tide table nineteen years and who is not on the return and who is at the far end of a route and who said no to being on it for a reason she has not given.

**A named place at which the thing is kept. A named person who keeps it.** The last two lines. The custodian. And the two of them are Registry functions, and this city has never once paid anybody for either of them, and the cost of it is five hours a week, every week, for as long as the care situation lasts, and the office may put somebody else in the place without asking this ward, and the hours of the person who was there do not stop.

And the form permits *none*.

Nobody wrote *none* for about six hours. That is not a figure. It is the length of two evenings and a Saturday and most of a Sunday, and it is the length of a ward hall in which a woman of sixty-one refused four separate times to let anybody be named, and a woman of twenty-four put the question in front of the room in the plainest words anybody in Lower Lock has ever used about anybody.

The question was: *will anybody here take it.*

She asked it once, on the Saturday afternoon, to about eleven people, and she asked it in the ordinary voice and she did not ask it twice, and she said before she asked it that it was not a compliment to anybody and was not a favour and that anybody who said yes out of that would be doing her a disservice.

Nobody said anything for about two minutes. There is a stove in that hall and it ticked, and somebody's coat was dripping on the flags.

And Marguerite Bray said, from the end of the table, with the pencil: "No."

And Ada Rusk said: "Then rule what I am going to say, and rule it in capitals, and then I am going to read it back and nobody is to stop me."

And it went into the delivery book, and it is in Bray's hand and it is the sentence that the ward sent up with the return of premises on the Sunday morning, and it is the eighth line of that return, and it is the only line on it in capitals:

**THIS DISTRICT HAS SIX PERSONS IN IT WHO CANNOT BE ASKED AND IS PERFORMING A CARE ACT FOR ALL SIX, AND THERE IS NO PERSON IN THIS DISTRICT WHO KEEPS ANYTHING FOR ANYBODY, AND THE WORD FOR THAT IS NOT WRITTEN BESIDE ANY NAME IN THIS HALL, AND NOBODY IS TO BE ASKED, AND THE COST OF IT IS FIVE HOURS A WEEK AND THAT FIGURE IS ON A PAGE IN THIS ROOM AND IT IS NOT A PERSON.**

---

The letter came up on the Friday.

It came in the bag with the count forms, because somebody in the ward had put it in the bag, and it was in Wenna Sarr's hand and it was four lines long and it was addressed to the River Desk and not to the man, which is the correct address for a letter to a person whose posting you are not supposed to know.

*Mr Rusk. I have read the form at the counter and I have copied it out and I am sending you the copy. The fourth column has a line that says state the person who keeps the thing kept. Nobody is going to write anybody's name on that line and I would like it written down that I have read it and that I know exactly what it says. I am not asking you for anything. I would like it recorded that I have read it. — W. Sarr*

Owen Rusk read it at the end of the second desk on the Friday afternoon and then read it again, and then did the thing he had been not doing for six weeks, which is to open the register he does not put on a table and see whether there was a line in it, and there was not, and the reason there was not is that he had decided on a stage of a run of wet nights in the fourth week of the month not to look up a name in a schedule of duties on a board by the second counter, and had said out loud in a hall that he could find it inside a week and would not, and had been right.

He wrote back four lines, and it took him a long time, and it went into the bag on the Saturday with the return of premises because it had to be witnessed going and a thing that goes in a bag with a ward paper is a ward paper and not a Registry letter.

*Mrs Sarr. I agree with you and I have not looked up the name. There is a name against the duty on a board by the second counter in the district office. I have not looked it up and I am not going to, and the reason is that the person who maintains the forms for that office is not anybody's enemy and has never been anybody's enemy, and she writes better forms than I do because she is frightened, and that is worth more to this ward than I am. The form is right. The line is right. Enter none. That is not a gap in your return. It is the truest thing on the sheet and it is going to Docketford in capitals because a woman of twenty-four wrote it in capitals and a woman of sixty-one ruled it. I am sorry that the answer is a sentence. It is what the answer is. — O.R.*

He did not send it round the hall. He put it in the bag and Ada Rusk said, looking at the four lines from the top of the sheet, "That is the first one of those you have written that does not tell somebody what to think," and gave it back.

The return of premises went up on the Sunday morning of the seventy-sixth day of the month in the ward's bag from the hall door, in a boat, with a copy of the ward's schedule of performed acts behind it and the six names on the fourth column entered as **none**, and the twelfth line of the first column empty, and the eighth line in capitals.

It went up with a line missing on it on purpose, and everybody in that hall knew it, and nobody filled it in, and that is the whole of what Lower Lock did in the week the counting season notice went up on a post at the top of Fetter Lane.
