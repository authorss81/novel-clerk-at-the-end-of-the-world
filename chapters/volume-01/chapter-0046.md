# Chapter 46 — The Certification

He asked the question on the Saturday morning of the sixty-first day of the month at about ten past nine, standing at the bottom of the seal stair, and he asked it badly on purpose, which is the first time in seven weeks he has done anything badly on purpose and the only reason is that he had had one night to work out that he did not know how to ask it well, and had got nowhere in it, and the certification itself is not signed until the Monday.

The question was this. A copy clerk of the River Desk may enter a filing on a supervised tray and may cover papers going up the stair and may sign the entry of himself on a matter as a party, and may not sign a continuation, a certification, a suspension or a hold, and those four words are on the card in his drawer in this building and have been since the third week of the month.

Lower Lock's return had been narrowed on Friday afternoon by the people who live in it, and what was left of it was four duties held by six named persons, each of which one person could do alone, and a scope clause of four words in a bridge mechanic's capitals, and a withdrawal right, and an appeal route with a name at the far end of it, and two observers whose separability this office can hold open indefinitely.

And the crossing's last supported legal status was suspended, under a load restriction that had not been affected by anything anybody had done since the tenth of the month, with an appeal window that runs out, and four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds of repair that nobody in this city is funding.

"Somebody has to hold it," said Owen Rusk. "And the only person I can think of is a copy clerk who lives in the ward, and I would like to know whether that is possible before I go and tell anybody in Lower Lock that it is."

The seal-stair clerk had been on that stair for twenty years and she did not answer him for about four seconds, which is longer than she had ever taken over anything.

"You have asked me the half of it," she said. "You have asked me whether a copy clerk may hold it. You have not asked me the whole of it, and I am going to make you ask me the whole of it, because in about three years somebody will go through that file and find that you had the easy half of this question in it and not the hard one, and it will look like a man who wanted a signature."

"Go on."

"The hard half is not whether you may. **Does an officer certifying a district return have a district.** That is the question and it has an answer and the answer is in item fourteen of a schedule in a book in the district office and I will get the book, because you have read the recitals and you have told me you have read them and you are still asking me, which means you have not worked out which of the recitals is about you."

She went down and came back up with a volume and put it on the ledge of the stair and did not open it, and stood beside it.

"Item fourteen is a second examination. It says that an instrument of a community is examined a second time by an officer having no district, and the whole of the reason, which you have read and which is right, is that the office could not tell on the face of an instrument a duty honestly performed from a status merely asserted, and a duty and a status are told apart by who is standing behind it.

"So the recitals are about the **filer**. A certification is not a filing. There is no filer in a certification. A certification is made by a person, in their own hand, without a seal, and it has no office behind it and no district and no district act, and that is what makes it weak, and that is what makes it the only instrument anybody has ever been willing to hold this crossing's status with, because it can be made by a person and a person can be wrong in public.

"You are not an officer of anything and you hold no seal, and if you were to try to certify that return as an officer of this office you would be doing the thing that is prohibited on your card and I would stop it and I would be within my rights and I would not enjoy it.

"But you asked me whether you are **of the district**. You are. You have been of the district since the month you were born, and you are going to be of it when you are forty, and there is nothing anybody can do about that and there is not supposed to be. And the instrument has a box on the second page and the box says: *the person making this certification is of the district named in it.* That is all. It is one box, it is filled in by the person, in their own hand, and it goes on the file, and the file is not sealed, and every party may read it, and the ward may read it and the office may read it and anybody at a counter may read it, and it cannot be taken off, and a person who has filled in that box may not afterwards say that his interest was not before the people who read it."

"And is that a bar," said Owen Rusk.

"It is a bar on the **second examination** and it is nothing at all else," said the seal-stair clerk. "That is the whole of the answer and it has taken me four years to be able to say it in one sentence, and the sentence is: **a person of the district may make a certification, and may not examine one, and the reason he may not examine one is that he would be examining his own ward, and the reason that is written on a form instead of left to character is that character is a thing this office stopped relying on in the Concord Year 44 for a very good reason.**"

"How does that help us. The ward cannot be examined by anybody who lives in it. The ward wrote that down itself in a letter to a Council in Concord Hall and it is on a public file."

"Yes, and I have read it, and it is the reason this is going to be possible at all," said the seal-stair clerk, "and not the reason it is going to be easy. There are nineteen names on the roll of countersignature in this building and eight of them are officers of other districts, and on a Friday afternoon in a flood season the whole of that roll looks at itself, and the two people in this city who have both already written the worst true thing about themselves in their own ink, more than once, in their own hands, and who have no interest in the subject of that work beyond what they have already put on the table, are a copy clerk on the second floor of this building and a counter clerk at the Mill Ward annex who received the receipt that took the crossing off this ward in the first place.

"You have been the person who reads back what a checker wrote for six weeks, and you were right about that every time, and it is a smaller thing than you think it is, and it is the same shape. Somebody watches. Somebody writes the worst thing they can about themselves. And the thing gets examined by somebody who is not of the ward.

"I am not going to tell you that you are permitted. I am going to tell you that it is a form, and it is on the third landing in a drawer with the other forms, and it is a **certification**, and it is signed by a person and not by an office, and it is witnessed by two persons having no interest in the subject of the work, and it is good for seven days, and it ends at the hour of the seal on the seventh day, and the person who signs it carries whatever is in it that cannot be put in it."

---

They put it in the long room at about half past three on the Monday afternoon, two days after he had asked the question, because that is the room that has the high windows and because a room that has the high windows is a room in which a thing like this can be looked at.

The reviewing officer read the form from the top without being asked to and then asked Owen Rusk the whole of the question, in front of everybody, which is the correct procedure and which Owen had not expected and which cost him about four seconds of standing still.

"Mr Rusk, do you have a district."

"Lower Lock."

"You are of the district named in this return, which is Lower Lock, and you have said so."

"Yes."

"Then read the second page out, and fill the box, and do not ask anybody in this room to help you with it, because a box filled in with help is a box that somebody can be asked about afterwards."

Owen Rusk read the second page out. It is a page about a person, and not about an office, and it says four things: that the person making it has no office in it; that the person making it is of the district named in it, if so; what the person making it has done in the last seven days in the matter of the subject, in plain words, in their own hand; and that the person making it cannot examine it.

He filled the second box in his own hand. It took him about four seconds and it is the only line he has written in seven weeks that is a fact about himself.

Then he came to the third box, which is the one that costs.

*What the person making this certification has done in the last seven days in the matter of the subject, in plain words.*

And Owen Rusk stood in a room with high windows and had to write down, in his own hand, on a public form, the true account of the last seven days of his own life in this case, and there was no way to do it that was not an account of himself, and every version of it that was short was a lie.

He wrote:

*On the fifty-fourth day of the month the river came up and I carried a bucket from the bottom of Bell Row in the dark between four and six in the morning, and that is outside the seven days this sheet asks for, and I have put it down anyway, because a sheet that asks for seven is not a sheet that asks a man to stop counting. On the fifty-ninth I was in this room and I put a question to the Deputy Registrar and it was answered on the record. On the sixtieth I carried a bundle up this stair. I have not signed any instrument in this matter and I am not signing one as an officer. I have a private book that leaves this building and it is not a copy of anything in it.*

He read it back to the room, at the pace of a man reading a menu, and Ada Rusk stopped him once — on the second sentence — because he had said *a question* where the sentence said *a question about the withdrawal of the district's return in the Concord Year 474*, and the difference is nine words and the nine words are the entire case.

And then he read it back correctly.

---

Junia Pell and Wend Voss examined it.

That is the plain way to say it and it is the whole of what the recitals require and there is no other procedure in this city. Two persons having no district in the matter, examining a form, in a room, in writing, each of them writing the worst true thing they can find about the subject of the work and about their own connection to it, and neither of them signing anything the district performs.

Pell did hers in the room, in her own hand, and she has now three of them on the file and the third is the longest, and it says the thing about a district-exclusion roll of nineteen names of which she is the fourth and which she cannot leave without leaving the next instrument with one observer, and it says that she is an officer of the office that produced the refusal column and of the schedule of refusals now in the file, and it says that she has read the district's schedule of performed acts four times and has twice wanted it to be less than it is, and it ends with the sentence: *I have no interest in the subject of the work and I have an interest in the office, and those are two different things and the second one is the reason this office can be believed on the first one.*

Voss's had come up from the annex on the Friday night in an envelope with the mill's postmark, and it is the sixth, and the line on the outside said: *this is the sixth and not a copy of the fifth, and it is the last one I can write without saying a thing about the mill.*

Owen Rusk read both of them in the room because a person who certifies reads the examinations, and he read them both twice, and at the end of the second one he put the page down and said the thing he had learned to say in a hall in the sixth week, which was:

"That is the same in both of them."

"Yes," said the reviewing officer. "And a separation of interest is a statement, and a statement by an officer of an interested office is a statement by that office, and the district wrote that down about itself in a letter to a Council in Concord Hall on the fiftieth day of the month and said *glad is not the same as enough*, and it is on the file, and this afternoon it is the reason a certification is possible and not the reason it is comfortable."

---

The scope went in first, and it went in in a bridge mechanic's capitals, because the ward sent it up in that hand and the form has a line for the scope and the line was filled in by nobody at a desk.

**Duty, and no status.**

Four words, on a form, in a public file, and every word of it is true, and the disclaimer about the safety, the condition and the use of the Ninth Weir was printed under it three times, and one of those three printings is on the form itself.

Behind the scope went the schedule of performed acts: a covering page, the interval, the two observers' names in their own hands, the withdrawal right in capitals, the reading clause with *and not of this office* in it, the four weeks of hours, and the six named persons with one duty each that one of them can do alone.

Behind that went the four pages with four names at the top of them. One of them carried a refusal in a cook's hand, dated. One was blank on purpose and the person who owns it had said so out loud in a hall on the Friday. Two had nothing on them at all.

And then the hold.

---

The reviewing officer read it out himself, because it is his business to read a thing that is going into a file with his office's name nowhere on it, and he read it in the voice he uses for the small print.

"**A temporary preservation of a legal status and an appeal window.** Subject: the last supported legal status of the Ninth Weir public crossing, as that status stood immediately before the roll entry of the fourteenth day of this month, and the appeal window of the load-restriction suspension of the tenth.

"Effect: that status is preserved, and that window is kept open, and nothing else is altered. **The work is not thereby made safe, funded, repaired or usable. The crossing remains closed to loads. This is not a grant, not an opening, and not a promise of either.** It is carried inside a certification and it is not carried by anything else.

"Term: seven days from the hour of the seal, ending at the hour of the seal on the seventieth day of this month. A hold does not survive the certification which carries it. Where the certification is not renewed the hold falls with it, and the crossing's status is then the status it stood in before the seal, which is suspended, and the load-restriction suspension of the tenth of the month is not affected by this instrument or by the falling of it."

Nobody in the room said anything for about four seconds.

"Mr Rusk," said the reviewing officer. "Read me the cost."

"There is a box for it."

"There is a box for it and it says *what the person making this certification carries*, and I am going to read the box to you because in about three months somebody is going to ask me what a hold costs and I am not going to answer out of my own head. Read it."

Owen Rusk read the box.

*That a preservation is a temporary keeping of a thing that is not resolved, and that the person who holds it carries the part that is not resolved, and that the part that is not resolved is a list, and that the list is on the instrument, and that the list is not discharged by the sealing of this instrument and is not discharged by any renewal of it, and that a person may take the list off the instrument by giving up the certification, and that giving up the certification returns the status to the position it stood in before the seal.*

"Underneath that," said the reviewing officer, "on the same sheet, is the list. Five lines. Read it out."

He read it out.

*One. The pension of Cobb Rennie, of two rooms in this district, who is of no roll in this city and has been for two years, and whom nothing in this matter reaches.*

*Two. Six persons of this district named on a schedule held in the intake year 486, of whom three are children, and for whom no protective return is entered, three of the five required parts of which exist and two of which are Registry functions nobody in this city is paid to provide.*

*Three. Four pages in a bundle, of which one carries a refusal, one is blank on purpose and two are unanswered.*

*Four. The repair of the abutment, four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds, unfunded, with a notice at the weir path inviting any district able to perform it to perform it.*

*Five. The receipt of the district and of its witnesses, promised in the answer of the nineteenth day of this month, on a day to be named and an hour to be named, and not given.*

Owen Rusk read the five lines out in a room with high windows and did not put the page down, and the last one was the one that made his mouth dry, and the reason it made his mouth dry is that it is the smallest and the oldest and the only one of the five that has been outstanding since the third week of the month and that he has had in his own hands the whole time and has not once been able to move, and it is the whole of what the volume's last week has been about and it is a receipt.

He signed.

One line, in his own hand, as a person, without a seal, on a form on the third landing, in front of a room with high windows, and it is the first line he has been allowed to sign since the card was drawn. It is not a continuation, and it is not a suspension, and it is not a hold. It is a certification, and the card says *a certification*, and nobody in this room has pretended for one second that the word on the card is a different word from the word on this form.

The answer to the card is on the second page of this form, in a box he filled in himself about four seconds ago, and it is not clever and it is not a way round anything. **This one has no office in it.** The four lines on the card are the four an officer signs, and there is no officer in this room and there is not going to be one, and a person of the district may make a certification and may not examine one, and he may not examine this, and he is not going to try, and the box says so in his own hand.

The clerk on the press weighed it, which nobody had asked her to do, and stamped it, and said: "That is a good weight for a return bundle," in the flat voice of a person who has pressed four hundred thousand sheets.

Then she read the new status out in the ordinary way, because that is what a clerk does at the end of a thing.

"Owen Rusk, copy clerk, River Desk, of the district named in it. Certifies the return of the district of Lower Lock, file 487-LL, in the form set out, for seven days. Holds one active preservation. Carries the list."

And then, in the same voice, to the room:

"That is not a promotion and it is not a reward and there is no form in this building on which anybody can be given anything for it. It is a term. It runs from the hour of this seal. It ends at the hour of the seal on the seventieth day of this month, and then either somebody certifies it again or the list goes back where it was on Friday."

---

He got up the hill at about half past four on the Monday afternoon and sat on the wall at the bottom of Fetter Lane for about ten minutes in the wet, and what he felt was relief.

It was a physical thing. It came up out of his chest about two minutes after he sat down and it was the first time in seven weeks that anything had come up out of his chest that was not a fear, and it went on for about four minutes, and it was a relief so complete and so undignified that he laughed out loud once on a wet wall in the rain by a river with a shut crossing above him.

And then, at about six minutes, the understanding arrived, and it arrived the way it always arrives, which is as the second half of a sentence he had already finished.

*That a preservation is a temporary keeping of a thing that is not resolved, and that the person who holds it carries the part that is not resolved.*

He had read that box out in a room. He had read it out because it was on the form, and he had read it out the way he reads out a checker, word for word, at the pace of a man reading a menu.

And it was true, and it was not a figure of speech, and it was the answer to a question he had been asking for six weeks in a different building, which was the question of what a ward could not afford to keep.

He could not afford to keep it either. That is what the seal was. It was not a thing that had been put right in a room. It was a thing that had been made small enough for a person in Docketford to hold for seven days, and the person is him, and what he is holding is a list of five lines, and the fourth of them is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds that nobody is funding, and the first of them is a pension that will never be paid, and the second is a girl of twelve and five other people and two Registry functions that this city has never once paid for.

And the relief was the wrong thing, and the understanding was the right thing, and he was going to get both of them, for seven days, and he was not going to get to have only the second.

He got up off the wall and went up the hill to the ward hall, where there were about nine people and a stove, and Ada Rusk, who looked at him in the doorway and said one thing:

"You did it in Docketford and you have come up here to say you did it, and I want you to walk straight past me and go home, because tomorrow morning I am going to tell this ward what it is that they have signed, and I am not going to have you in the room when I do it, for the same reason I sent you down the hill on Wednesday night."
