# Chapter 24 — What Ada Does With It

He got up at four and got the Thursday marker set at seven forty, because the marker was not a thing anybody could delegate to a clerk who had not been there, and then he came down to the copying floor at eight, and by twenty past nine he had understood that the day and the night he had spent deciding how to use a schedule had been spent on a question that was no longer his, and that it had been removed from him in a granary on the Wednesday night by a man from the mill who had read it out loud precisely because nobody else in the room could.

The ward hall at nine on the Thursday morning of the thirty-first day of the month was not a ward meeting. Owen knew that from the doorway, because a ward meeting has a slate and a standing order and about half the people sitting down, and this had no slate and everybody standing up and there were twenty-two of them and a delivery book open on the long table with a pencil lying beside it, and the granary committee is seven people, and about fifteen of the twenty-two were not on it at all.

"You are late," said Ada, without turning round.

"I am."

"You have been late four times this month and I want you to know that I have written every one of them in the book, and not in a hostile column, because there is no hostile column." She put her hand flat on the book. "Do not do the thing you were going to do."

"What thing was I going to do."

"Sit at the end of the table and find out what we know before you tell us what it means," said Ada Rusk. "I have watched you do that four times. Sit down."

He sat down. Marguerite Bray gave him the second chair from the end and put the delivery book in front of him, open, at a page with a line ruled on it, and the line was in Ada's capitals and it read:

*Terms of the ward's performance, proposed the 31st day. To be read into this book and, if agreed, performed.*

---

The twenty-two people in the hall were not a granary committee and the reason they were there was that Ada Rusk had gone into the granary on the Wednesday night, at the double count, and had heard a man from the mill read a column of dates and hours out loud, and had then done the thing she does, which is to stop being the person with the information and become the person who puts it in front of a room.

"I asked eight people to come up here at seven this morning," she said. "I have got twenty-two. I am not going to say that the extra fourteen are wrong. I am going to say what this meeting is, and then I am going to read the thing out, and then the room is going to do what the room does."

She put a sheet of paper on the table. It was a clean sheet of the copying kind, and there were two hands on it, and the second hand was not Marguerite Bray's.

"You read the four grounds," Ada said to Owen, without looking at him. "Out loud. All four. Slowly."

So he read the four grounds out loud in a ward hall on the Thursday morning of the thirty-first day of the month, standing at the end of the long table because he had been told to sit and it turned out he could not do both.

A roll. Two witnesses with interests separable from the district's. The duty performed in public, at the place, on the days, in the manner the return names. And a schedule of the refusals the office had made in that quarter, published.

He had done it in about ninety seconds and he stopped and Ada said, "That is a long thing to have read quickly," and he said, "I read it in nine seconds, I have been carrying it for a day and a half," and Ada said, "Then read it again in nine seconds and then explain it, because these people have not read a schedule and there are two of them in here who cannot read and one of those two is me."

So he explained it. He explained what a precedent was, and that it was not a rule, and that it was the Registry's own record of something it had done once in a district called Coldwater on a public footbridge, and that it had given them a hundred and forty pounds a year against the work, in writing, on the day they asked.

There was a noise at the end of that. Not a big one. Twenty-two people in a room finding out at once that there was a precedent.

"Right," said Ada. "That is the good half. Mrs Bray, will you put the four grounds in the book in his words and not yours."

Mrs Bray wrote them. She wrote them in her ruled hand and she read them back and she ruled a line under them, and Owen understood as she did it that the four grounds were now a thing in the ward's own document in the ward's own hand and that this had happened about four minutes after he had decided it was going to happen in about nine days.

"Now the other half," said Ada, "and I have done this part and I am going to do it in front of you and not in a corridor, and Mr Rusk, I want you to understand that I am doing it in front of you on purpose and that it is not cruelty and it is not even an opinion, it is a procedure."

She turned round to the room.

"Four grounds. A roll. Two witnesses. The duty performed in public, in the manner the return names. A published schedule of the office's refusals." She put one finger on the table. "We have the first one. We have had it thirty-six years and it is in this book and it is the best thing in this ward and I would put it against anybody's. We have the second one, more or less, and the woman who has told us in writing that her interest is not separable is sitting in this building most days of the week and she has offered to do it again every Friday for as long as the thing runs, and I have read that page twice and I would have read it four times if I had been let. We do the third one at seven forty in the morning in the rain and we have done it for three weeks and we are going to do it on Friday."

A man at the back said, "So we're in."

"No," said Ada. "We are in three out of four and the fourth one is the office's, and I have been up since five working out which of the three I am least frightened of and it is the third, and I want to explain to this room why the third is the one that is going to cost us, and I want a person from the mill to write this down because I am going to say it badly."

---

She said it, and she said it badly, on purpose, and the room got it anyway.

"Performance in public is not a duty. It is a shift."

She put her hand on the table.

"A duty is a thing a person has agreed to do. A shift is a thing with an hour on it. If we are going to be *watched*, then somebody has to write down who was there, and what time, and doing what — otherwise a stranger comes in a month and asks me and I say *I was there* and he writes *the crew leader says she was there* and goes away and writes it down, and that is not a check, that is a man being believed. So it is not a duty. It is a **roster**. And a roster is a document. And a document can be read by somebody who was not there."

Nobody in the room said anything at all, and Owen Rusk understood, at the end of the table, in the middle of a sentence he had not been in the middle of, that he was the person in the room who had least understood this for the longest.

"I have been reading that schedule for three weeks as a thing that gets me money," Ada said. "It does not get me money, or it gets me a hundred and forty pounds a year twenty years later if every one of us is still standing, and the first thing it gets me is a piece of paper with our hours on it in ink."

"And whose hours," said somebody.

"Everybody's," said Ada. "Not the crew's. Everybody's. The pump at four in the morning is Ilse's and Bram's and it is on the board every week and anybody can come and read it. The standpipe rota is four houses and Cobb Rennie and it is a thing people are ashamed of and it is going to be a thing that is written down where a stranger can read it, and that includes the night I could not get there and Ilse did, and it includes the Thursday when nothing happened and we all went anyway."

She stopped and looked round the room and said the sentence that Owen was still thinking about at the end of the following week.

"That is the frightening one, and I want it written down as the frightening one, and I am not going to pretend to anybody in this hall that it is not."

---

Then she read out her terms, and they were on the sheet in her own hand, and there were five of them, and she had written them at the double count on the Wednesday night with a bit of pencil on the back of a factor's note.

**First.** The ward performs the duty on a printed roster. One line for each person, each act, each day, with the hour. The roster is kept at the hall and a copy goes on the weir path.

**Second.** The two witnesses name the acts on the roster, in their own words, and the roster carries both names for every week it runs.

**Third.** Any person who is not of this ward and not of the Registry's office may come to the hall, on any day, without notice, and read the roster against the ward's own book, and may take a copy away, and no person of the ward may refuse them and no person of the ward may ask them why.

**Fourth.** Any person in this ward may put a complaint about the roster in writing at the hall, and the complaint goes in the book in pencil the same week, and it does not need anybody's name on it.

**Fifth.** The roster is performed whatever the return does. If the return comes back on Friday, we do the roster. If the return does not come back on Friday, we do the roster.

There was a very long silence in the hall at the end of the fifth one, and Marguerite Bray came forward and turned the book round and said, in a voice Owen had not heard from her in four weeks, "That is the whole of it, then."

"That is the whole of it," said Ada. "Mrs Bray, will you read it into the book, and I will say each one and you will write it and then I will sign underneath, and Mr Rusk can copy it out on his machine tomorrow and it will be a ward document and not a Registry one."

---

Owen did try to slow it down, and he did it in front of people.

He waited until the terms had been read, and he waited until the room had begun to shift the way a room shifts when a thing is about to be agreed, and then he said:

"Before anybody agrees to that, I want to say one thing, and I want to say it as somebody who has been wrong four times in a month, and then I will sit down."

Somebody said, "You have not been wrong four times."

"I have been wrong four times," said Owen, "and Ada knows how and Pell knows how and you do not need to."

He got about a sentence into it. He said that a third party reading a roster of hours is a thing that has not happened in this ward and could not be assessed, and that the terms were being agreed in a morning by a room that had not asked what happened when a stranger came twice, and that he had a duty to say that.

Ada Rusk did not interrupt him. She let him get to the end of it, and it took him about a minute, and then she said one sentence, in front of twenty-two people, and she said it loudly enough that everybody in the hall heard it and not only him.

"Mr Rusk, you are doing the thing you have done since the seventh day of the month, and I have been telling you about it every week, and you are doing it in front of the room this time and I am not doing it in a corridor, and I want that noticed."

"I do not know what you—"

"**A man who keeps a thing alive by holding on to it has no business telling a ward what it may perform.**"

The room made the noise a room makes. Not laughing. Twenty-two people looking at a Registry clerk and deciding whether to take a side.

"Say the rest of it," Ada said, "so he can answer it and so everybody can hear whether there is a rest of it."

Owen stood at the end of the table and had nothing. He had four weeks of careful work in him and a card with four forbidden lines on it and an instrument he was not allowed to sign, and the whole of what he had been about to say was that he was worried, and worry had been his instrument for six weeks and everybody in that hall had watched it fail.

"No," he said. "That is all of it."

"Then sit down," said Ada, "because you are standing at the end of my ward's table being a man in a good coat who does not like a thing, and I have four of those and one of them is my brother and I have never once said a true thing to any of the other three about the fact."

And then Marguerite Bray put the pencil down and said, "Before we go on. There is a condition."

Ada turned round. "Mrs Bray—"

"You have written five terms and they are good terms and I will rule them in the book this morning if the room agrees them. But there is a condition and it is mine, and I have been sitting on it since half past ten last night and I have not said it because I wanted to see whether he would say it, and he has not said it, and you are not going to say it either, so I will."

She turned the delivery book round and put it in front of her.

"Third term. Any person not of this ward may read the roster and take a copy away. **For as long as the thing runs.**" She looked round the room. "That means Thursdays at four in the morning to whoever he likes, because Ilse Mern is out on that pump on Thursdays and that is when the roster is done and that is when a stranger would see it. It means a person standing in the pump house in the dark asking questions about Bram Tillet's Tuesday. And it is going to go on — as long as the thing runs, and the thing runs as long as we keep filing, and we will keep filing because there is nothing else to do with a bridge on marginal land."

"Yes," said Ada.

"Then the condition is this." Marguerite Bray took her hand off the book. "We will be checked in public, on a calendar, by a person from another district, for as long as the thing runs. We will do it if the wage comes out of it."

There was no noise at all.

"That is not a condition," said Ada. "That is the opposite of a condition, that is the reason anybody is here, Mrs Bray, and you know it and you have written the whole thing down in one line because you knew I would do the same thing three paragraphs later and dress it up."

"I did not write it because I knew anything," said Marguerite Bray. "I wrote it because I have ruled a book for thirty-six years and I have watched this ward agree to a great many things on the strength of a good afternoon in a warm room, and every single one of them was somebody else's money. If we are going to be read by a stranger then I want a line in this book that says *and the wage*, and I want the line in the book in front of the room that is agreeing to be read, and not afterwards when it is too late to refuse."

"So it is a condition," said Ada Rusk, "and it is the right one, and I am not going to argue with a woman of sixty-one in front of twenty-two people."

"You are not going to argue with me about anything ever again," said Marguerite Bray. "I have decided that, and I am not going to tell you why, and you are not going to ask me."

She wrote it in. She wrote it in her own ruled hand on the same page, under the five terms, in a slightly different pencil, and read it back.

*Agreed: that the ward perform the terms above, and that the terms be performed for as long as the return is performed, upon the condition that the crew's wage be brought into the return and the return not be performed by persons who are owed it. Condition of M. Bray. Wage not funded.*

She read the last two words twice before she put the pencil down.

And then twenty-two people in a ward hall in the fourth week of the rain season agreed to be checked in public, on a calendar, by a stranger from another district, for as long as the thing runs, on a condition that costs money nobody has, and one page of a delivery book has both of those facts on it in two hands, and the page is the ward's and not the Registry's, and Owen Rusk had no part in either half of it.

---

He stood up at half past ten because he was going to be in the way, and Ada Rusk walked out with him to the door, which she had not done to him since the seventh day of the month.

"Mr Rusk."

"I am not going to apologise for it."

"I know you are not, and I did not tell you to, and I have told you twice in a hall in front of the ward because I wanted it said where they could hear it and not where it could be denied." She put her hand on the door frame. "You are not a bad clerk. I want to say that to you in a doorway, once, and then I am going back in there, because you have spent a month as though being careful is the same as being in charge and it is not and it has never been and it will get somebody killed, and the somebody is going to be me, because I am the one who has to stand on the top path at seven forty in the rain and the man who keeps the tray is not."

"I know that," said Owen.

"Then go and find me somebody from another district," said Ada, "because the third term is worthless without one and I have not got one and I am not going to ask anybody in this room to be one, because everybody in this room has something at stake and that is exactly the problem, isn't it. It has to be somebody with nothing at stake in this ward. It has to be a person who will turn up in the dark and say *I saw this on this day and I saw this on the next day* and go home."

"That is not an officer's job."

"No," said Ada, "and it is not your job, and it is not mine, and if you go and ask somebody in that building you will get a building."

Owen Rusk stood in the doorway of the ward hall for a moment with the rain starting, and thought about four names, and about the fact that two of them belonged to women a woman he had not spoken to in two years had had words with, and about the fact that he had carried those four names in his own hand for four weeks and had used none of them.

He went down the hill at eleven. The renewal was due the next day, the thirty-second, a Friday, and the return had been entered on the fifteenth, and seven days from the fifteenth was the twenty-second, and the twenty-second had been adjourned, and the twenty-fifth had been the day it was done, and the next one was the thirty-second, and there was no arguing with a clock about which day it wanted.

The four names had been in his notebook since the seventh day of the month. The advocate had given them to him in a kitchen in the second week and had priced two of them and had told him that the route needed a person at the far end who was not him and not her and not the Office, and he had carried them for four weeks and used none of them. He was going to go and ask one of them for a Thursday, and he was going to come back having used one of them for something it was not for, and he wrote that down on the ferry so that he would not be able to pretend to himself on the Friday that it had been an accident.

And the first thing he understood, standing on the wet hill with the ward's five terms and one condition in his head and no authority to sign any of it, was that he was going to have to go and ask badly first.
