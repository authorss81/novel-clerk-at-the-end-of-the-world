# Chapter 56 — Nether Sill

The count sheet for Nether Sill is a public document and it took him four minutes to get, and it took him about forty minutes to read, and the forty minutes were in a room at the Glass Stair on the ground floor with a window that does not shut, on the Wednesday of the eighty-sixth day of the month, with the light going and the wind coming off the river in a way that made the sheets on the desk move.

It is one page. It is headed with the name of the settlement and the year, and it has four columns, and it is a **reconciliation of the Concord Year 486**, which is the last time anybody reconciled Nether Sill, and which is therefore the county's only description of what Nether Sill is.

*Premises. Nine.* He wrote that down and then he read the whole page.

The nine are listed, in the order the counterman went down the sheet, and they are:

**One. The sluice house.** Hanna Roe, by the head of the cut. A house of one.
**Two. The mill house.** Amos Searle. A house of one, and a workshop, and the mill is worked by the family and the family's name is on the sheet twice, once as premises and once as a work, and the form has nowhere to keep a man and his work in two different lines and has kept them in one.
**Three. The barn at the low end.** Unoccupied. Premises of record since the Concord Year 462 and unoccupied since the Concord Year 480.
**Four. Nine. Seven. Eight. Five. Six.** And these six are: **a house at the head of the cut, a house on the mill road, two houses on the river side, a house and a shop.**

And the sheet says, in the fourth column, in the ordinary form's voice, the reason it is four columns long:

*The Count is a count of premises. A dwelling is a premise. A workshop is a premise. A barn is a premise. A shop is a premise. A room in a building is not a premise unless it is a dwelling, and a dwelling is a place with a door and a roof and its own water, and a room in a mill has a roof and has a door at the bottom of a stair but has not got its own water and has not got a door to the outside, and cannot be made one by a district writing it on a sheet.*

**A room in a mill cannot be made one by a district writing it on a sheet.**

Owen Rusk read that sentence eleven times on the Wednesday afternoon in a room with a window that does not shut, and then he did what he always does, which is to work out whether the sentence is a rule or a fact or an excuse, and he worked out that it is a fact about this mill and a rule about every mill in this river valley, and that the rule and the fact have been sitting in the same column for about a hundred and forty years, and that the column is the reason this settlement is on a list.

Then he read the fifth thing on the page, which is in a different hand, and it is the hand of a man who had made a career of writing, and it is under the columns and it is dated the ninth day of the month in the Concord Year 486, and it is a note in the margin of a public document:

*Reconciled of premises. The cut is held. Nine premises of record. Six of the nine were empty on the day and have been empty since; the district has not removed them and the district has been asked four times in nine years and has not removed them. Persons found on the day: thirty-one. Premises producing water: nine. Sluice: maintained. I do not propose to move the six. If they are struck, the six households that have come in since will be counted against four premises and the settlement will fail on arithmetic and not on fact, and I would rather it failed on nothing. — T. Vance, counting clerk, Nether Sill sub-table*

Owen Rusk read that five times too, and then he did the arithmetic, and it took him about a minute and a half, and it is this:

**Nine on the sheet. Four on the ground.**

The sluice house. The mill house. The barn. And the fourth is the **shop**, which is the front room of the mill house with a counter in it and a door onto the mill road, and which the sheet counted separately in the Concord Year 470 and has counted separately ever since, so a man and his sister and their father live in one building that the county has been counting as two premises for eighteen years, and the county has never been to check, and the count is not wrong, it is a description of a thing that has changed and nobody has looked at it.

**Three empty premises, unstruck, asked about four times in nine years.** The district has not removed them. That is the district's answer and the district is right, and the answer is doing this settlement the harm that the settlement is about to be counted out for.

And the eight households in the mill. The bend flooded in the seventh week of the rain season of 487, the water came up to the sill of the mill and stayed nine days, and there are eight households living in two rooms at the top of a working mill because their houses are under four feet of silt and the silt is not going anywhere this year, and the county's count form, printed in February, has nothing to say about a room at the top of a mill.

That is the whole of the case and it took him a day and a half to find it and it is entirely made of arithmetic.

---

He told Ada Rusk on the Thursday night at the long table in the ward hall, with the delivery book open and about nine people in the room, and he told it as a form and not as a story, because that is how it must be told if anybody is going to act on it.

He put the sheet on the table with his finger on the fourth column and read out the sentence about a room in a mill, and he read out the margin note in the counting clerk's hand, and he said:

"That note is from the Concord Year 486 and it is the second time in a year that a person in a public office has written down something that was true and inconvenient and been correct, and I want it noticed. He did not move the six. He wrote that the settlement would fail on arithmetic and not on fact and he would rather it failed on nothing. He is not a warden and he is not on a list and nobody has ever asked him what he thought he was doing, and he is the reason I know what I know."

"And what do you know," said Ada Rusk.

"I know a thing I have known since the eighth week of the month of rain and have not let myself have," said Owen Rusk, "which is that the sheet does not count houses. It counts **premises of record**, and a premise of record is a line in a book, and a line in a book is not a thing that anybody in this case has ever been in charge of keeping. There is no column anywhere in this city for a house that has come back, and a mill room is not a house that has come back, and there is nobody in this county whose job it is to make a room at the top of a mill into a premise of record."

"What is whose job."

"I do not know," said Owen Rusk. "It is not mine, because I am not allowed to sign it, and I have a card in a drawer with four lines on it and one of them is the word for what a person makes when nobody else will."

Nobody in the hall had anything to say about that, and one of the older men at the back said *write to the district office*, and Ada Rusk said, without turning round:

"Do not tell him to write to the district office. He works in the district office. He has worked in the district office for eight years. He has sat two floors above a book that says what premises this county has, and he has not opened it, and I would like everybody in this room to let that stand as what it is and not as anything else."

---

Nell Arbour did not come, and she was asked and she said no in nine words, and the nine words were on the Thursday and Owen Rusk wrote them down in the register he does not put on a table and did not argue with.

*No. I will not go up that landing. Ask me again in a year.*

The reason was not the nine words. The reason was that the landing at Nether Sill is a bend off the same reach as Coal Hill, and there are two women at two landings on that reach who keep two tide tables, and one of them is a woman of thirty-five who has not been asked anything since the year before last, and one of them is a woman who has kept a table for nineteen years and who is standing at the far end of a route in a district four miles from here and is not on its return, and the two of them have not spoken in two years over a tide table, and neither of them has ever blamed the other aloud.

Nell Arbour would not put herself on that reach on a Registry errand, and she said so on the Thursday in about a sentence and a half, and the sentence was: *"I am not going to go up there and stand in a room and be a woman from Docketford between two women who have not spoken since the flood year, and then go home and have it be a thing I did for a case. If I go up there I go up there for the tide table, and I have not gone up there for the tide table in two years, and the case can do without me on Sunday."*

And then she gave him the thing she had, which was the only thing she had, and she gave it to him on the Thursday in a bundle with a string on it and it was **a sheet of a Count for the Concord Year 486 for a different settlement**, and it was there so that he could see what a form of this kind looks like when it is correct.

"Three columns of figures in three hands," she said. "Look at that one for the season, then look at that one for the last, and then tell me which of the three of us has been writing the figures."

"I have not got to the three hands."

"You have not got to the three hands because you have been reading about a sluice," said Nell Arbour. "Look at the three hands. Two of them are the office. One of them is a warden. And in nine years the office's two hands have never once written a figure that the warden's hand has had to correct, and in nine years the warden's hand has never once written a figure about a person. Not one. In nine years. **Everything the Guard writes about, they write about a building.** And I want to know whether that is a rule and it is a good one, or whether it is a habit that has got itself a rule, and I have not got anybody in this county who can tell me and you are the only person I have ever met who reads a form in four minutes."

---

They went up on the Saturday in a survey boat with a girl of seventeen from a landing below the mill, who turned out to be called **Prue Hallam** and who had a way of talking that Owen Rusk recognised inside about a minute without being able to say where he had met it.

The reach is two hours up in good water and four in what was in it, and it is the last nine miles of anything built by a person, and the last four hundred yards of it is a bend with a silted inside edge and a cut on the outside of it and a sluice at the head of the cut that a woman of fifty-four opens twice a day, and a mill on the inside of the bend, and four buildings on the inside of the bend, and one road.

The first thing Owen Rusk understood, standing on the inside of that bend with the boat coming in under him, was that Nether Sill is not a poor place. It is a working place. There is a mill with a wheel in it that is turning. There are four boats on the mill road and two of them are loaded. There is a queue of about nine people on the mill road at about ten in the morning on a Saturday, which is a **market**, and the market has been going long enough that the ground is worn and there is a post with a board nailed to it, and the board says the price of meal.

**A market, with no premises of record for the ground it stands on.**

He said it out loud to Prue Hallam and she said, "The market is on the road. The road is on the bank. The bank is not anybody's. Nobody has ever asked us for a thing about the market and if they do I will tell them the market was here before the counting and the counting is a later thing than the market, and it is."

The second thing he understood, at about half past ten, was that the eight households in the mill are not hidden and have not been hidden and are not suffering, and that the most frightening fact about Nether Sill is how ordinary it is.

There is a woman doing washing on a door. There are two children on the mill road on a bicycle made for one. There is a man of about thirty sitting in a doorway sewing a coat. There is a smell of bread. There is a bed in the room at the top of the mill that has a curtain round it, and the curtain is there because it is a room in which a person sleeps, and a room in which a person sleeps is the definition of a dwelling that this county has spent a hundred and forty years deciding is not one.

And the fourth thing he understood, at about eleven o'clock, standing in the sluice house with Hanna Roe, was the thing that is the whole of this case, and she told it to him in about four minutes without being asked for it and without once being asked anything.

"There is a cut at the head of the bank," she said, "and it is mine, and I open it twice a day and I have done it since I was twenty-eight, and it is the only thing between that bend and nine hundred yards of low bank and four houses and a mill, and the county knows I do it, because the sheet says **sluice: maintained**, and it says maintained in a counting clerk's hand, and it is the only true sentence on that page."

"And if the settlement is struck from the count," said Owen Rusk.

"Then the sluice is not a work that is maintained in any column in this county," said Hanna Roe, "and the only place in this valley where anybody has written down that this bend is held is a piece of paper that a public office is going to destroy, and the next time the water comes up to the sill, which it will, because it came nine days this year, there is no record on this river that there was ever a cut at the head of it, and a wall that is not in anybody's record is a wall that a man in an office in the spring can say was never there, and there is no form in this city that will put it back."

She looked at him.

"You are the man who certified the crossing he lives behind," she said. "So you know what a form is. I have watched you look at a form since you came off that boat. What I want to know is whether you can get my name into a column, and I am going to tell you now that you cannot, and you may write that down."

"I did not ask you to — "

"You were going to," said Hanna Roe. "Not out loud. You have been in my sluice house for six minutes and you have not said the words *sluice, maintained* once and you have looked at the page three times. Somebody from the office always wants my name in the second column, and the second column says the name of the person who performs the act, and I have never put it in, in nine years, and I will tell you why, and then you can go away and think about whether I am unkind.

"If my name is in that column then the sluice is **Hanna Roe's**, and when the count comes back wrong — and it will come back wrong, because there are six empty houses on a sheet and eight families in a mill and the arithmetic is the arithmetic — then whoever reads it will come and ask Hanna Roe how a thing in her own name was allowed to fail. And I will be sixty-eight in this weather and I have opened that cut twice a day for twenty-six years and I am not going to be a woman who let a river go into a bend because of a thing I wrote in a column to be helpful."

**Nobody is to be asked. And the word is not to be written beside anybody's name.**

"I am not asking you to be helped," said Owen Rusk. "I am asking you what a room at the top of a mill would have to be to be a premise of record."

"That," said Hanna Roe, "is a much better question, and I have got an answer to it, and you are going to be disappointed, and the disappointment is not mine. It is the county's. A room at the top of a mill is a dwelling if it has its own water. Not my water. Its own. A tap and a drain and a door to the outside on the ground, and a number on the door, and a person who is at that number and can be asked at it, and the number has to be given to this county by somebody in Docketford, and it is not me and it is not the mill and it is not that girl on the boat. It is the district office. There is a schedule of premises of record for this district and it is kept in the district office and it is a **book** and I have seen a copy of it in the count room at the Glass Stair on a public stand, and it is four hundred years out of date in about nine places, and one of the nine places is a road on this bank that has got nine houses on it and a market and a post with a price of meal on it.

"You want to know if somebody could give a number to a room on the mill road," said Hanna Roe. "Somebody could. There is a form for it. It is not a difficult form and it is not a long form and it is not a favour, and I have never asked for it, and I have been asked twice by a woman from Docketford who came out here in the flood year with a basket and I did not take it off her then either, and I will tell you why I have not asked, and it is not pride.

"Because a number on a door in a book in Docketford is what a settlement is **counted as**, and a number is a number, and when the count comes back wrong the number will be in the column and the number will be the reason, and a room is not a reason. **A room is a room.** Somebody has to say the room is a room, in a hand, in a place where a person who lives in it can be asked whether it is true, and that is all any of this is, and it has not been said, and I have twenty-six years of a cut and about nine houses that have never had a number and I have never once been frightened, and I am not frightened now, and the reason I have never been frightened is that **nobody has ever come and told me I was the reason.**"

Owen Rusk stood in a sluice house at the head of a cut on the inside of a silted bend two hours up the river on the eighty-ninth day of the month and understood three separate things at once, and one of them was that he had come eighty-nine days up a river to be told by a woman of fifty-four that she has never needed anybody and does not intend to, and that this is the second time in eight weeks that a woman in his own life has said something of that shape to him, and that both of them were right and that he had gone ninety minutes down a river to be told it by somebody who does not know he exists.

And the second thing he understood was that she was not refusing the column. She was refusing the *name*. And the difference is a name, and a name is the whole of the instrument, and if he went back to Docketford with a form and a number and got a room on the mill road into the schedule of premises of record, then the second column would still want a name, and the name would still be hers, and the thing she had just refused would still be the thing that had to happen.

And the third thing he understood, at about twenty past eleven on the eighty-ninth day of the month, standing up, in a sluice house, the way he thinks now, is the thing that is going to cost him the whole of the next three days and that he will not see until Sunday morning.

*There is a book. It is a schedule of premises of record for this district. It is kept in the district office. It is four hundred years out of date in about nine places, and one of the nine places is a road on this bank.*

*It is on a public stand at the count room at the Glass Stair, which is a ground-floor room I have walked past eleven times in eight years.*

*And it is also two floors below the counting clerk at the district office, and I have sat two floors above that room for eight years, and I have not opened it, because on Monday I was going to write an objection about a form, and because on Sunday I am going to be in a room with a warden and a stone and a column that wants a number, and because the whole of what I am good at is arguing about forms and the whole of what is needed here is a person putting a number on a door.*

Prue Hallam was on the mill road with the bicycle, waiting, and Owen Rusk said, out loud, to a sluice house and a woman of fifty-four and a cut and a bend:

"Right. Thank you. That is the first useful thing anybody has said to me in nine days and it is going to cost me the next forty-eight hours and I have not told anybody that yet and I am telling you because you are the only person on this bend who has not asked me a question today."

"Ask me one," said Hanna Roe.

"May I write down that the sluice is maintained, and by what means, in my own hand, on a sheet of my own, and not put it in front of any office in any season?"

"You may write down anything I say," said Hanna Roe. "That has been the arrangement for twenty-six years. **It is putting it in a column that I mind.**"

They were on the water at about half past two and the light was going and Owen Rusk sat in the bow with a sheet of copying paper on his knee on which he had written eleven lines and had read them back twice, and he worked out, on the water, at about a quarter past three, standing up in a boat, which is a thing two people in this volume do:

*Item one. Nine on the sheet and four on the ground, and three of the four are half of one building.*
*Item two. Three of the nine are empty and have been since the Concord Year 480 and have been asked about four times in nine years and have not been struck, and the district's answer is correct.*
*Item three. Eight households in two rooms, and a room in a mill is not a dwelling because it has not got its own water.*
*Item four. A room is a dwelling if it has a tap, a drain, a door to the outside, and a number on it, and a number has to be given by the district office, and there is a book.*
*Item five. And there is a second column that wants a name, and the name is the one thing nobody at this bend will give, and I have understood in the last two hours that the name is not the problem and it is the whole of the problem.*
*Item six. The sluice. A work of record, maintained twice a day by a woman of fifty-four who will not be named, and if the settlement is struck the sluice stops being maintained in any column in this county and the next flood has no record behind it.*
*Item seven. And I have not opened the book.*

He wrote the seven lines out again on the water and did not add an eighth, and the reason he did not add an eighth is that there is an eighth and the eighth is the only one that would have worked, and he could not write it because he did not know whether it was true.

He got back to the mill slip at about half past five and the boat was let go and the light was going and there was nothing on the slip but eight men and a girl of seventeen and a woman of fifty-four who had come down from the cut to see the boat off, which is a thing people do.

"You will write," said Hanna Roe.

"I will write tomorrow," said Owen Rusk. "It is a Sunday. I will get there at about eleven and I will write the objection in the proper form and put it in the second column in a figure and not a name, because I have thought about it for two days and it is the only thing I have."

"Then you have not thought about it for two days," said Hanna Roe. "You have thought about it for an hour and a half and you have written seven things on a sheet in a boat, and I have read your face for two days, and you are a man who is going to write down the right answer to the wrong question, and I want to tell you that I have watched a great many men from Docketford do it and it is not a matter of bad faith. It is a matter of there being a form in front of them."

She got up on the slip steps.

"Do not come back on Tuesday," she said. "Come back on the Sunday after, and bring the number, or bring nothing, and either of those I will take. Do not bring a figure."

---

Owen Rusk came down the river on the Saturday night and got the last post up to the field at Cutter's Fields at about eight o'clock, and the man of sixty was still on the trestle table with the lamp lit and the case was shut and the stone was in it, and the woman of about thirty was on a stool at the tent door with a slate and a pencil and four figures on it, and she said, without being asked, and in the ordinary way a person in a field says the hour:

"They go in at first light tomorrow. Nether Sill first. It is first on the schedule and the first one is always first, because the first one is the one nobody has had a night to think about."

"And the Warden."

"The Warden does the second one and she has done the second one since her second season and she has never once done the first one and she will tell you why if you ask her and the reason is worth hearing." The woman put the pencil behind her ear. "Do you want the schedule for the Sunday?"

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"You will have it," she said. "It is a printed sheet and there are nine of them in that case and I will take one out and it will be a piece of paper and it will be the whole of the fight, and I have been doing this eleven years and I will tell you what I have learned about it, and then I am going to bed.

"**It is never the argument. It is always the thing you did not do on the Saturday.** And there is no form for the thing you did not do on the Saturday, and the figure goes in the column anyway, and the column is a figure and the settlement is counted out on a number that was correct when it was written and is wrong now, and there is nobody in Concord Hall who is going to read the column and know that."

She went into the tent. Owen Rusk stood at the trestle table at about ten to nine in the evening with a lamp burning on it and a wooden case with a hasp on it and a printed board forty feet away with eleven settlements and eleven figures on it, and thought about a room at the top of a mill with a tap and a drain and a door to the outside and a number on it, and about a book two floors below a counting clerk, and about a woman on a slip at the top of a cut who had told him to come back with the number or bring nothing and not to bring a figure.

He went home and did the tray and it took him until a quarter past ten and he was an hour and a quarter late, and it was the first time in eight weeks that he had been more than forty minutes late, and he had not noticed it until the Thursday.
