# Chapter 87 — A List in a Hand

He came up to the second floor on the Tuesday morning of the twenty-fifth day of the third month at about a quarter to ten and the light in the room was already on, and Halden Pike was standing at the shelf with his coat still on and his hat in his hand, which in eleven years Owen Rusk had seen him do once.

"**I am going to ask you for nothing,**" said Pike, "before either of us says anything else, and I want it understood that I have said that in the room and not in a corridor, because I have watched about four men in this county be given a thing in a corridor in the last ten years and every one of them believed afterwards that they had asked for it."

"You have not asked me for anything."

"**I have not.** Close the door."

He closed the door. The bulb on the flex buzzed. Pike put his hat on the shelf beside the district's book of premises of record and took out of an inside pocket a folded sheet of a copying paper that has gone the colour of a wet slate, which is the colour of everything in this building, and it was soft at both folds, which meant it had been folded and unfolded a great many times over a great many years.

"**The second of the month of the Concord Year 474,**" he said. "**That is the date on it and I wrote it on it and I have never shown it to anybody. It is the only piece of paper in this county in which anybody has ever written down the shape of what this office cannot account for, and I want you to understand that before you turn it over, and I am telling you the date first because the date is the worst of it.**"

He unfolded it on the shelf and put his hands flat on the shelf on either side of it.

"**The list is of the lines in the district's book of premises of record that this district office has never been able to account for. Not the ones that were struck. Struck is an act and it has a stroke on it and a stroke is a fact. These are the ones nobody ever struck.**"

It is four columns, ruled in ink, and the handwriting is a careful clerk's hand and it is not the hand of the man standing next to him, and Owen Rusk said so.

"No," said Pike. "**It is not mine. It is a clerk's. I dictated it and I wrote the headings and I signed it, and the clerk's hand in the body of it is a man who worked in this room from 466 to 481 and whose name is not on this piece of paper and I am not going to give you his name, and that is the only thing in this county I have ever refused anybody twice.**"

There were about ninety lines.

The four columns were headed: *the line in the district's book* — *what there is at the address* — *the last season any return reported it* — *and what was done about it.*

And there were four kinds, and Pike did not number them, and the sheet did not either, and a man who dictated it in 474 and did not number them knew what he was doing.

"**First kind. A premise with no instrument of water against it and no return against it in four consecutive seasons. Two hundred and six of those, and I have not got the years in this column because the years are in the book and the book has never been read down.**"

He put a finger on one of them and read the whole line out, and it is eleven words long.

"*Line four hundred and nine. A dwelling, described as a dwelling, with a door and no water against it and no return in four seasons.*" He took his finger off. "**Four hundred and nine. That has been four hundred and nine since 466. I have never been to it. Neither has any officer of this district office, in this century, in my eleven years or in the eleven years before mine, and it is in the book as a dwelling, and a dwelling is a premise, and a premise is a door, and there is a line in a book in this room that says there is a door at that address, and nobody knows what is behind it.**"

"Why has nobody asked."

"**Because nobody is hurt by it,**" said Halden Pike. "**That is the answer and it is the whole of the answer and it took me about four years to be able to say it in one sentence. Two hundred and six addresses where nobody lives and nobody is going to be turned out and no water is being drawn and no fund is being spent, and every one of them is a line in a book that a count is made of, and the count is a count of doors, and a door that is not there costs this county nothing whatever, and I have been the deputy registrar of this district for eleven years and I have signed the count of this district eleven times.**"

"**Second kind. A premise returned as returned, meaning the house is there, by a person who did not go, in a hand that is not the hand of a person who goes. Ninety-four.**"

"That is worse than the first," said Owen Rusk.

"**It is worse than the first by a distance and I will tell you why and then I will not say it again. The first kind is a door with nobody at it and nobody is hurt. The second kind is a person writing down that a door has nobody at it, in a return, in a form, in a season, having not gone. That is a statement about a place made by a person who did not stand in it, and it is in a public book, and about eleven hundred returns go up a stair in this county every season and not one of them has a box that says whether the person who filled it in was at the address.**"

"**Third kind. A premise struck out of this district's book with a stroke and nothing else. Sixty-one. Every one of the sixty-one has a stroke and no reason and there is no form in this county that supplies one and I did not ask for one in 474 and I have not asked for one since.**"

"**And a stroke is a fact,**" he said. "**A stroke is the only one of these three hundred and seventy that is a decision somebody made. Two hundred and ninety-nine of them are a line nobody looked at and sixty-one are a decision nobody recorded, and the sixty-one are the ones I would put in front of a person in Concord Hall tomorrow if I had anybody to put them in front of, and I have not, because a stroke with no reason behind it is only legible from inside this building.**"

"And the fourth," said Owen Rusk.

"**The fourth kind is nine lines long and it is the only one I have ever been frightened of,**" said Halden Pike.

He put one finger on the first of the nine and did not read it out, and then he read them out, and there are nine of them and they are all in the same head of water.

"**Nine premises of record, in a settlement this office has never returned in any season since the Concord Year 471, against which there is an authorised draw in the ledger of this district that nobody has been able to trace to a place since the year 478, and which is a real draw of real water that the city has been using, and which no schedule has ever named, and which the district office of Docketford has drawn every season for the last ten years and cannot account for.**"

"**Slackwater,**" said Owen Rusk.

"**You have not looked at it and neither have I and neither has this office since the year 478, and I know about it because the ledger against this district has a head on it that a man wrote in the year 478 and nobody has been able to marry to a place since, and that is a water office's difficulty and not mine, except that it is in my district's ledger, and the district's ledger is the thing I am the deputy registrar of, and I have known for ten years that there is a place in this county drawing water that the county has never looked at, and I have never been to it, and I have wanted to go for about ten years.**"

He took his hand off the paper.

"**And it is not on the schedule of settlements to be reconciled for the season of 489. It is not on the season before. It has not been on one this year, or last, or in any of the years I have been in this building, and there are no premises of record for it in this district's book, because somebody struck them out in the year 471 and put a stroke on them and no reason.**"

"How much does it draw."

"**I did not write a figure and I could not have written one, because the fourth column of this sheet is the last season any return reported it and the last season any return reported Slackwater in this county's books is before 471, and the figure that is in the ledger is in a water office's book and not in mine, and the water office is across the cut and I have been into that room twice in eleven years and I was not asked for anything either time.**"

"Then how do you know there is a draw."

"**Because a district's ledger is the second of the things I am the deputy registrar of, and there is a head on it that a man wrote in the year 478 and nobody has been able to marry to an address since, and I have asked a water office in this county one question about that head in eleven years and I got the correct answer in nine seconds, and the correct answer was that the head is in a return of authorised draws and the return of authorised draws is not a return of places.**" He put the sheet down. "**And the water is real, and the city has been using it, and it has been in this district's ledger for ten years, and it has never been in this district's book, and a settlement that is not in a district's book cannot be put on a schedule that is made out of a district's book.**"

"So nothing can touch it."

"**Nothing can touch it,**" said Halden Pike, "**and I have known that for ten years and it is the reason I have wanted to go for about ten years and I have not gone, and I would like it noticed that wanting to go is not going and I have never dressed it up as anything else. And I will say the other half of it, because I have not said it in a room in eleven years and you are the fourth person I have said it to.**" 

"**There is not one office in this county that will ever send anybody to look at a place its own books have already lost. That is not a mercy. I have written it down twice in my life and both times I hoped somebody would come, and nobody has come in eleven years. And I want it noticed that I have said *it is the only place anybody in this county is safe* in this room, and it is true, and I have not written it down anywhere, and that is twice I have said it out loud and once I have written it and that is as far as I go.**"

---

"There is one column on that sheet that is empty," said Owen Rusk.

"**All four of them are filled in. The fifth is the one that is not on it, which is a column headed what was done about it, and I have filled that in ninety times out of ninety and what I have filled it in with, in every line, is *nothing*, and I dictated it that way and I would not have it any other way, because if I had written *referred* in one line somebody in Concord Hall would have found the paper in about four years and the whole of it would have become a story about a man who referred things.**"

"Ninety," said Owen Rusk. "About ninety lines and the count is ninety and the other counts are two hundred and six and ninety-four and sixty-one and nine."

"**Three hundred and seventy,**" said Pike. "**And somebody is going to count them one day and get a different number, because two hundred and six was two hundred and eleven in 471 and I have carried the earlier figure forward, which is a thing I have criticized this office for doing for ten years, and I have done it in a list I made by hand, and I would like that noticed and I am not going to be thanked for noticing it myself.**"

---

Owen Rusk had the sheet in front of him on the shelf and had read all four columns and he had been in a room with a man for about half an hour and he had said nothing at all for about four minutes, and then he said the sentence he had been carrying since the month of rain, which he had not spent in a lane, and had not spent in a hall, and had not spent on anybody.

"**And did you hide them inside a favourable reading, and sign it.**"

Halden Pike did not move.

"That is the question," he said.

"That is the question."

"**You are asking a deputy registrar of this district, on the second floor, in a room with one bulb in it, whether in the second month of the Concord Year 474 he took a figure that a man in Concord Hall had put a favourable construction on, and a favourable construction that would have gone into a return, and wrote it down, and signed it with his own name, and did the thing the reading made possible.**"

"Yes."

"**Yes,**" said Halden Pike. "**And the reading was favourable, and it was correct, and the engineer's finding of the fourth was sound, and there were four hundred yards of bank at the bottom of Fetter Lane that were going to go, and I have never in fourteen years had cause to doubt the finding, and I have read the finding twice a year for fourteen years.**"

"You signed it on the ninth."

"**I signed it on the ninth.**"

"They drowned on the seventh."

"They drowned on the seventh," said Halden Pike, "**on ground that was not cleared. And I want the four dates in the order they happened, because I have never had them in the order they happened and I have thought about them for fourteen years and I have never had them in a row. The fourth, the engineer's finding. The seventh, twenty-two drowned on ground that was not cleared. The ninth, I signed the withdrawal of this district's return. The thirteenth, the water came on and a hundred and eighty-one people were living on the flat above Fetter Lane.**"

He said it in the voice she uses for a price.

"**And the order of those four dates is the whole of my life and it has never once been in writing in any room, and I have given you a list of three hundred and seventy lines and I have not given you the order of the four dates until this minute, and I would like it noticed that I did that on purpose and that the purpose was to see whether you would ask me the other question first.**"

"I asked the other question first."

"You asked the other question first," said Halden Pike, "and you did not answer it, and I am not going to answer it either, and I want it understood that I have not refused to. **I have answered it. I did hide them inside a favourable reading and I signed it, and the reading was correct, and the finding was sound, and twenty-two people were drowned on the seventh on ground that was not cleared, and there is no version of the ninth in which those two things are not both in it, and I have said in one sitting that I did not lie and I would sign it again, and I have said it on a runner's sheet at my own instruction in 481 and it is a public document and it is in the register, and the register goes to the Council's counter once a year and about eleven people read it.**"

---

Nobody thanked anybody.

"You are not going to thank me," said Pike, "and I have told you not to, and the reason I have told you not to is not modesty. It is that this is in the month of the year 488 and it gives back to the four hundred yards of bank at the bottom of Fetter Lane nothing whatever. Nothing. Not a name, not a line, not a figure, not one person out of a hundred and eighty-one is any better off than they were in the second month of 474 because a list of three hundred and seventy lines is now in a room with a bulb on a flex."

"I know," said Owen Rusk.

"**And I want to say the next part in a room and not in a field, because a field is where people agree, and I do not want agreement, I want it written down that I said it.**" He did not move from the door. "**The hundred and eighty-one went up onto the flat on the tenth and the eleventh of the month and they are there now. Fifty-one of them are dead. I know that because I am the deputy registrar of this district and the burial register is a book in this room and I have read it, and I have read it once a year since 474, and there is no column in the burial register for how a person got off a bank.**"

"Then why have you read it once a year."

"**Because the alternative is not reading it once at all, and I tried that for about nine months in 474, and what happened in the nine months is that I stopped being able to tell the difference between what I did and what I was given, and so I read it once a year, and that is the whole of my practice and it is not a practice anybody in this county could use.**" He put his hat on. "**Give it back in about four years and you will have the number and I will have said it out loud, and the two hundred and six and the ninety-four and the sixty-one and the nine will still be the two hundred and six and the ninety-four and the sixty-one and the nine, and fifty-one is fifty-one, and that is the whole of the arithmetic and it is the only arithmetic in this room that anybody can check.**"

"**I know that you know, and I have thought about it for eleven years and this is the first time I have had it in the room, and I would like it noted that I did not get it in the room and that I got it nowhere, and that I have been the deputy registrar of this district for eleven years with three quarters of a sheet of foolscap in my inside pocket.**"

He put his hat on.

"**And I am not on your side, and I have not been on your side, and I want that said before anything else goes in a drawer, because a man who gives a thing up in the ninth year and gets thanked in the ninth year is a man who has been waiting to be thanked, and I have not been waiting to be thanked, and the reason I am not is that there is nothing to be thanked for and the only person who could be thanked is the man whose name is on the withdrawal of the ninth, and that is me, and I sign ninety-one contested closures in this district's register and I have been the first name on it since I asked for the register in a room of thirty people in 477, and there are three names in this district out of ninety-one, and the first one is mine.**"

"Then it is on the register," said Owen Rusk.

"**It is on the register. And I asked, in 477, in a room of thirty people, for the register to have a fourth column, and I described the fourth column at some length, and the answer I got was that the register is a register of contested closures and that a line in a book that nobody has struck is not a contested closure and is not a closure of anything.**" He put his hand on the door. "**That is a different door and it is a quieter one and there are no handles on it. I have been at the second one since 477 and I have just shown you where it is and I am going back to the counter.**"

---

Owen Rusk sat in the room with the light on until about half past four on the Wednesday afternoon of the twenty-sixth day of the third month and copied the sheet out in full into a longhand book of his own, and it took him the whole of the afternoon and about eleven pages, and he did not shorten the ninety nothings, and he entered the two hundred and six and the ninety-four and the sixty-one and the nine as three hundred and seventy and then wrote under it, in his own hand, that the figures are as at the second month of the Concord Year 474 and that two hundred and six was two hundred and eleven and the deputy registrar has said so.

**And he made a copy, and a copy in one man's hand is a document, and the office has one document on this list and that is a sheet of copying paper in an inside pocket, and after the Wednesday of the twenty-sixth of the third month of the Concord Year 488 the office has two, and about four years from now there will be a file with two names on it, one of which is the man who made the list and one of which is the man who is holding it.**

He did not go to Slackwater. He was in this county nine miles from a place with nine premises of record and an authorised draw in this district's ledger that nobody has been able to marry to an address since the year 478 and twenty-two households and a chapel that closes, and he was not going to go, and it was not restraint and he wrote down the reason in the same book in one sentence and the reason is the standing and not a virtue.

**A settlement this county's arithmetic cannot reach is a settlement this county's arithmetic cannot damage. And a person who goes to one of them, on his own initiative, in a season, with no form, is doing the one thing this county has no column for, and if he writes it down afterwards somebody will want to know how he came to be the man that went, and there is no form that answers that either.**

Then he closed the book and put it on the shelf of the room he has the key to, and did not take it down, because a person may not take a document out of a district office on his own initiative and there is a tray and a counter and a form for a thing leaving a building and he had not got a form, and **there is not one instrument in this county for a man who wants to give something away.**

**The season of 489 is certified on Friday the twenty-eighth at about ten in the morning at the west gate. Two days. And in about four years a person in Concord Hall is going to ask this office what happened in the season of 489, and there are three documents in a drawer with a man's name at the foot of them and a fourth will go in on Friday, and a list in a book on a shelf with two names on it, and the office is going to produce all six, and he will not be in the building, and that is the correct arrangement.**
