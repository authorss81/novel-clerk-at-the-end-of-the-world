# Chapter 14 — The Index

The tray has no schedule. Owen had known that in the abstract on Wednesday and understood it in the flesh on Sunday morning, when he came in at eight because there was nothing else to do with a Sunday, and found that the ground floor of the Registry building on a Sunday contains exactly two people: him, and a duty clerk at the Front Desk counter who was about twenty-six and who had been on the counter since seven and who said, without being asked, that the index room was open because an index that can be locked is not an index.

The rest of the building was cold. The press was cold. There was a smell of cold ink and hot dust that a hall makes when the lamps are not lit, and his boots made the only noise in it.

He had come in with a question, which is the only reason any man has ever come in on a Sunday to an empty building, and the question was narrow and it had taken him four days to narrow it, because the first version of it had been *who put the transfer into the world* and that version was unanswerable in a building of this size, and the only way to narrow it was to stop asking about people and start asking about the **route**.

The transfer had a number. Every entry that crosses a district office has a district number, and Lower Lock's had one, and it was the number on the sheet Owen had refused to countersign on the first day of the month. But the sheet had arrived *at* the annex counter from somewhere, and things that arrive have a way of coming, and the way they came was not in the district register, because a district register records a district's holdings and not the road a thing travelled to get here.

Owen knew that, because he had keyed the receiving register at the annex for ten weeks and had never once looked at the column above the receiving line, which was the column headed with an office's word for *received from*, and which is filled in with a district number about once in nine cases and with a **county intake number** about once in forty.

He went and got the index.

It is called the Front Desk index and it is six volumes on a long table under a slope of glass, and every document the Registry of Continuant Works has ever received in Docketford has a card in it, and a card has five things on it: the number, the date received, the receiving office, the originating office, and the shape of the thing. That is all. There is no sixth thing. There is no summary and no subject and no name, and the cards are alphabetical by number, and a man can stand in front of six volumes for a whole morning and learn the exact shape of everything the Registry holds and not one single thing about what any of it says.

He found the transfer's number in four minutes and read the card, and this is the whole of it:

*Transfer entry, district to district. Received Mill Ward annex. Date received: 11th day, C.Y. 486. Originating office: County intake, Fetter Bend sub-table. Intake number: 461/F-B/9. Two leaves attached. Registry witnesses; no party from the originating district. Seal and receipt on the date of receiving.*

*County intake, Fetter Bend sub-table.* Not a district office. Not Docketford. A sub-table, two miles upriver, that takes in things from a stretch of river that has nine villages on it and no district of its own, and that files everything it receives under a single intake series with a year and a sub-table and a number, and that has never in ninety years issued a document anybody could name.

Owen read the card eleven times, because he wanted to be sure, and then he went and got the intake register, and found that the intake register for 461/F-B/9 is in the third of the six volumes — a volume he had never had cause to open, because in nine years he had never had cause to open any volume, and a volume is not a thing anybody opens. It is a thing you *consult*, and the difference between a volume and a drawer is that anybody may consult a volume in a room with a door on it and a clerk in it, and nobody may open a drawer in a tray.

The register is not indexed. You find your intake number in the day-book for the sub-table's intake year, which took him twenty minutes because the day-books are arranged by sub-table and Fetter Bend is fourth of six, and then you read forward. What he wanted was the **originating entry** — the thing that entered the sub-table's intake series, before it was forwarded to Docketford, because the originating entry is the first time anybody in the whole of this chain held a piece of paper about Lower Lock, and it would carry a name.

The day-book for the intake year, at the ninth of the month, said: *Made out at this sub-table: 1 no-local-record schedule, one sheet. Form 12-B presented at the counter, not received.*

At the tenth: *Form 12-B presented at the counter, twice. Not received.*

At the eleventh: *Received for intake: 1 receipt, signed, presented in duplicate, forwarded to Docketford district office, Mill Ward annex, with schedule and counterfoil. The schedule attached bears the date of the ninth.*

Three days, three entries, one hand, and none of the three entries referred to any of the others. That is the way a day-book is kept and it is how it should be kept, and a man standing in front of one in a public room at a Sunday table is not supposed to be able to put them in a row. Owen Rusk put them in a row in about ninety seconds and then stood there with his hands on the table and looked at the ninth of the month, where an office had written down that a man had asked for something and had not received it, and eleven lines below, where the same office had written down that it had received a document from him, and had attached to that document a schedule dated the day he asked.

And the *presenter* column on a forwarded item is not a name. It is a household reference. Owen read it, and read it again, and then he sat back on a stool in a room with six volumes under a slope of glass on a Sunday morning, and felt the private pleasure of a man with a shelf in front of him arrive at him like cold water, because the column said:

*Presented by household 14-B, Fetter Lane, Lower Lock. Unattested. No district steward. Not a district act.*

There was a man. There was a house and a street and a number on it and he had brought a thing to a sub-table two miles upriver on the eleventh of the month of a year ago, and the sub-table had written *unattested* and *no district steward* in a column, and had forwarded it anyway, and a person in that building had read those two words and had sent it on, because an unattested item is not a rejected item, it is an item that travels with a note.

He had the day-book open at the eleventh under his left hand and the index card in his right, and he put the two of them side by side on the sloping glass, because that is what a man does with two genuine records about the same piece of paper, and what he had was a contradiction, and it was the small kind, which is the only kind there is.

The card said *two leaves attached.* The day-book said *with schedule and counterfoil.* A leaf is an instrument with a scope of its own, and he had spent the better part of a night in a hall in a good coat learning that the annex in question had two of them and that they were never both called the schedule. A counterfoil is not a second leaf. A counterfoil is the office's own copy of the thing that was handed in.

> **MARGIN NOTICE**
> Subject: originating entry, county intake 461/F-B/9.
> Present support: contested. Two records of what was attached.
> Review interval of the originating entry: renewed twice. Not renewed a third time.
> The notice reports no further matter.

He read it twice, and then he checked it against the two pages in front of him, because he did not trust a thing he could not corroborate, and it was correct on all three counts, and it was still almost nothing. It did not say whose hand wrote the day-book. It did not say which of the two records was the right one, and it did not touch the presenter column at all, and the third line of it was the first time in his life that the margin had handed him a **term** instead of a date.

A renewal is a decision somebody made on a day. Two of them had been made, by somebody, in an office he had never been inside, on two days he would never know; and then somebody had sat somewhere a third time in front of the same line and had decided not to, and had written nothing, because a day-book does not say *I decided not to*; a day-book says what came in.

There is a word for a thing left out on purpose and the word is omission, and Owen had known since the first day of the month that the Registry of Continuant Works was very good at them, and he had had no word for it at all. Now he had a word, and the word had a term on it, and a term is the only kind of number he had ever wanted to be given.

---

And then Owen went back to the index, because he had a second question and the second question was the one he had been carrying since the fourteenth.

The intake series for the Fetter Bend sub-table is one series, and it runs in a single unbroken column of numbers, and the transfer's number sits in the middle of it. He read them backwards, because the number before it and the number after it are the only two things in the Registry that are guaranteed to have been filed near the same piece of paper by the same person on the same day, and the intake year runs in a single unbroken column of numbers with no breaks in it anywhere.

*461/F-B/7. —*
*461/F-B/8. —*
*461/F-B/9. Transfer entry, district to district. Mill Ward annex.*
*461/F-B/10. District return. Received for intake.*

District return. Not a district return *of* anywhere. A district return received for intake from the same sub-table in the same intake year, four weeks after the transfer, and the card gave him the district on the card, because a card gives you the shape of a thing, and a district return has a district on it by definition.

**ALDER ROW.**

And the card, in the fifth column, in a hand that was a printed word with a space in it, said:

*ALDER ROW — 461. District return. Received for intake. No forwarding office. No Registry witness. Not examined.*

*Never opened.*

Owen read the last two words four times.

He was careful about it, because he had been reading cards for an hour and he had learned the difference between a thing that is absent and a thing that is reported, and this was not *no record*, and it was not *refused*, and it was not any of the four words a Registry uses when a thing does not exist. It was **never opened**. Which is a statement about an act, performed by somebody, at a time, and it is a different *order* of statement from *no record*, and it is a different order again from *no such document*, and the difference is the whole of what he had spent a fortnight learning about a bridge: somebody had looked at a card and had decided what to write on it, and the thing they had decided to write was not *we have nothing*.

*Never opened* means: we have it, and we have never opened it, and somebody in an office thought that was worth saying.

It had been written, and Owen understood that with a small cold place behind his sternum that he had been in exactly twice before, that it was dated twenty-six years ago and that it sat in an intake series next to a transfer that had destroyed his ward, and that in twenty-six years nobody had opened it, and that the phrase *never opened* had been typed by somebody who had wanted a third person in twenty-nine years to understand that it was a decision and not a failure of filing.

He put his hand out towards the card and then he did the thing he had been doing for six days, which was to look at the number on the tab of a locked drawer in a tray two floors below and *not*.

He did not go and look. He wrote the number in his notebook and nothing else, and he did not write the shape of the tab, because he already had the shape of the tab and did not need it again, and he closed the book.

---

The duty clerk at the counter had been reading a novel through the glass for an hour and had not moved, and when Owen came out of the index room with the intake register's number written on a slip in his hand, the girl put the book down and did the thing that clerks do, which is to make it easy.

"You want the intake register."

"Yes. Intake 461/F-B/9, and the day-book it sits in."

"That is a county intake, not a district holding." She said it as information and not as an obstacle, which he was grateful for in a way he had not expected to be. "It is the third volume. I can fetch it for you, it will take a minute, or you can go back in and sit with it, except we close the room at four on a Sunday for the sweep and I will have to chase you out, and I do not want to chase anybody on a Sunday."

"Fetch it," said Owen, and then stopped, because he had heard himself, and because a request is a thing and he had learned that in a hall full of people and not in a room with one clerk in it.

"Fetch it," he said. "And put me down for it. In the register. Properly. Today. With the number and the volume and the reason."

She stopped with her hand on the second drawer.

"There's a reason to put things down."

"Yes."

"People don't ask for that."

"I know," said Owen. "That is the reason."

So she wrote it, in the same book, in the same hand, in the same ink, on the line under Sunday the twentieth, and the line said: *Request. O. Rusk, tray. County intake 461/F-B/9 and day-book, vol. 3. Reason: comparison against District Return 487-LL.* And the clerk initialled it, and gave him the counterfoil, which is the copy that goes to the office and is not the copy he keeps, and told him that the office copy would be filed with the request and that a copy of the request would be sent to him in a week by the ordinary post because anybody may have a copy of anything he has asked for.

"Thank you," said Owen.

"That's not usual."

"I know that as well," he said.

He sat down on a stool in an empty hall with the register's counterfoil in one hand and the intake register open on the table in front of him, and he was fifty seconds into a very good afternoon — the intake register is the most beautiful document in Docketford, it is a day-book and it is kept without opinion, and a man can learn more about how a place runs by reading four pages of one than by reading a year of returns — and then he stopped, and looked at the line on the counter, and understood what he had done.

He had done the thing that the ward's file had done. He had gone into a room and asked a public question, lawfully, in the correct form, and a public question in the correct form is written down, and the writing down is the price, and the price is that in eleven days a clerk in a district office will open a file that contains his name, his posting, and the fact that on the twentieth day of the month he asked to look at intake 461/F-B/9, which is the intake number of the document that removed the Ninth Weir from his ward.

Which was on the level of the building. In the ground floor of it, in the tray he was trusted with, in a drawer with a black cord round the handle and a brass tab on the face, was a file with a name on it that somebody had typed the words *never opened* about, and the name of that ward was four streets from where he slept, and he had spent his whole life walking past the end of that street and thinking *that's just the end of that street*.

And the thing he had done that finally took his legs out from under him was not the file in the drawer. It was that he now had a line in a register that was not his.

Owen Rusk had spent fourteen days frightened of an institution. He had a fortnight of his own terror to look at, in a notebook, with a bad clasp, and he had priced it and it was expensive but it was his, and it went where he carried it. This was a line in a book in a drawer in a room he did not work in, written by a woman of twenty-six because he had asked her to, in the correct form, lawfully, in the ordinary way that anybody may ask for anything at all in Docketford.

That was what it was like to be a line in a register. He had spent a month learning what a refusal in a register was worth to the person at the other end of it, and he had known — because it had been in the first volume of a book he had read on the first day of the month — that there were three of them inside a two-day window, in a standard form, in standard ink, initialled at the foot by an office and not by a man, and he had given them up on purpose on the sixth day of the month in a warm room, in front of two people, and the price had been that he would not look.

And now he had looked, and what he had found was not the three refusals, which were still sitting in the same volume four lines above his own finger, and would be there for ever, and would never be explained. What he had found was that looking was free, and that being written down was the only cost, and that the cost was the same for everybody and was paid at the counter by whoever asked.

He sat there for a long time.

Then he did the second part of it, and the second part was the part that was new.

He did not copy the intake register.

He wanted to. He wanted it the way a man wants a knife. The originating entry was in it, and the originating entry had a name on it, and the name was a man who had carried a folded sheet to a sub-table two miles upriver in the wet and been written down in a column as *unattested*, and if Owen had that page in his own notebook then the name would be his to use on a day when a name was the thing he needed, and he was almost certain that such a day would come before the rain stopped.

He put his hand flat on the register and left it there for about a minute and a half and then he did not copy it.

The reason was not honesty. He had a notebook and the reason was not that he was an honest man, because he had written a false statement in the small hours of a Tuesday and had had to tear it into pieces on a wet step.

The reason was this. A man who copies a county intake register has a copy of a county intake register. That is not a moral position; it is a fact about the shape of the thing afterwards. The register is the Registry's, and it stays in the Registry's room, and nobody ever looks at it twice, and a man in it at a Sunday table with a coat on is nobody in particular. But the moment a page of it is in a sewn notebook with a bad clasp, then the notebook is a document that contains a copy of a county intake register, and from that day the notebook is a different object. It cannot be lent. It cannot be read on a table in a hall. It cannot be handed to a man at a counter who is not the ward's clerk. And the second time that anybody in Docketford comes for Owen Rusk — and there will be a second time, and it is more likely than not within the quarter, because a man who has put his name on four documents in three weeks attracts exactly that kind of attention — the second time, the search will be of the notebook. Not of a drawer. Of him.

It is not the law. It is not even a rule. It is the shape of a thing, and he had learned in a month to read shapes of things for a living, and he read this one in about ninety seconds, and then he did something he had never done in his life, which was to take his hand off a document he wanted and understand that he was frightened of power rather than paperwork, and to write that down too, in the same breath, because the two thoughts arrived together and he was not going to let himself keep the comfortable one.

He wrote, at the bottom of the page, and then he read it back and did not improve it:

*The intake register is not copied. Not because it is theirs. Because a copy of it in my own book makes the book a thing a man may be searched for, and the next man who comes for me will not be the worse than this one.*

Then he copied the request counterfoil into the ledger, because that was his, and the counterfoil was a line in a register with his name on it, and if he was going to be a line in registers he was going to keep his own copy of the fact.

---

At four o'clock the duty clerk chased him out of the index room, as she said she would, and it was still raining, and he walked upriver with his hand in a glove and the notebook in the inside pocket on the left.

The hall was shut and dark and Wenna Sarr's stall was lit, four doors from Marguerite Bray's, and she was out on the boards in the rain doing the thing stallholders do at the end of a bad day, which is putting the day's trays back where tomorrow's can find them, and she saw him, and did not stop, and said, without preamble, in the voice of a woman who has been composing a sentence for two days:

"You read it out in there on Saturday. I was not in there. Ada told me what it said and Ada is a good girl and she will not be the one to be asked questions on my account." She set a tray down. "So I have read the answer, because Kray lent it to me and I cannot read it, and I have had it read to me twice, and I have one question and it is not about the bridge."

"All right."

"It is about the girl," said Wenna Sarr. "I have been in this ward thirty-one years and I have buried my brother and I have raised his girl since she was four, and I have been going to the mill's factor on Tuesdays since before your bridge ever came up in anybody's mouth, and I take Ivy, because I cannot read what they hand me and a child can carry a paper, and she has been carrying my notices down to that counter for a fortnight and has not once been asked to explain one to me."

She turned round and looked at him in the rain.

"So you tell me what is actually wrong with her. Not the ward. Not the paper. Her. Because you have been in this business for a month and you have not asked me one question about her, and I have noticed, and I am not going to let a month go by the way a fortnight went by."
