# Chapter 17 — Sixteen

The corridor outside the Deputy Registrar's office on the second floor is eleven feet wide and it is the only place in the River Desk building where a man can stand still without looking idle, and at ten past nine on the morning of the twenty-third day of the month Owen Rusk was standing in it with a bundle of unrouteable post under his arm, because the tray has no schedule and a tray on a Wednesday is a queue of things that cannot be delivered, and eleven feet of corridor is where a man with a bundle stands.

Halden Pike came out of the office with a hat under his arm and the door open behind him, and stopped.

"Rusk."

"Sir."

"You are holding that the wrong way. The string's wet and it will take the corner off the cover." He said it the way he said everything, and then he stood in the corridor and did not go, which Owen understood to mean that he was about to say the thing he had come out to say and had not intended to say in a corridor in front of a clerk with a bundle.

"Do you want me to come back later, sir?"

"No. There is no later. I have a quarter to close and a Friday in front of it and I have been waiting for a Friday for a term." He turned and looked down the corridor at the stair. "You have got a case on the tray. Come and stand here and don't pretend you came out for the post."

Owen put the bundle on the floor, which was against every rule in the building, and stood.

Pike did not begin with the number. He began with a word.

"The trouble with failure," he said, "is that it is a statement about a district, and every district in this city has a lawyer or a steward or a grievance, and all of them will come to this building and say *not failure, sir, and here is a witness*, and I will have to hear it, and I will have to hear sixteen of them, and then I will have to decide sixteen times, and by Thursday I will have decided four of them and I will be a man who decided four." He took his hat out from under his arm and turned it over. "Lateness is not that. A late return is not a failing return. A late return is a return whose district has not yet come to this office with something this quarter, and there are districts in this county that have not come to this office with anything since the flood year, and the difference between the two is not a matter of degree, it is a matter of who has to be in the room on Friday."

"Because they cannot argue with a date," said Owen.

"Because they cannot argue with a date," said Pike, "and because the argument that closes a stability order is not *these districts are failing*. It is that this office cannot fund sixteen of them and that a duty performed for a term by nobody at all is not a duty anybody is performing, and that the honest thing to do is to stop pretending it is one and put the whole number in a column." He put the hat on. "It is one instrument. It closes or suspends them together. It is under my solvency authority and it goes to the Council's counter on Friday, and it is the only instrument I have ever signed that I know will reach public appeal, and I have made my peace with that on the way to signing it."

"Sixteen," said Owen.

"Sixteen. Docketford and outlying, of the returns failing this quarter." And then, because he could not help it and had never been able to help it, he recited them, in order, in the flat voice of a man reading a shopping list he is tired of, and Owen stood in the corridor and did not have his notebook out and listened in the order he was given them and let the whole list go into him in order.

Lower Lock. Coal Hill. Saltpan Row. Nine Elms. Corvie Wynd. Ash Wharf. Tarn Bank. Meadowgate. Broad Rigg. Little Fetter. Havelock Reach. Stonecross. Wick End. Fetter Bend. Gallants Row. Copperfield Lane.

He had written it down on the third day of the month from memory, on risk, out of a conversation he had not been invited to, and it was still correct, and it was the sixth name in the list both times.

"Sixth," he said.

"Sixth," said Pike. "And I want you to understand something, Rusk, because I have watched you for a month and you are going to spend the rest of the day on it and get it wrong. It was sixth on the twelfth. It is sixth this morning. Your return was entered on the fifteenth and it has not moved a line on that sheet, and it is not going to, and no document you or I or that ward can produce is going to move a line on that sheet, because the sheet was made in the morning of the twelfth by two clerks in an office and it is a schedule, and a schedule is a schedule."

"You made the schedule on the twelfth," said Owen. "Three days before I entered anything."

"I made the schedule on the twelfth because the twelfth is the day the quarter's arrears are struck, and it is struck on the twelfth every quarter and it was struck on the twelfth in the year of the big flood and it will be struck on the twelfth in the year after this one, and on the twelfth a district that has not come in is a district that has not come in." Pike looked at him. "You have had a fortnight, and you have done in it what nobody in nine years has done, and I am not going to pretend to you that it has moved you a place on a list I struck three days before you started."

Owen stood in the corridor and looked at the man.

"I have the list in a book," he said. "All sixteen. From the third day of the month, out of my own head, in my own hand. I could put that book on a table in that office on Friday morning."

"I know you could."

"It would do nothing," said Owen. "It would be worse than nothing. The moment that book is on a table in that room, a clerk in the district office has written a Registry clerk's name against a schedule, and the clerk is me, and the ward is mine, and the case in that room is mine, and I stop being a man who read a list out of a supervisor's mouth and I become the eleventh thing on it." He put his hand out, palm up, and did not raise his voice, because the corridor was eleven feet wide. "That is why I am not going to do it, sir, and I would like it noticed that I am saying this in a corridor to a man who is looking at the floor."

Pike looked at the floor for a moment longer and then up.

"Noted," he said. "And it is the correct decision, and it is worth nothing, and I am going to sign on Friday at ten past four whether you put the book on the table or not."

"The instrument is drafted?"

"The instrument is drafted and it has been drafted since the twentieth of the month and it is waiting at the counter for one signature, and there is nothing on it that I would change, and the only thing wrong with it is that it is not signed." He picked up the string on Owen's bundle, still wet, and held it out. "Take that the right way. It is drafted. It is not secret. Do not go up that hill tonight and tell a ward that something is coming in a form they cannot check, because they will spend two days on it and I will have to be accurate at them, and I have not got the accuracy for it this week."

Owen took the bundle.

"I am going to have to go up that hill tonight," he said.

"I know you are. Then go and be useful, and do not go and be right."

---

He went to the second desk instead, which was the first genuinely difficult thing he had done in a month, and it was difficult for the reason that everything difficult in this business turned out to be difficult: because the answer was already in a book and the answer cost nothing, and taking it cost somebody else something to write down.

"A stability order under the Deputy Registrar's solvency authority," he said, putting the two words in the air in front of her, "closing or suspending named failing district returns together. If a district has entered a return in the current quarter, can it be named on the order?"

Pell wrote *stability order* on the corner of a sheet and underlined it once, which she did for words she intended to use.

"Yes," she said.

"Then say why, so that I have it in the right words."

"Because the order is made on the quarter's schedule, and the schedule is struck on the twelfth, and the order is not a finding about today. It is a finding about the quarter, and a district that came in on the fifteenth came in inside the quarter, and the Deputy Registrar's authority is over districts whose returns are failing, and a return that was entered on the fifteenth is a return that was absent on the twelfth." She looked up. "And it is worse than that, and this is the part you have not thought about yet. The order does not say *these sixteen are failing*. It says these sixteen are failing **as at the twelfth**, and it will be dated, and the date on it will be after the fifteenth, and there will be a document in the file that is a return entered three days before the instrument that names it, and no clerk will ever be able to say the district office did not know."

"Can it be challenged on that?"

"It can be raised. Whether it can be won is a different question and I do not know and neither does anybody else in this building, and that is the answer you are going to get and I am giving you now so that you do not spend the night deciding you already know." She put the pen down. "It is not a load restriction. Write that down, because you will hear those two words in the same sentence in the next ten days and you will not be able to tell them apart afterwards. It is not a load restriction and it is not a closure interval and it is not a suspension of an individual status, and the one thing it is not is any of them. It is an instrument that takes the standing of a number of returns and puts them in a column, and the column is the whole of its power."

"Write it down," said Owen. "Please. All of it, and the date, and the fact that the schedule was struck on the twelfth, and put your name at the bottom as the person who found it, and I will put the finding on my own ledger tonight with your name on it, and if it ever gets read out in a room it will be read as two people and not as one man in a corridor who was told something by his supervisor."

Pell looked at the sheet for a while.

"You understand what you have just asked me to do."

"Yes."

"I want to be certain, because I want to say it and I would rather say it than have you notice it in six months." She put both hands flat on the board. "Writing that down makes me a participant. It is not a favour. From today I am a person who has an interest in the outcome of a stability order, and so is every person who reads a stability order, and so are you, and the difference is that I will have written mine down, and a person who has written their interest down is a person who can be challenged on it, and can be sustained, and can be sustained by herself, as of yesterday." She picked up the pen again. "You have done to me this afternoon exactly what I did to that return on the seventh day. I want you to notice it. And I want you to notice that neither of us is going to be thanked for it, and that I have decided not to mind, and that you may find that harder than I do."

She wrote it for eleven minutes, in her square hand, and signed it, and dated it the twenty-third, and gave it to him.

Owen read it standing there and had to stop halfway through.

"You have written *the receiving district return has not performed the duty and did not offer to*, and that is not in any of this."

"It is in the file," said Pell. "It has been in the file since the fourteenth and you have not read it since the fourteenth because you have been reading your own documents. Go and look at the annex schedule at ten o'clock tonight, Rusk, and then tell me I have not been right about anything all afternoon."

He put the sheet in the inside pocket on the left, next to a docket stub.

"Second thing," he said, "and then I will go and do the round."

"I have not got a second thing."

"You have got a question you have been waiting for me to ask, and you wrote it on the first page, and I have read the first page, and it has a name on it and no address at the end of it." He put his hand flat on the board, on her page, and did not move it. "The appeal route. It has been open since the seventh day and it has a name at the front of it and nowhere at the back, and a route with nobody standing at the far end of it is a piece of paper with an address on it, and I have been avoiding that box for three weeks because the answer is that I have to go and ask a person to put their name in a public record for a ward they have never been inside."

"Yes."

"Well, I am asking. Who is it?"

"The advocate gave you four names and told you two of them are women she has had words with, which means you have had them for three weeks and have not used them, and it means she has already priced the two for you and does not expect you to like the answer." Pell squared the tray card. "You do not want me to say one of them. You have not come up here for a name. You came up here for the order, and you have got the order, and you have a sheet with my name at the bottom of it, and if I hand you a name now you will go and use it this week, and the week after that, when the review is over and the order is signed, you will not go, and that will be the last time anybody in the delta ever stands at the end of that route."

"What do you want me to do?"

"Go to the ward," said Pell. "There is a woman leaving the return tonight, or there is going to be one, and she is doing it because your instrument told her she was allowed to, and you are the only person in Docketford who understands that in about a week. Go and watch her do it. And then come and ask me again, and I will give you the name of a person and I will give you her reasons, and you will go and be told no kindly, and you will come back and tell me she said no, and it will be the first conversation you have had in a month with somebody who is not being paid by either side."

---

The hall at Lower Lock on the evening of the twenty-third had a stove going and about thirty people in it and a smell of wet coats and paraffin, and Owen Rusk stood at the back with a notebook he did not take out, and Junia Pell sat on the bench by the door because she had come up on the half-price of the return and had told him on the road that she was not going to be asked to explain anything to anybody, and Wend Voss stood at the back by the door as well because he had walked up the hill on his own time for the second time in a month and had told nobody he was coming.

Nessa Kray was seventy-one years old and had sold seed in Lower Lock for forty-one years, and she stood up at about half past seven, in the middle of somebody else's business, and she did it in a way that stopped the room, because the whole room knew who she was and what she was for.

"I am not here about the bridge," she said. "I have nothing to do say about the bridge and I have not read the paper and I do not intend to learn. I am here about my own page, and I want it read out, and then I want to say four sentences and I want them written down exactly, and Mrs Bray can do that, and I will wait."

Pell read Kray's page out. It was one sheet, one adult to a sheet, and the act recorded on it was the act of maintaining the Ninth Weir public crossing weekly before two observers, at the river path, on the days recorded. It was, as everything in the return was, perfectly correct.

"I signed that page on the seventh day of the month at that table," said Kray, "and I did it because there were nine of us signing and I wanted this ward to have a name, and because I have been in this ward longer than anybody in the room has been alive and it has never once mattered whether I was on a piece of paper, and I have never needed anybody to agree that I am a woman who sells seed."

She put her hand flat on the table, which she had learned from watching Marguerite Bray do it for sixty years.

"And it turns out that is not what the paper says. The paper says I go out in the rain and maintain a crossing every week before two people, and I have not been within thirty yards of that bridge at any hour of any day since my husband died, and I could not tell you which of the nine posts is the one with the crack on it, and I have walked past the end of that path four times in my life and that was for a funeral." She looked round. "I thought a branch was a name. It is not a name. It is a thing you do, and I have not done that thing, and I am seventy-one, and I am not going to be asked twice a year by a stranger from some office whether I went out in the rain."

Somebody at the back said, "Nessa—"

"No," said Kray, pleasantly. "And that is the last of the four sentences and I said it out of order, and Mrs Bray, you have got it the way I said it and not the way I meant it, and if you want it written the way I meant it you can ask me and I will say it again slowly and you can copy it slow."

The room had gone completely quiet. Owen Rusk, standing at the back with his hand in his coat, understood that the only sound in it was a man in the third row turning a hat over in his hands, and that he was not going to be able to say a single word for the next two minutes, and that this was correct, and that if he had been able to say a word he would have made it worse.

"I am not leaving the ward," said Kray. "I have been on this pitch since before the hall had a roof on the north side and I will be on it when the ward has two. I am taking myself out of a piece of paper, and Mrs Bray is going to write that I did it, and I am going to leave the page on the table, and I would like whoever is taking minutes to write down that I did it *myself*, standing up, with my reasons, and not because anybody asked me to and not because anybody in this room was against me."

Nobody in this room was against her. That was the part nobody had a sentence for, and it was sitting in the room, and Owen could feel it: thirty people who had all voted for a bridge in a flood year and who now had a woman of seventy-one telling the truth at them in front of a stove, and not one of them on her side and all of them in the room.

Ada Rusk was standing by the wall with her arms folded. She did not move for the whole of it. She had voted against the transfer; she was not in this return; she was the crew leader who had set the marker these three weeks; and Owen understood, watching her, that she had made a decision in the last five minutes to say absolutely nothing at all, and that it was the hardest thing in the room to do, and that she had done it because a woman who had said no once in a hall had earned the right to say something now without a rescuer.

Marguerite Bray wrote it down. She wrote it in the delivery book, in pencil, in her own hand, with the day and the hour, and then she read it back, and Nessa Kray said *yes, that's it*, and Bray ruled a line under the entry and wrote the date a second time underneath, and Owen knew what that second date was, because he had watched her do it four times in a month, and it was the date of the event and the date of the recording, and they are never the same date, and the whole of her life is the keeping of that difference.

Junia Pell wrote her own observation in the margin of the withdrawn page, in her own words, and Wend Voss wrote his under hers, and neither of them said a word about what they had come up the hill for, and then Pell came and stood beside Owen at the back while Kray went out into the rain to close her boards.

"That is the first one of the nine," Pell said.

"That is the first one of the nine," said Owen.

"You have not said anything."

"There is nothing to say that I am entitled to say," said Owen. "She read her own page, and her own page was my page, and I wrote nine of them and I checked that each of the nine had signed and I did not check that each of the nine had a thing inside the scope to do." He put his notebook away without opening it. "That is the whole of my part in it and it is worse than the scope box, and I am going to have to live in a building with it, and the instrument survived it anyway, and I do not know which of those two facts is going to keep me awake and I expect it will be the second one."

"No," said Pell. "It will be the first, for about three weeks, and then it will be the second, for the rest of your life."

Down the hall, Ilse Mern was telling Nessa Kray, in a low voice, that Kray had said a thing in there about a seed tray, and Kray was saying that there were two of them in the wrong stack and that had been going on for two years. Two women at the back who had not spoken to Owen at all turned round and looked at the door Kray had gone out of, and one of them said, to the other, in the flat comfortable way that people in a small place say a thing they have already decided:

"Well. She walked."

Kray was forty yards up the lane in the rain, shutting her boards, four doors from Marguerite Bray's front door, and she was going to be there in the morning, and the day after that, and the day after that, and she was going to be the first name in the history of Lower Lock to have walked off something, and there were two hundred and thirty-nine other people in the ward who were going to be told that by somebody, and the instrument that had permitted it was nine pages long and had cost three weeks and one good hand and one page, and it was still on a table in a hall.

Owen Rusk went out into it at half past nine and did not go home, and walked up to the mill's factor's shutter to ask one question about a Friday.
