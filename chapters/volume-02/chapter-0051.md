# Chapter 51 — The Hour of the Seal

He sat down at the desk at about ten minutes to three with about forty minutes left on a certification and a tray of two hundred and forty sheets of correspondence that cannot be routed, and he did nine of them, and at about twenty-five to three he put the pencil down flat on the copying paper and did not pick it up again for the rest of that hour.

Nothing happened.

That is the whole of it, and it is worth a chapter. A certification that arrives at the end of its term does not produce a form and does not produce a notice and does not produce a clerk, and there is no room in this building in which anybody could be told, and no person is required to know when a thing they were never told about expires. He had asked for the certification. He had filled in the box on the second page in his own hand, in four seconds, in a room with high windows, and it was the only line he had been allowed to sign in seven weeks, and it had a term on it, and the term was about forty minutes away when he sat down.

At about half past three, on the ground floor, in the record of the district returns, which is his posting and is a book on a table that anybody may read and which he has been the officer of since Friday morning, a line changed.

He went down at ten to four. He did not hurry, and he made himself do it slowly and on purpose, because he had learned something in seven weeks about the difference between finding a thing out and being told a thing.

The entry is in a column headed *status of the work*, and it is a line about a work and not a line about a district and not a line about a person, and it reads:

*Ninth Weir public crossing. Status suspended. The preservation entered inside the certification of the sixty-third day has run its term and is fallen with it. The appeal window of the suspension of the tenth is closed. Last supported position: as at the fourteenth day.*

And the day and the hour of the change are in the margin of the book in ink, in a hand that is not Owen Rusk's and that is not any person's hand at all, because that column is ruled in a room two floors up by a machine and no clerk in this building has touched it and none of them ever will.

He read it twice. Then he read the line above it, which was his own, sealed on the Monday of the sixty-third day at about half past three in the afternoon, which is still there, and which has not been struck, and which is not going to be struck, because instruments in this city do not get struck when they end, they get superseded, and the instrument below it supersedes it, and the instrument below it is a line in a book with no signature on it at all.

Both entries were true. That was what he stood there and understood, with his hands flat on the edge of a table on the ground floor of a building where he is a stranger.

The whole of what he had done for seven weeks was two true lines in a book. One of them had come to the end of its own life at about half past three in the afternoon on the seventieth day of the month, and nobody had lied, and nobody had done anything wrong, and the status of a public crossing had gone back to being what it was on the fourteenth day, which is suspended. The crossing is closed to loads. It was closed to loads on the first day of the month of rain and it is closed to loads now. The crack in the abutment has moved. The repair is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and unfunded. The notice nailed to a post at the top of the weir path says that any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it, and there are four people a day who can read that notice, and not one of them is anybody.

And the appeal window.

He would not have thought about it if the line had not said it. Four weeks it had been open. He had read it out himself, in a room with high windows, in the voice he uses for the small print, and the reading had gone: *that status is preserved, and that window is kept open, and nothing else is altered.*

The window was the second half of it. He had carried the second half of it for seven days as though the first half were the work, and the first half was a legal status, and the second half was the only part any person in Lower Lock ever intended to use, because nobody in that ward has ever wanted a crossing to be open. They wanted to be able to say that the closing of it was theirs to answer for, and there is a difference, and it is the difference this whole case has been about since the first week of the month.

The window closed at the same hour. It closed because a hold does not survive the certification which carries it, which was on the second page of the form in a box, which he read out loud in a room, and which Ada Rusk had stopped him on once, on a different sentence, for nine words, and the nine words had been the entire case.

So: four weeks in which a person might have appealed a load restriction. Gone. Not refused — **expired**, which is a different word, and which appears in no schedule of refused requests, and which will appear in no register of contested closures of this district, and which has nothing whatever to do with the ninety-one entries in the register that Halden Pike asked to be the first name in on the afternoon of the sixty-sixth day of the month in a room of thirty people.

Owen Rusk wrote two lines in the register he does not put on a table. He did not write the five lines. The five lines were on the instrument and they were going to stay on the instrument, and the difference between the two was a sentence, and the sentence was the whole of what the certification had been.

*That a preservation is a temporary keeping of a thing that is not resolved, and that the person who holds it carries the part that is not resolved.*

The term on it had gone at half past three. The part that is not resolved had not gone anywhere. It is still exactly where it was, and it is not resolved, and there is now no instrument in this city that has a person on it who is answerable for it, and that is not a punishment and it is not a rule and it is not anybody's decision.

It is the natural consequence of a form that is good for seven days.

---

He got up the hill at about half past five and it was the first week after the rain season and the lanes were doing what they do, and there were about nine people in the ward hall and one lamp and no stove, because the stove is lit when there is business and there was a stove on the Thursday before and there was not one on the Monday.

He had worked out on the hill what he was going to say, in the way he works things out, which is by standing up on a wall in the wet.

It was going to be short. It was going to be the four sentences he had drafted on the wall, and it was going to be accurate, and every word of it was going to be true, and he had known since the Wednesday that it was not going to be enough, and he had come anyway, because a thing that has ended is told to the people it was done for, in the room, out loud, and not in a letter.

He told them at about twenty to six.

He said: the certification ran seven days from the seal at about half past three on the sixty-third and it ended at the hour of the seal this afternoon, and it was not renewed, and there was no instrument to renew it with, and he had not gone and looked for one.

He said: the crossing's status is suspended as it was on the fourteenth, and it is closed to loads, and the load restriction of the tenth stands and is not affected, and the repair is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and is not funded, and the notice is still on the post.

He said: the appeal window of the tenth is closed as of an hour ago, and it was open for four weeks, and it closed with the certification, and nobody refused it and nobody has to answer for it.

And then Ada Rusk, from the wall by the door, where she was standing with a slate under her arm, said:

"What did it cost."

Not *what happened*. What did it cost. She said it the way she says the numbers in that hall, in the voice she uses for six in the evening at twenty minutes to six in the morning, and it was not a rhetorical question and she did not wait long for it.

"Four weeks," said Owen Rusk. "And a form."

"That is not an answer, and you know it is not, and you have come up this hill to give me one, so say the other one."

"Five hours a week," he said. "Of somebody's life, on a page, in a room, every week for four weeks. And the two things I could hold in my head at one time, which I could not do before the Monday, and which I can do again from about an hour ago, and I did not know until this afternoon that I could not."

Marguerite Bray, at the end of the long table with the delivery book open in front of her and the pencil not in her hand, said:

"Rule what he said, and rule the hour, and rule under it that the person who carried it is not under anything now."

She wrote it in about four seconds in her own ruled hand, and she did not read it back, and she put the pencil down, and she said the thing she says.

"And rule under that," said Bray, "that no person in this room is under it either, and that includes him, and that I am not going to have anybody in this hall spend the next year being careful about a form that has finished."

Nobody said anything for about half a minute. There was a woman of about fifty at the back who had come in out of the wet and had not sat down.

Then Ada Rusk said the second question, and it was the worse one, and she asked it in the ordinary voice.

"Right," she said. "So what was it for."

Owen Rusk stood in a ward hall in Lower Lock at about ten to six in the evening on the seventieth day of the month of a rain season that has ended, and he found that he had an answer, and that the answer was four words long, and that it was not his.

"A status with a date on it and a window in it," he said. "For four weeks. At a cost of five hours a week that was never anybody's five hours because nobody was ever paid for them."

"That is a good answer," said Ada Rusk, "and I want it written down, and Mrs Bray is going to write it down, and then I am going to say the other half of it and then I am going to go and prime a pump."

"You do not prime on a Monday."

"I prime on the nights the pump does not take," said Ada Rusk, "and it did not take on Sunday, and Wil has it in the book with the day on it, and the other half of it is that we could not have had four weeks of a window for a crossing we all know is shut unless somebody in Docketford had been willing to be the one holding it, and you did that, and I am not going to pretend it was nothing, and I am also not going to pretend it was a bridge."

And she wrote the four words on the slate and gave it to Bray and went out into the wet.

Marguerite Bray wrote it in the book in her own hand, under the line about the cost, and the day, and the hour, and the room accepted it, and nobody congratulated anybody, and about four people left.

---

He walked down Fetter Lane at about a quarter to seven and stopped at the post at the top of the path, because the notice was there and he had read it seven times in seven weeks and he read it an eighth time out of habit.

*REPAIR OF ABUTMENT, NINTH WEIR. Four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds. Any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it. Inquiries to the district office, second counter.*

It was in the weather. It had been in the weather since the flood. Four people a day could read it.

And about eleven inches below it, on the same post, on the same nails, there was a second notice, and it was newer, and it was not from his office, and the paper was a different weight and the ink was a different black.

*NOTICE OF THE COUNTING SEASON, DOCKETFORD DISTRICT. The Count for the season will be taken in the Concord Year 488. The Count is a count of draws, of premises of record, and of works maintained, and out of it come the water, the maintenance fund, and the standing of households in this district. The counting season opens on the first day of the month. The schedule of settlements to be reconciled for this district has been printed and is posted for notice at Cutter's Fields, in advance of the season. Persons who consider that a settlement, a work or a household of theirs has been wrongly included or wrongly omitted are invited to make enquiry of the count room at the Glass Stair at any hour the room is open.*

It was about eleven lines and it named no settlement and no street and no household, and it did not have to. Every person in a river city knows by roughly the time they are thirty whether their premises are of record, and this notice was not asking them. It was telling them the date.

Owen Rusk read it twice, standing at a post in the wet at the top of Fetter Lane at about ten to seven in the evening, with a ward hall at his back and a shut crossing above him and a tray of correspondence he had not finished downstairs.

*A settlement, a work or a household of theirs.*

He looked at it for a long time. There is no count of premises at the top of that path. There is a crossing that is not a premise, and a pump house, and a granary, and a pitch at the bottom of a slope, and thirteen households of record, and two hundred and forty people, and a return with four words of scope at the top of it, and a schedule of performed acts in four weeks of hours with six names on it, and a roster board in a tin under two nails, and a standpipe rota with five names.

None of those is a premise. Not one. And the ward had spent seven weeks learning to say so out loud to a building that would not give it a hearing, and it had got the hearing, and it had got the four words, and it had got a status with a window in it for four weeks, and at about ten to seven on the seventieth day of the month of a rain season that has ended, it occurred to him — standing at a post, in the wet, the way it now occurred to him about four times a week, which is a thing he has stopped apologising for — that for seven weeks the argument had been about whether his ward **existed**, and that he had never once, in any room, been asked whether his ward could be **counted**.

He had a tray of two hundred and forty sheets of mail that could not be routed upstairs, and a book downstairs with a new line in it, and eight hours until the building shut, and eleven days until the season opened.

And Ada Rusk's four words were still drying on a slate in a hall behind him, and they were a status, and this notice, on the same post, wanted premises.

Owen Rusk turned the second notice round with two fingers so that the rain was not running down the face of it, and read it a third time, and then he went down the hill to do the rest of his tray, and it was going to take him until eight o'clock, and he was going to be forty minutes late home, and that was a thing he had done twice a week for seven weeks and had entirely stopped minding.
