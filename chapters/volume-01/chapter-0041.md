# Chapter 41 — The Water Comes Up

The water came up on the Saturday morning of the fifty-fourth day of the month between two and four in the morning, and nobody in Lower Lock saw it happen, because it does not happen like that. It happens for a day and a half first, in the colour of the water and in a smell off the weir path that nobody can name and everybody recognises, and then at some point in the small hours it stops being a river two hundred yards away and becomes a noise in the wall of the granary.

Owen Rusk was asleep at ten past three and woke because the lamp in his own front room had moved. That is not a thing a lamp does. It is what happens when the floor of a house that stands on a spoil of forty years of somebody else's spoil is put under load by a river that has come up four inches in a night, and the joists take up, and everything on the joists shifts about a sixteenth of an inch, and a lamp on a table that was level is not level.

He lay still for a moment and worked out that this was the last water of the season. Everybody who has spent a winter in this valley knows what the last push sounds like, and everybody knows the sentence that comes after it, which is that the season has not ended and there is one more in it, and the one after that is the one that gets you. Nobody said the sentence. It did not need saying. It had been said in this ward every year since before the ward was a roll entry.

He was wet to the knee within twenty minutes. There was no sensible order to it and he did not try to make one. Boots. The good coat, which he has never once been able to explain to anybody in Docketford and which he put on because it was the only warm thing he owned. The register, out of the inside pocket on the left, into the inside pocket on the right, because he had learned in five weeks that a thing that is in your coat on the wrong side is a thing that gets wet and a wet register is a lost register.

Then out into a street with about an inch of moving water in it, going up.

---

Ada Rusk had the shed door open and the lamp out of the way by the time he got to the top of Fetter Lane, and she was not shouting, and that was the first thing he understood about the scale of it, because Ada Rusk does not stop shouting, she simply shouts less, and the difference between the two is a night and a decade.

"Two feet at the top step at four," she said. "The weir path is under. The top path is under at the second bend. The channel bar is somewhere in the second bend and I am not going to send anybody down there in the dark for a bar."

"How many at the top?"

"Four households at the top of the lane, and that is four households and not four people." She was already pulling a lamp toward the door. "Standpipe is the first thing. If the pump does not take by five then we are carrying every pail in this ward up from the bottom of Bell Row in the dark, and that is a thing I am not doing with a girl of twelve in it."

"The rota is a Thursday rota."

"Yes."

"It is a Saturday."

"Yes," said Ada Rusk, "and the rota is a Thursday rota, and I am going to do the Thursday rota on a Saturday night because the pump is going to be a Saturday night pump whether the paper says so or not, and I am going to write it on the paper that it was a Saturday night, and you are going to read it back to me afterwards word for word and you are not going to make it Thursday."

---

They primed the pump by hand from about twenty past four.

That is not a sentence about a pump. A pump at Fetter Lane is a thing with a lever and a spout and a well-head outside the door that has to be filled from the bottom of the lane, and there is no charge in this city for water and there has not been a grant for it since the fourteenth of the month, and what a ward does at four in the morning when the pump will not prime is a physical act performed by five people in about two hours with a bucket chain and a great deal of swearing, and it is not in anybody's return and it is the reason the ward's water is what it is.

Owen Rusk was on the bucket chain at twenty-five past four in the morning in the rain in the dark with a lamp he could not see by, and he found, somewhere in the second hour, that he was frightened in a way he had not been frightened in six weeks, and that the fear was not of the water and was not of a hearing and was the plain animal fear of being a person who is not good at a thing, in the dark, in weather, doing a thing badly while people who are good at it watch him.

The pump did not take at five. It took at twenty to six, on the seventh stroke, on a bucket that Ilse Mern had carried up the lane herself and which she put down without comment and which was heavier than it looked.

Cobb Rennie was on the chain. He is fifty-eight and has been off the Registry roll for two of the last nineteen years and has walked a child of twelve home every night in the dark without once being asked to and without once knowing why, and he is the oldest person on the rota and he is not a branch of anything and his hours are not on the return in any capacity at all and he was on the bucket chain that night because a rota is a rota.

And the Sarr household was on the rota, and the rota says a household and a household of two is a woman of about sixty and a girl of twelve, and the girl carried a pail on a stick up Fetter Lane twice before six in the morning in the dark in weather like that, and Wenna Sarr carried the other one, and neither of them said a word about it then and neither of them said a word about it afterwards, and both of them would be in a hall two days later where a woman of sixty-one ruled things and a man read things back.

---

The ward performed.

That is the sentence that matters and it is the sentence that took Owen Rusk until about nine in the morning to understand was true.

The roster says: marker Thursday and Friday at seven forty. Channel clearance Wednesday night. Pump prime Thursday at four. Granary double count Wednesday night, written up Thursday afternoon. Standpipe rota carried. Roster read in public at ten past six in the evening.

It is raining hard enough on that Saturday morning that nobody in the ward could have argued, from where they were standing, that any of those things had been done. The water was four inches deep in the lane below the pump house and rising. The top path was under at the second bend. The weir path was under entirely and would stay under until Monday. The bins in the granary stood in ten inches of water and a corner of one of them had gone.

They put the markers in at about eight in the morning, in daylight, in the rain, on a Saturday, with three people on the top path above the second bend and two at the path end, and they stood there for an hour and forty minutes in the rain and the whole ward watched two of them do it from a hundred yards off, and nobody's paper says a Saturday.

Ada Rusk stood in the shed doorway with the rain running off the corrugated lip in a sheet in front of her and watched it, and she said one thing to Owen Rusk that she said out loud so that four other people could hear it, and it was:

"Right. Now it is on the paper and everybody in this ward has seen a stranger see us do it, and that is worth more than it was on Friday and I would like that written down with the hour on it."

---

The checker's sheet came in at about half past eleven in the morning and Owen Rusk read it back.

He wants to be exact about what that means, because the whole of six weeks has been building to it and it is a very small thing. The ward's acts are written down by whoever performed them, in that person's own hand, on a sheet. The sheet goes into the schedule. The schedule goes to the ward's book. And the reading of the sheet back — out loud, in the hall, with his hand on the page, at the pace the reader reads — is a check, and a check is a thing that catches a man in a hall choosing what a sentence was, and on the thirty-eighth of the month Ada Rusk had stopped him twice for it in front of twenty-four people and told him that the reading is a man choosing what a sentence was and if you choose then there is no check in it.

So on the Saturday morning he stood at the end of the long table in the ward hall with the wet collar of his good coat drying on him and read the whole of Saturday's sheet, word for word, at the pace of a man reading a menu, and did not choose one word of it.

He read: the rise, the times, the depth at the top step, the second bend, the weir path under, the pump primed at twenty to six on the seventh stroke, the marker at eight, three on the top path and two at the path end, one hour and forty minutes, the bins standing in ten inches, the first bin spoiled in about a sixth of itself.

And then he got to the last line and stopped, because he had been about to read it and had read it four times already on the walk down the hill and had read it wrong four times on purpose.

The last line said: *the seed stall at the bottom of the pitch is gone.*

And Owen Rusk, who cannot help himself, who has been unable to help himself for six weeks, prepared to say *the seed stall at the bottom of the pitch is damaged.*

It is a smaller word. It is the word a man uses when the person who wrote the sheet is standing nine feet away in a wet apron and is seventy-one years old and has had the same pitch for forty-one years, and damaged is a word you can say to a person like that without taking anything off her, and gone is a word you cannot.

"**The seed stall is gone,**" he said. "Do not make it smaller because you are standing next to me."

Nessa Kray was at the end of the table in a wet apron with her arms folded, and she had not spoken in a hall in a month, and she had walked away from a branch of this ward's own return in front of about thirty people and had never said a word about it since, and she said this one and then she sat down.

"Read the next bit," she said.

He read the next bit.

And that was the whole of Owen Rusk's function on the night the water came up, and it is worth saying plainly because he has spent six weeks learning that a thing nobody will do for him is a thing he has to find: on the worst night of the rain season, in a ward of two hundred and forty people, the only office anybody needed him to fill was the one where a man stands in a hall and says what a sheet says, and lets a woman of seventy-one stop him.

---

He got his hand open a little before midday, and it was the same hand.

It is the hand that cut itself on the steel lip of the seal press's catch tray on the fifteenth of the month, and it had healed, and it had been healed for about three weeks, and it opened again on Saturday morning at Fetter Lane because a bucket handle is wet iron and his hand had been in wet iron for two hours. It is not a serious injury. It is a small cumulative thing and he has had it since the third week and it is the only mark on him that anybody in this case can point at.

Bram Tillet, who is a ferry cook at Wexford Landing and one of the four who said no on the seventh day of the month, looked at it and said one sentence.

"That is going to be worse on Thursday," he said, "when you are holding a pen in it in a room in Docketford in front of forty people."

"Thursday I am reading a bundle," said Owen Rusk, "and a bundle does not need a hand."

"Then read it on Thursday," said Bram Tillet, and went out into the rain.

---

The count of what the night cost was done in the hall at about four in the afternoon, and it was done properly, which in this ward means that every person who had lost a thing said so out loud in front of the room and the room wrote it down and did not comfort anybody.

Two feet of water at the top step at four. The weir path under and staying under. The top path under at the second bend and the marker put in above the water and not in the water, which is a thing Ada Rusk had done on purpose and which cost her the only place the marker can properly go. The channelling bar gone into the second bend and not to be looked for until the water drops. Ten inches in the granary. One bin spoiled in about a sixth of itself and a second bin that was not yet written down. Four households whose doors were shut and whose floors were wet. A girl of twelve who had carried a pail on a stick up a lane twice before six in the morning. A man of fifty-eight on a bucket chain at twenty-five past four. Nine crew unpaid for four months, standing in a shed in the wet, being told that the thing they maintain has a schedule on it and the schedule is being kept.

And the seed stall at the bottom of the pitch, gone.

The seed stall is in neither leaf of the annex. That is not a thing anybody had remembered for six weeks and it turned out this morning to be the most useful sentence in the case, because it is the only reason a woman of seventy-one was not, this morning, standing in a hall with a claim in it. The transfer moved three things out of this ward: the crossing, the Fetter Lane pump and the granary. It left the seed stall where it was, in a place that no instrument in this city has ever named, and there has been no document about that pitch in four years, and there is not going to be one, and the pitch is under a foot of water with the trays in it.

Marguerite Bray wrote the whole of it in the delivery book, in her own ruled hand, in the second column, with the day, and she read it back to the room, and nobody said anything about the seed stall at all after about the second minute, which is the sound a hall makes when it has been told a thing that cannot be improved and has decided not to pretend otherwise.

---

Nobody had thought about the seed stall in six weeks. That is the honest way to put it and it is not a criticism, because the stall is a pitch with trays on it and the trays go to a dozen households and the dozen know whose trays they are and the pitch is a fact in the way the crossing is a fact and the pump is a fact, except that the crossing and the pump are in a transfer and a granary book and a schedule, and the pitch is in nothing at all.

It went at about eleven o'clock on Saturday morning, which nobody saw, because a thing that has been in the same place for forty-one years does not go in front of anybody. It went the way a market pitch goes, which is that the gravel under it is the market's and the market is two streets up and the market had a factor, and the factor is a man whose business is on a Saturday morning and who had four hundred trays of his own in a shed and no way of knowing that a foot of river had come up under a woman of seventy-one at the bottom of a pitch in a ward nobody in Docketford has heard of.

The trays were under. They are trays — shallow wooden things with a lip — and a foot and a half of moving water gets into a tray and stays in it, and what came up on the Saturday afternoon was slime and the black smell of a floor that has been wet for nine hours, and when Owen Rusk walked down to the bottom of the pitch at about two in the afternoon to see what a gone thing looks like, he understood that he had never in his life wanted so much for a document to be wrong.

There is no document. There has never been a document. The pitch was made by a market and stands on a market's gravel and is not in a ward's return because the ward has never claimed it, and this ward has never claimed it because Ada Rusk's mother kept a seed stall at the bottom of that pitch for thirty years and Ada Rusk has been on the top path at seven forty in the morning since she was nineteen and has not once walked to the bottom of the pitch to look at it.

That is the whole of it. A woman kept a stall for forty-one years and nobody wrote it down, and the reason nobody wrote it down is not that the Registry is careless. It is that a person who is doing the most ordinary thing in the world every day of her life does not need a witness, and the Registry does not maintain a column of things that are ordinary, and neither, in the end, does a ward.

At about half past two Dory Anstruther got down to the pitch with a pole and made two trips into the water and brought out a dozen trays and stacked them on the dry stones at the top of the pitch in a row, and did not throw any of them away, and did not say anything at all while he did it, and when somebody said *those are no good, Ness* he said *they can be scrubbed* and would not be moved off the stones.

At about four o'clock in the afternoon, with the water still up, Owen Rusk stood in a lane above a river in Lower Lock and worked out the shape of the rest of his life in about ninety seconds, standing up, in the rain, the way he had learned to do it in the sixth week because sitting down with a pencil had become a way of avoiding things.

The hearing is on the fifty-ninth. That is five days away. The next renewal of the ward's return is on the sixtieth, and the renewal is a review and not a binding, and he cannot sign it and has never been able to sign anything, and the interval is seven days and it has been seven days every time.

The crossing is closed to loads and is going to be closed to loads on the fifty-ninth, and the abutment is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and unfunded, and the notice at the weir path still invites any district that can do the job better to come and do the job, and the weir path is under ten inches of water and nobody is going to read the notice.

And the ward has just performed for the first time in six weeks on a day that is not a Thursday, in the rain, in the dark, with a pail chain, and there is a woman of seventy-one who has lost forty-one years of a pitch in one night and the ward's paper does not claim the seed stall, and there is no instrument in this city she can put in front of anybody, and she is not owed anything, and she knows exactly what she is owed and it is nothing.

The water was not going to drop before Thursday. That was not a forecast, it was an arithmetic of a weir with a step in it, and a man who had spent six weeks learning to look at a physical act before he put a description of it on paper could see it from the top of a lane with his shoes full.

So the ward was going to go to a hearing in Docketford on a Thursday in the rain, in the seventh week of a season that has not ended, having performed in the dark the night before, with its crossing shut, its pump primed by hand at twenty to six, a bin spoiled in a sixth, a stall gone, a wage four months in arrears, and a return that has been renewed for review five times and has never once been binding.

And in three days a woman of seventy-one was going to stand in that same hall and ask the room for nothing, because she had already asked the room for nothing on the seventh day of the month and it was written down.
