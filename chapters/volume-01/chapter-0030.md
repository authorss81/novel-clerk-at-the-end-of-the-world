# Chapter 30 — Performance

The first thing that happened on the Thursday morning of the thirty-eighth day of the month was that a woman came down the hill in the dark with a lamp in her hand and a pad under her arm, and the second thing was that Ada Rusk had the hall full by a quarter to six, which for a hall in this ward is the middle of the night.

"You are all here for the roster," said Ada, "and I am going to say one thing about it before we start, and I have been up since four trying to write it and I want it said in this room and not at a table afterwards."

Twenty-four people, and it was dark outside and the rain had stopped for the first time in three days.

"A roster is not a duty. I have said that in this room and I am going to say the other half of it now, and the other half is that a roster is a thing about **every hour of everybody in it**, and it is not about the bridge, and it never was."

She put her hand flat on the table.

"Everything we have put in front of anybody this month has been about a crossing. A crossing is out there. I have been out there in the dark four times this month and a stranger has never been to it and a stranger is not going to be allowed to go and look at the crack in the abutment, because he could not tell a good abutment from a bad one and neither could I, and he would come back and write down whatever his clerk wrote."

Before she would come up the hill at all she had wanted the ward's own book in her hand, and Ada Rusk had walked it down to the bottom of Fetter Lane in the dark and put it into both of her hands without a word said about it, and the book had gone back up inside Ilse Mern's coat. It is a small thing and it is the only condition the stranger made that the ward could have broken by accident, and it is the reason the rest of the day is a check and not a visit.

"From first light," said Ivet Sterne from the back, "to dark. And I have told the man from the Registry I am not going to the weir path, and I have told him why, and he agreed with me in about four seconds, which is the only reason I am here."

"Then here is the fright," said Ada. "On the thirty-first I asked you to agree to be read by a stranger, and every one of you agreed, and I did the asking and I did it well and I want that on the record because it was the best half hour of work I have done in four years." She looked round the room. "What I did not say, and what I have thought about for four days and have not slept properly about, is what it means to be read. It does not mean somebody comes and watches a bridge. It means a piece of paper with our names on it and the hours we were there goes into a hall and anybody at all can come in and read it. Any person. A stranger. A person with a grudge. A person from the mill, or a person from this building, or a person who wants a job and decides one of us is too slow."

"That is the fright," she said, "and I am not going to soften it, and I am not going to say *but it is worth it*, because I do not know that it is worth it, and it is my job and it is eight of your hours and I am not the one whose hours they are. We are doing it. I have made my peace with it in the last four days. I am telling you about it because in three weeks one of you is going to be furious about something on that sheet, and I would rather you were furious at me in this room tonight than at a piece of paper in a fortnight."

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Ilse Mern said, from the middle of the room, "Do it."

"Do it," said Ada, "and the first act is at seven forty and I want everybody in this room to know where they are going to be for the rest of the day, because there is a woman with a pad and she is going to ask all of us, and some of the answers are going to be embarrassing."

---

The marker went in at seven forty with three people on the top path and four at the path end, in the first dry morning in three days, and the river was up another two inches than it had been on the Monday, and nobody was pleased about that and everybody wrote it down.

The channel was clear. It had been cleared on the Wednesday night and the bar had not come back, and Ada wrote *clear, no bar, outfall low after the rain stops* in the ward's book and Ivet Sterne read it back and wrote under it, in her own hand, *and I was there and the water at the step was a hand's width below the sill.*

The pump was primed at four in the morning by two people and again at half past seven by one, and it took, and the standpipe at the top of Fetter Lane was running by nine.

Ivet Sterne came to the pump house at about ten and stood in the doorway for a quarter of an hour and asked three questions, and two of the three were the same question and the third was *who is not here today*, and Ilse Mern told her, and the person who was not there was a man with a mended collarbone who is a branch of the return and who is not going in the water, and the outside observer wrote down the name of the person who was not there.

That is a check. Owen Rusk, standing on the step of a pump house at a quarter past ten in the morning, understood it for the first time as an actual object rather than as a phrase: a piece of paper on which the *absence* of a named man is an entry, in a stranger's hand, and a stranger's entry cannot be argued with by the person who did not come.

At half past eleven a girl of twelve came up the lane with two pails on a stick and went down to the standpipe and back, twice, and Ivet Sterne asked her two questions and then asked Ilse Mern a third, and Ilse Mern said, "That is Ivy Sarr and she is on the rota and I am on the rota and that is the rota," and pointed at a sheet on the wall with four names and a fifth, and Owen Rusk stood on a step and looked at the five names and thought about six protective returns and two Registry functions that nobody is paid to provide, and could not think of a single sentence to put on a form, and did not try.

The granary double count was written up on the Thursday afternoon, and the writing up took two hours and ten minutes, and it is the dullest act in the return.

Bins against the book, twice on the Wednesday night, by two people who are not the same person twice, and the second count is written out in full in the ward's book on the Thursday afternoon, bin by bin, in a hand that is not the counter's hand. It came out at **two weeks and a bit**, which was the first time the figure had gone down inside a month, and Bray wrote the day under it in the same pencil, and it is written out in full in front of a stranger from a landing upriver, and the stranger copies it out in full, and neither of those is anything anybody would have done in this ward in October.

And then, at about ten past six, in the hall, the roster.

It is a sheet of paper with a heading on it and columns under the heading, and it is the first document this ward has made about itself rather than about what it wants. That is the thing Owen Rusk understood at about a quarter to seven in the evening while watching a woman of sixty-one read out somebody else's handwriting, and it is worth more than any of the other things in the month, and it is a thing about grammar.

A district return is a claim. It says *we perform this duty* and it asks the Office to treat it as one, and everything in it is directed at a reader in a building two miles downriver. A roster is not directed at anybody. It is a list of names and hours with two witnesses' names at the foot of it and a line for a complaint, and its grammar is the ward's own return's grammar — a named subject, an act, observers, a scope, a route for a person to complain along — **applied to a task instead of to a crossing.** The first line of it is a duty and every line after that is a fact.

That is the difference between a claim and a thing you do again next week.

Marguerite Bray read it out, at the pace she reads everything, which is slow, and she read every line including the lines about the four houses on the standpipe, and the hall sat and listened to the whole of everybody's hours being read out loud for the first time, and Nessa Kray was at the back of it and had come and had said nothing, in the way she has said nothing about a bridge since the twenty-third.

Then Ada Rusk said, "Registry's man. Read back what the outside observer has written. Word for word. That's your job and you don't get to do anything else today."

---

He read it back badly.

He had never read anything aloud from a hand he did not know, in front of a room, in a hurry, with a lamp going, and he got through the first two lines and then he did the thing that every clerk does, which is to summarise, and he said *and the pump was primed at four and it took*, and Ada Rusk stopped him in front of twenty-four people.

"Word for word," she said.

"That is what it says."

"It is not what it says. It says *and the water at the step was a hand's width below the sill and the sill has grit on it that was not on it on the thirty-first*." She was not angry, which was much worse. "Mr Rusk, that woman wrote a sentence about a sill and you turned it into a sentence about a pump. If a stranger is going to be read by a stranger, then the reading is the entire instrument, and the reading is a man in a hall choosing what a sentence was. If you choose, then the whole thing is a man in a hall choosing, and there is no check in it, and I have just handed you a piece of paper and told you that you are the document."

She put the pad back on the table.

"Again. Word for word, and put your hand on the page so I can see you are reading it and not remembering it."

Owen Rusk read it back again, with his hand on the page, and he read it all the way through, and the third line was a sentence about a man who was not there and it is a sentence he could not have made up and it is the sentence that made the day into a check.

And then, at the bottom, in her own hand, the last line, which she had written at the top of the pad before she arrived and had not shown to anybody:

*This ward has agreed that its hours be read. The wage on this roster is four months in arrears and is not funded. I will not soften that and I will say it every time I come.*

She read that one herself, and then she put the pad down and said, "That is my line and I wrote it this morning and it is the only sentence in two pages that I would refuse to sign again."

Then Ada Rusk asked for it to be read aloud.

---

Nobody slept much in Lower Lock on the Thursday night of the thirty-eighth day of the month. That is a thing about wards and it is not a complaint, and the seven days since the terms were agreed had produced a great deal of very short argument at very short hours between people who had never before had an opinion about each other's mornings.

The return came up on the Friday, the thirty-ninth, and the reviewing officer was in, and it was the third renewal in a fortnight and it was on terms, and Owen Rusk entered the act.

The act was one line and it is in the file now and it says that on the thirty-eighth day of the month the district performed the maintenance of the named work in the manner named in this return, on a roster produced by the district, before two observers, one of whom is an officer of this office and one of whom is not, and that the roster is produced at the ward hall and a copy is held at the weir path, and that the roster was read in public in the ward hall and that a person not of the district and not of this office was present throughout from first light to dark.

He signed the box that said *entry of a filing on this tray.* He did not sign anything else, because there was nothing else on the card to sign, and the card was in the drawer where it lives, and the four forbidden lines were on it where they have been since the third week of the month.

It was renewed, for review, on the same terms, for the third time in a fortnight. The two observers were re-separated in writing, in their own hands, once for this renewal. The crossing's standing is a duty the district performs and nothing else. No grant is restored. The pump grant is suspended. The load-restriction suspension of the tenth of the month is unaffected and is not superseded. The Day 14 roll entry of the fourteenth stands and has not been amended and will not be. The abutment is four thousand one hundred and sixty and unfunded. The interval runs from the thirty-ninth and the next is the forty-sixth.

Nothing was won. That is not a way of putting it. Nothing was won, and everything has been done, and those are two different sentences, and the second one is the one the Registry's own precedent asks for.

---

He walked down the hill at half past six on the Friday evening, the thirty-ninth day of the month, in the first dry evening in a week, and did the arithmetic one more time, because he had got into the habit of it and because it was the only thing in his life he could still get exactly right.

He had a return renewed for review with a date on it and a roster in a hall with everybody's hours on it in ink, that anybody may come and read, and a condition written in a delivery book by a woman of sixty-one that says *and the wage*, and the wage is not funded and is not going to be.

He had four days of the pump at the standpipe and four households and a man of fifty-eight on a rota, and a roster records hours and does not record service, and no hour that Cobb Rennie stands on that standpipe in the dark is an hour that goes anywhere near his pension, and he was on the rota because he is the one who comes, and that is a different fact and it does not touch the other one.

He had four names in a notebook with no word *custodian* written beside any of them, and three of the five parts, and the two that are missing are the two that nobody is paid to provide, and he had six people to protect and six instruments to build and not one route.

He had the sixteen named returns in a book, in a column, and on a public docket, and their numbers in a granary committee's hands because Ada Rusk read one of them out loud at a double count on a Wednesday night, and he had not put his book on a table and he was not going to.

He had a batch of forty-one entries, an initial, a date, a schedule he did not copy, three numbers he has handled inside nine years off route lists, and the practice of an Office that is still doing it this quarter, forty-one times, in four words, on a table in a waiting hall with twenty people reading it. And he had two forty-ones, and no reason on earth they should match, and that was the thing he was going to have to be careful about for the rest of his life: a batch of forty-one residence statuses struck in one afternoon in the fourth quarter of Concord Year 471, and forty-one dispositions printed in a standard form in the quarter now, and the only thing the two of them have in common is that a man with a straight edge and a man with a form both got to forty-one in a quarter without either of them noticing.

He had a stability order on a docket with no hearing date on it, signed by a careful man at ten past four on the twenty-fourth of the month, naming sixteen returns, Lower Lock sixth, and it was public, and it was appealable to the Glass Stair on application, and nobody had applied, and the application was not his to make because a co-petitioner is a petitioner and not an advocate and he had learned that in a kitchen in the second week of the month.

And he had a name-shaped hole in a working week and a name that had come back on a returned letter with two pins on it, and he was not going to tell anybody, ever, and there was no office in the city that would be asked about it.

And he had, on the level of the ward, a thing he had not had in a month: a room of twenty-four people who had done a thing he had nothing to do with, in public, on a day, in front of a woman who had told him to her face that being legible is not the same as being paid, and who had then written that sentence down in her own hand so that it could not be taken back.

The ward did not need him. He had understood that at about eleven o'clock on the Thursday morning, standing on a step, watching a man be recorded as absent, and it had not been a wound and it had not been a relief. It had been a distinction, and the distinction was the only thing available to him and it turned out to be a very large one: the ward did not need him and the *document* needed him, and the document is a thing made out of language, and Owen Rusk is a man who is good at language, and that is the whole of the trade and it is not nothing, and it is not the same as being the person who did it, and it never will be.

He did not get pride. He got a Thursday, and a wage of four months, and a sentence in a stranger's hand, and a roster that will be in the same hall next week with the same names on it and one more week of hours on it, and the knowledge that it will be there next week because the ward has said in a book that it will be there next week, and that he has nothing to do with that at all and could not stop it if he tried.

---

He got in at about nine and the plate was on the table and the lamp was lit and the register was in the inside pocket on the left, and he sat down, and he did not do the arithmetic again because he had done it twice.

He took the register out and opened it at the last page and read the four lines at the bottom of it, which is where he writes the things that are not on any form: the refusal that had no name on it, a sentence he had written word for word at two in the morning and then torn up, the return he had entered, the four lines about a batch.

And he understood, sitting at his own table at nine o'clock on the thirty-ninth day of the month of a rain season in Concord Year 487, that there was not one person's name in it.

Not one of the people he could help. Thirty-nine days of the worst and best work of his life, and the register held sixteen district returns and four ward names, and the word *son* in a line he had read off somebody else's page and never written, and a rule he had made for himself in a warm room in the second week about not writing down the names of people he had not spoken to.

He had kept a register. That is exactly what it was and he had been right about that and Pell had been right about it in a hall and he had been proud of being right about it. And a register of facts with no person in it is a book that could be produced in any room in this city by any officer who ever wanted to look, and read, and mean nothing whatever, because nothing in it is anybody.

Owen Rusk took the pen up. He wrote the date, because he always wrote the date. And then, under the date, in his own square hand, the first time he had ever put it on a page in his life and the last time he ever would:

**Marta Rusk.**

He did not write anything under it. He did not put it in a case, or a claim, or a correction, or a schedule, or on a table in a hall, and he did not tell the ward, and he was not going to tell anybody, and nothing was restored and nothing was owed to her and the eleven days did not come back and the eight months did not come back and the correct record says she is dead and the Office delayed.

Her name went in the register and stayed there, and Owen Rusk shut the book, and put it in the inside pocket on the left, and blew the lamp out, and went to bed with a renewal entered for the day and a ward to read in a week's time and a name in his own hand for the first time in his life, and the answer's date still sitting in a piece of paper in a docket in the last week of a season that no instrument in Docketford had ever fixed the end of, and a hearing that did not exist yet on a docket that anybody might read.
