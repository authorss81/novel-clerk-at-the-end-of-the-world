# Chapter 9 — Petition

On the twelfth day Owen had one afternoon to get nine pages into a docket, and he had a way to do it that took about forty minutes, and the way was to write it himself.

The short-form attestation is a single sheet. It is the instrument a copy clerk uses when the facts are already on file and only need to be gathered into one place for a countersignature: it names the subject, states the acts, states the witnesses, and carries a statement that the filer has compared the copy with the record. It is the most trusted page in the building. It is why a Registry word is worth anything. And every word of it has to be true, which is why it is the one thing in this Office that cannot be produced by a man who has not read the record.

Owen wrote it on the back of a spoiled sheet at half past one. He wrote it in the form's own idiom, which is a good idiom, which he has never had cause to complain about. He put in the subject and the acts, four of them, with the dates. He put in the witnesses, two, by name, and the wording of their observations exactly as each had written it. He put in the ward's ratification and the four recorded dissents and the six names that could not answer. He put in the scope exactly as Ada Rusk had dictated it, all of it, including the sentence about safety and condition and use that no witness in the return was competent to speak to, and including the sentences about the crossing's last supported legal status and the repair nobody is paying for, which the ward added on the second day of the morning, after a boy came out of a spillway.

And at the foot, where the form gives the filer a box, he put his own name, which is a statement about him and says nothing whatever about the ward.

It was, without any qualification whatever, an accurate document. Every proposition in it was supported by something a person had actually done. There was not one false statement in it. He had gone over it twice with the return open in front of him, and it was the best work he had done in a month, and it was going to be counted by somebody today, because the countersignature desk cleared at three.

He took it to the second desk and put it on the corner of it and said, "This needs a copyist and a countersignature, and it clears at three."

Pell read it standing up. It took her about ninety seconds, which was twice as long as it needed, and he watched her get to the foot of it and stay there, and he understood, about four seconds too late, what he had brought her.

"You've put your name in the filer's box," she said.

"That's what the box is for."

"It is. I didn't say it wasn't. Have you got two witnesses to this sheet?"

"Yes. They're on it. Their own words."

"Their own words about the acts."

"Yes."

"Read me the box above their names."

He read it. The box above the two witnesses' names is the box on the short form that says *the persons whose acts are recorded above have each acknowledged the record of them, and no other person has.*

"You haven't got them in the room," said Pell.

"They're in the ward."

"Then this sheet says two people in a building said yes. That's not a witness, that's a rumour with a seal on it." She put the sheet down on the corner of the desk and squared it, and did not push it back, and did not take it, and did not say the word *no*.

"You are the copyist of this desk," she said, "and I am the copyist of this desk, and if I take that sheet I am certifying a witness line with my hand on it, and there is nobody in this room who can say those two people were in the room. Not the four you asked in the hall. Not the woman who keeps the roll. Nobody." She looked up. "I have been in this Office three weeks and I have signed my name under four hundred things and every one of them I have seen. I am not going to start with that one."

"Pell—"

"Short form is for gathering. It is not for carrying. You have written a summary of a ward's return and handed it to a clerk and asked her to certify that the ward said so." She took her hands off it. "I am not refusing you. Put it in the tray. It is not mine to take."

And she went back to her tray, and the sheet sat on the corner of the second desk in the middle of a copying hall for a quarter past two, and at three o'clock it went back to Owen's own desk unentered, unsigned, and with the countersignature slot empty, and nobody in the hall said a word to him about it, which was worse.

---

He was in the corridor outside the seal stair with the sheet still under his arm when Marguerite Bray said his name.

She was standing against the wall with her hood down and her ledger under her arm, and Owen understood that she had been there for a considerable part of the afternoon, and that she had watched the whole thing from about nine feet away, and that she had not wanted to be in any room.

"Short form," she said.

"Yes."

"You wrote it yourself."

"Yes."

"And she wouldn't copy it."

"She wouldn't sign the witness line. She's right about the witness line."

"Mm," said Marguerite Bray, and looked at the floor, and said, "I've been a ward steward for thirty-one years and I've had four things asked of me by people who were right and one thing asked of me by people who were wrong, and you are the first person in this business who came to me and said *you tell me* and then wrote it down and then couldn't get it entered, and I have been standing here since two o'clock trying to decide whether you were a fool or a liar."

"I'm neither. I'm a copy clerk."

"So I have found out." She took the ledger out from under her arm and held it in both hands. "Are you going to argue with me about the short form? Because I have listened to the argument twice this week from two different clerks and I did not understand either of them and I have been walking up and down this corridor for an hour."

"No, ma'am."

"Good." She turned and went towards the Glass Stair docket office, and Owen went with her, and it was the first time in a month that anybody had walked into a room in that building because she had decided to.

---

The Glass Stair gets its name from a roof that has been glazed since before the Blanking and leaks in every weather the city has. The sound of that roof on a wet afternoon is the sound of a large room with no one in it who is comfortable. There was a docket counter at the foot of the stairs with a brass rail and a clerk behind it, and a queue of nine, and the clerk on duty was about sixty and had the manner of a man who has learned that everything said to him at that counter is a thing somebody will be sorry about.

Marguerite Bray did not queue. She went to the rail and put her ledger on the ledge and said, in the voice Owen had heard her use on a wet boy once and had never heard used on anybody else:

"I am the steward of Lower Lock ward. I file a petition for a hearing."

The clerk looked at her. Then he looked at the queue, and then at his own hands, and said, "Against what instrument?"

"Against the transfer of the maintenance of the Ninth Weir crossing, entered on the Mill Ward return in the autumn of last year, and against the lapse of its status at the continuance roll on Monday, and against the conditions in which this ward's households now stand."

"That is a lot to put in one petition."

"Yes."

"Madam, I can refuse a petition, and if I refuse it I have to write a reason, and the reason goes on the file and the file is public. I would have to write that a ward's steward may not ask this office to explain how it came to hold a public crossing." He said it without any inflection, as though he were reading a form. "I am not going to write that. Give me the words. I will enter what you say."

"I know the words," said Marguerite Bray, "because I have been sitting in a kitchen for eleven days being told what they are, and nobody has written them down."

So Owen stood at the rail and did not say one word while a woman of sixty-one with a hand-kept ledger dictated a petition, at some length, in the flat unhurried cadence of somebody reading out a delivery book, and he understood as it went along that he had spent a fortnight learning how to write a document and was now, for the first time in his life, being present while one was written by a person who was better at it.

It did not allege an error. It did not use the word forged or false or conspiracy, and it did not mention the two days, and it did not mention his inquiry, and it did not ask for the transfer to be undone. It said that the ward's households had performed the maintenance of a named public crossing in the weeks preceding this petition; that two living observers with separable interests had recorded four such acts; that the crossing's status would be entered on the coming roll with no district performing it; that the ward had ratified a transfer it did not understand at a meeting at which one of its members had stated an objection; and that it asked the office to *hear the ward's return and to say on the record what a ward may perform and who is obliged to pay for it*.

That last clause was not Owen's and it was not Marguerite's either. It came out of the middle of it, in Marguerite's own voice, and it was the most frightening sentence Owen had heard in a month because it was the sentence the ward actually wanted, and it was a question about money, and money is the one subject on which the Office is honest and the ward is not.

The clerk entered it. It took twenty minutes. It was entered, not granted; there was a box on the form and he ticked the box that said *entered* and he did not tick any other box, and he wrote the docket number on the stub and gave her the stub.

"There is no hearing date," he said. "I cannot give you one and you have not asked me for one, because you know you have not got one."

"I know."

"Answer is due in seven days in writing. Not a hearing. An answer. It will be signed and it will say what the office has to say, and it will not be a hearing, and I want that written down at the top of the stub so that nobody can come back and say they expected otherwise."

Marguerite Bray read the stub. She did not thank him. She said, "Now say the other thing, which is the thing you actually want me to hear, and say it now rather than in a month when I have to find out you knew it and said nothing."

The clerk looked at her for a moment.

"Your petition does not hold the crossing's status," he said. "A petition is a request. I have entered it in the docket. On Monday the continuance roll goes on as it goes on every week, with or without anything of yours in the docket, and the crossing will be entered the way it would have been if you had not come up that stair. Nothing about a hearing holds a roll. A stay of a roll is a different instrument, it is made by a different office, and it is not available in the time you have."

"Thank you," said Marguerite Bray. "That is what I came to hear and I could not have asked you for it without telling you why."

"You could have," said the clerk, "and a good many people do, and I would have had to write down *you did not ask*."

Owen stood at the rail and listened to a man he had never met tell a ward steward, kindly, and in advance, that nothing she had done had helped. It was the most helpful thing he had heard in a month. He understood why, and the understanding was not a comfort: a man who says *this will not save you* before you have spent a year finding out is a man who has saved you a year, and every officer Owen had worked beside for nine years had let him spend the year.

---

"Co-petitioner," Marguerite said, on the stair, with the stub in her hand. "I am the filer. You will be on it as a co-petitioner, because the office requires a Registry officer to be named on a petition that a district returns into contention, and because I am not having a docket in this building with nobody from that Office on it, since I know what that office is like."

"That's not why."

"It is not the reason I would give in writing," she said. "It is the reason, and you may have both."

And Owen Rusk, a copy clerk with no authority to change a status, went up the Glass Stair and back to the Fourth River Desk and became a co-petitioner in his own name in a case he could not win by being correct, which is a thing the Office had no form for, because in nine years he had never seen it done and had certainly never thought of doing it.

At the desk he wrote the one thing he had come down to write, on a sheet of the form's own paper, and it took him twenty minutes, and it is the only document in this business that carries his name for anything at all.

**STATEMENT OF MY PART, by Owen Rusk, Copy Clerk, Fourth River Desk.**
*I was the clerk of the Fourth River Desk. On the seventh day of this month I was instructed by the Mill Ward annex, in writing, to prepare a notice of ward ratification in the form provided, and the instruction included the direction that ward notices do not carry public works crossing language. I prepared the notice. I filled the scope box with a reference to the transfer registered at the Mill Ward annex rather than with a naming of the three works transferred, one of which is the Ninth Weir public crossing. I put my initials in the box the form gives the preparing clerk, which certifies that the sheet is my work and that the scope was filled as instructed, and which certifies nothing else whatever. I did not tick the box that certifies the notice accurate and complete, and I did not read it when the district office ticked it without me, and I let that stand, on a document of mine, about a ward of mine, with a word in it I knew to be true and knew to be unread. I did not name the crossing, and I knew at the time that the crossing was one of the works transferred and that the majority of the households in the ward had not read the transfer. I did not at any time make objection to the notice, either when I was instructed to prepare it, when I initialled it, when it was posted, or in the week after it was posted. I was not in the ward meeting of the seventh day until after it had begun, and I was removed from it when I attempted to state the omission. I state these facts because I am aware that the effect of the notice upon this ward was to obtain signatures to a consent whose subject the signers could not read, and I do not ask for that to be excused. I ask only that it be on the file.*

*Pell.*

That last word was not hers and she would not have permitted it. He had written *Rusk*, and she had put *Pell* under it, which is what a Registry counter-signature looks like, and which is not what this was.

"I did not witness that," she said, when he put it down in front of her. "I did not see you draft it and I did not see you write the statement, and I am not a party to it."

"I know. I want your name on the entry so that the thing is dated by somebody who is not me."

"Then I will do the date and not the entry, and you will let me do it that way, and you will not go and tell a docket clerk that a junior clerk refused a countersignature." She read the statement anyway, all the way through, and gave it back. "The date is what I am on it for. Nothing else."

"It's true," she said, "and it is the worst thing you have ever written, and it is the first document anybody in this building has put in a drawer that was about a person instead of a schedule." She went to her tray. "And if you had written it four days ago I would not have signed the observation. So do not thank me, and do not tell anybody I did anything. Pike has been in twice asking whether a countersignature desk was free this afternoon, and I would find out what he wants before I let him ask a third time."

---

He filed his statement into the same file as the ward's petition, so that the Office's transfer and the ward's return would sit in one drawer, and the docket clerk did it without comment because it is allowed and because a docket clerk in his sixties has stopped being surprised by the shape of a case and started being surprised by the number of them.

Then he went back downstairs and put the short form in his own drawer, and looked at the nine pages of the ward's return, and worked out, with a clarity that was almost restful, that he was not going to be able to enter them.

There was no qualified signer. That was the whole of it. The return was signed by nine adults and witnessed by two living observers with separable interests and narrowed and disputed and complete on its face, and it could not be entered by the Office because every officer in the building who could enter a filing was a person whose own name would be on it, and the one return in the room that wanted entering was a return about a ward he lived in and a case he had brought and a draft he had copied, and there was no version of a Registry of Continuant Works in which the man who wrote a return could also lodge it.

He was still at the desk at six when the runner came down the stair with a sealed envelope and put it on the rail in front of him, and the seal on it was not the district's and not the Office's.

It was a Council seal, and round the edge of the flap in a small steady hand was a second signature that was not the Registrar's, and Owen stood there with his hand flat on the envelope for slightly longer than a man needs to stand with his hand flat on an envelope.

---

Two days to the roll. Seven days to an answer that was not a hearing. A ward that had just been told, kindly and in advance, that nothing it had done had saved it.

Pell had a supervised tray at the second desk with a case on it and nobody entering for it, and the case was a return for a widowed household in Fetter Bend, and it was waiting — Owen knew, because he had looked — for an assisting clerk, and there was not one in the building, because the last two assisting clerks had gone to the seal stair and were not coming back.

And on the first floor, in an office with a door that shut properly, a man who had spent a quarter saying the word *maybe* had the sixteen names in front of him again, and had begun, on the morning of the twelfth, to write them at the top of a sheet in a column, because a sheet with names on it is a schedule, and a schedule can be signed, and a signature is a thing that is actionable by Friday.
