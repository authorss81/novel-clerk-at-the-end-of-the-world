# Chapter 71 — Nobody Had Looked

The second season of a ward is a thing that happens to the calendar and not to the people in it, and the first Tuesday of it looks exactly like the last Tuesday of the one before, and the only thing anybody in Fetter Lane could have said was that the granary had been open and empty for six days.

There is a stove in the ward hall and about eleven people in it on the Tuesday morning of the thirteenth day of the second month of the Concord Year 488, and the hall is not a meeting and there is nothing on the table anybody has to sign, and the weather has turned again the way it does in that month, which is to say that it has not turned at all, only felt different, and everybody has noticed and nobody has mentioned it.

**The ward's week does not stop.** The marker goes up on the Thursday and the Friday at seven forty. The channel goes on the Wednesday night, because the channel is a Wednesday and has been a Wednesday since before the return. The pump is primed at four in the morning on the Thursday, which is the hour at which two people get up in this ward to do a thing for six other people who are not going to be asked about it, and one of the two households has a girl of twelve in it, and the girl is not on the return and has never been on the return and carries the notices to the mill's factor on Tuesdays because her aunt cannot read what the Office hands her.

The crew is nine. The wage is four months in arrears. The crossing is closed to loads and to anything above four tons, and it has been closed since the tenth day of the month of rain in a year that ended six weeks ago, and the abutment is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds, and the notice at the weir path inviting any district able to perform the repair is in the weather and has not been taken down and has brought nobody.

He was up Fetter Lane at about half past three on the Wednesday morning because he had not slept, and there is a pump house at the top of the lane and a standpipe at the bottom of it, and the ward primes that pump at four in the morning on a Thursday, and he knows that, and the pump house was locked and the standpipe was dry and there was nobody in the lane.

That is the whole of it and it is worth putting down in the order it happened rather than in the order it is worth something.

**Nobody from the district office is on the rota. There is no column for it and there is no form. The roster that carries the hours is the ward's own document, on the ward's own paper, and it has five names on it, and any person not of the district and not of the office may read it against the ward's book on any day without notice and may take a copy, and nobody may ask them why, and the wage attached to it is four months in arrears and is not funded anywhere in this city that anybody has found.** And the second of the ward's four terms is that no person of the Registry speaks for this district, and it was ruled a term and not a request on the thirteenth day of the first month by Marguerite Bray in about nine words, and Owen Rusk did not agree to it and did not refuse it and is bound by it, and the person it costs is not in this district.

He stood in the lane at about four feet from the door of the pump house for about a quarter of an hour, on a night that was not a bad night, with nothing to do and no form on which a person may stand in a lane, and then he went back down and did the tray and was on it at a quarter past seven.

Cobb Rennie has asked for the same thing three times this season and has been told it three times, and the last of them was on the tenth day of the second month in about six words on a path above a mill race, and he said *good*, and he has not walked slower since, and a barrow of silt goes into another man's water every week and is on no record in this county and has no column, and he has said good about that as well.

Owen Rusk did the tray on the Tuesday morning and was on it at a quarter past seven, and there was nothing on it from anywhere, and he did it again in the evening and there was nothing on it from anywhere then either.

---

He did the arithmetic in a lane because his own rooms had a table and the table had a lamp and the lamp made him think he was working, which is a thing he has been doing since the autumn and has not told anybody about.

It is not a difficult sum. It took him about forty minutes and it is the whole of what the season was, and he wrote it out on the back of a sheet from a tide table in pencil because there was nothing else in the building to write it on, and he wrote it in two columns, and the columns were these.

**LEFT. The document. RIGHT. The last date on which anybody in this county is on the page went and looked at the place the document describes.**

The schedule of settlements to be reconciled, printed in Concord Hall in the last week of the Concord Year 487, posted in advance at Cutter's Fields on the seventy-seventh day, and it names eleven places. Right-hand column, in eleven places: nothing. Nobody went anywhere. It was printed from a return.

The return of authorised draws, made on the twenty-eighth of October by a man with twenty-two years in a room, taken from the season before it and corrected. Right-hand column: nothing again, and this time not even the return's own method has changed since the year the wall went, and the figures in it are figures of quantities, and a quantity is a thing that was measured or an administrative act, and nobody in this county has ever been told which.

The count sheet of a settlement, one page, four columns, and it is the county's only description of the place. Right-hand column: the year in its second column, and three of them say four hundred and seventy-nine and one says four hundred and eighty-one and seven say four hundred and eighty-six. Nine years, and a settlement that is on a printed list of places to be reconciled this season has not been looked at since the year in that column, and the reason it is on the list is that it is nine years old.

The count, certified on the ninth, qualified, nine of eleven. Right-hand column: **nine.** Nine settlements, and he was at most of them and has the day-book's nine lines to prove it and the two that are missing on a sheet wired to a board.

The fifth column, headed year of last survey. Right-hand column: nothing. Not a year old. Nothing.

And then, at the bottom of the left-hand column, in a different ink because it was a different week, the one figure on the whole of that board that anybody in this county can check.

**LOWER LOCK. 486. Eleven.**

Eleven doors, and eleven is the correct figure, and it is correct because a crew of nine went up a weir path every week of the flood season with a tape and a spade and wrote the number down themselves, and it is the freshest figure on that board and it is nine days old, and it is the only row anybody on it can verify without a boat.

And beside that, on the same sheet, in the other column, he added the third column of the board because he could not help it, and the third column came to **seventy-seven**, which is the doors of eleven places, and which has nothing whatever to do with the water, and which is the column he read nine times in nine days and argued about in a field in front of thirty people, and it is the wrong column, and he has known it is the wrong column since the fourth day of this month and has said so out loud twice.

He looked at the two columns for a while, and then he wrote one line under them, and the line is the whole of the season and it is not a discovery he made so much as a thing he stopped being able to unsee.

**Not one document of the season was made after anybody had been to the place it describes, and the one figure on that board that anybody has looked at is the one that got a reason column.**

Because that is the arithmetic. A settlement goes on a schedule of settlements to be reconciled because of what it draws, and the draw comes off a return, and the return is made of premises of record, and a premise of record is written down the last time anybody had a reason to write it down, which is the last time a door was built, a door came down, or a person came forward to be counted. **A door does not write to anybody.** He had said that in a field in the first week of the season in about four sentences and had thought it was the discovery. It is the mechanism. The board is not a list of places that need counting. It is a list of places somebody chose before they looked, chosen for being unlooked-at, in a building two hundred miles up a river, out of a column of doors, by a person who did not go and did not need to.

And a settlement is not struck from that list when it has been counted. It is struck when nobody wants to go. And the two kinds of coming off are the same instrument, and one of them has a schedule of premises attached to it and a date and a warden and a figure in a box, and the other one has nothing at all on any paper anywhere in this county, and there is no form for it, and **there is no row for it.**

---

He went down to the field on the Wednesday afternoon of the fourteenth because a board is a thing a man can look at instead of a person, and the lane is only so long.

The day-book was off the trestle table. That was the first thing he saw and he stood and looked at the bare trestle for longer than a man needs to, because a public day-book with a quota at the head of it is the one sheet in Docketford district that anybody walks up to read, and it had been up for nine weeks and it had gone nine, and the season had closed inside the sixth week with the sheet two lines short of a quota of eleven and nobody had put a figure anywhere about the two.

In its place was a new one, blank, with the same printing and the same head line and a line for the quota that had nothing in it. Not a season's book. A shape that would become one in the last week of the year, when the people who fill that line in fill it in, which is the same people who filled it in last year.

Above it on the frame were the count of the ninth, and Kade's single sheet under the count with one loop of tarred cord, and the reason on it that is a page long and is not a figure. The woman who maintains the board was on the ladder with a scraper in her hand, doing to the season's account what she had done to the standing extract three weeks before, and the man of sixty was holding the ladder and had been holding ladders for a long time and did not say anything while she did it.

Owen Rusk read the count. He had read it four times on the Friday it went up and once in a hall with thirty people in it, and he read it again because the board is where he goes.

Then he read the schedule of settlements to be reconciled, which is still on the frame above the count with its ten names' predecessor, eleven names, five columns, and he read the third column down the way he reads every column in this county, which is the premises of record, and he said the row numbers out loud under his breath without meaning to, because he has never in his life been able to help it.

Nine. Four. Six. **Eleven.** Two. Nine. Seven. Three. Eleven. Nine. Six.

Three rows at four hundred and seventy-nine, one at four hundred and eighty-one, seven at four hundred and eighty-six, and a count that was not taken last year at all, and a board on which the fourth row is a ward of two hundred and forty people and the eleventh is a man who apologised in writing for being late.

"You are counting it again," said the woman who maintains the board, from the ladder, without looking down.

"I am."

"You have counted it nine times this season and it is the same nine figures and one of them is yours." She came down two rungs and looked at the frame. "**The new one goes up on the twenty-eighth.** Ten names on it. They have told me there is a column on it that is new and they have not told me what it is and I did not ask, because I have maintained that frame for nine years and I have never once asked what goes on it, and that is the arrangement and I would like it to keep being the arrangement."

"Ten names."

"Ten names and a column I have not seen." She put the scraper in the bucket with the nails in it. "I do not mind a column. I mind a column that has to be filled in by somebody, because the ones I mind are the ones that come back with something written in them that is not a figure, and then I have got to wire up a column that is not a figure in a field behind a tannery, and there is no form in this county that says what a person should do with that."

Owen Rusk stood in the field behind the tanneries with his hands in his coat and did not say anything to that, because there was no answer to it that was not a sentence about a document, and every one of those was already going to be on somebody's paper by the twenty-eighth.

Junia Pell was at the end of the second desk on the Tuesday afternoon with the returned form in her hand, because a form that comes back from a counter is read by a clerk before it is read by an officer, and she is the clerk, and she had been in the building three months and eleven days.

"Four lines from a counter in Concord Hall and every one of them is correct," she said, "and I am not being funny, and I have read it four times because the third one is the one that will be quoted. *The district office is requested to complete the column or to state that it is unable.*" She put her finger on it. "**You are not able. That is now the district's answer about a column in the district's own hand and it is going up a stair in about a minute and it is going to come back in June with a note on it, and in a few years somebody is going to ask this office to state, on the record, whether it has ever surveyed anything.**"

"I wrote the sentence across the cells."

"I know what you wrote across the cells, and it was a good sentence and it was not a survey, and a sentence is not a year, and the Office is right, and I would like you to notice that you have just been taught a new word by a form and that the word is *unable*, and that nobody taught it to you and nobody meant anything by it, and that it is a word a person in this county uses about a thing he has not done and cannot do, and it is going to be in a file, and in four years it is going to read as though you had tried."

He looked at it for a while.

"**Then I am going to be able to say that I sent it anyway,**" he said, "which is the one sentence I know to be true and it is a small one and it is the whole of what I have."

"That is the one sentence you know to be true," said Junia Pell, "and I have written *thin* about you once already this season and I am not going to do it twice about the same man."

He walked back up the hill at about half past four and did the tray and was on it at a quarter past seven, and there were two things on it and both of them were dates.

The first was a slip from the seal stair. The form with the fifth column on it had gone up on the Monday and had come back down on the Tuesday afternoon, in the same bag, in the same hands, with a note at the foot of it in a clerk's hand at a counter, and the note is four lines long and is correct, and it says that the fifth column is not completed, that the Office does not accept a sentence in a column reserved for a year, that the district office is requested to complete the column or to state that it is unable, and that the return is returned for completion and not for the file.

*"Unable,"* said Owen Rusk, to the tray. *"There is a word in this county for what I have done and it is a word a person is born knowing and I have had to be taught it."*

And the second thing was the form itself, with the ten cells in the fifth column empty and the note at the foot of it, and the day in the top right corner in the same hand as the note, and there was a space left for the seal of the count room and nothing in it, because the count room does not seal a thing it has not finished with.

Sixteen days. He had said that out loud to himself on the Monday and it was still sixteen on the Wednesday, and on the Friday a sheet a yard wide was going to come across the cut with eleven names on it and the quantities that had chosen them, and the man of fifty-five had said he would put it in his hand himself if Owen Rusk was in the room, and that it was a Tuesday and Fridays and the other days when the fields were under that he went up the reach, and that the season was over and there was no reason in the world to be in that room on a Friday except the reason everybody in this county has now, which is that a document is a document and a sum made with a pencil is a conversation.

He put the pen down and did not leave until about eight, and he did not go up the reach, and he did not write to anybody, and on the wall in front of him the fifth column of a form for the season of 489 was ten empty cells and a clerk's note in four lines saying that a sentence is not a year.
