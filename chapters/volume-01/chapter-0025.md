# Chapter 25 — Be Told No Kindly

The four names had been in his notebook since the seventh day of the month in a column headed, in his own writing, *the far end*, and under that heading there were four lines, and beside two of the four lines he had written the word *priced*, which was a word he had taken from her mouth and had never given back.

Elias Trant, millwright, above Wexford Landing.
A holder of a tide table at Coal Hill, and I have never once been told her name. — *priced*
Ivet Sterne, ferry tally, Wexford Landing. — *priced*
And the fourth, which he had never written a surname for, because she had not given him one and he had not asked and that had been a rule he had set for himself in a warm room.

He got the ferry up to Mill Ward on the Thursday afternoon of the thirty-first, three hours after he had been told in a ward hall to go and find a stranger, and told himself on the boat that he was not going to Arbour Kitchen, and he was not, and that was fine, and he was going.

---

Arbour Kitchen at four in the afternoon on the thirty-first had nine people in it and a woman asleep on the bench by the stove with a folder on her knees, and Nell Arbour was at the back with her ledger open and her left hand held slightly away from her body in the way it had been held for six years, and she did not get up.

"You have not been to see me for four weeks," she said.

"No."

"That is not a reproach, that is an inventory. I want it on the record that you did not come, and that I noticed, and that I am not going to make a speech about it, because you would only write the speech down." She turned a page. "What do you want."

"I want somebody from outside this ward to come and check a roster in public, once, on a day of the ward's choosing, and I have got four names and I have carried them for four weeks and used none of them."

"Then you have come to the wrong building twice," said Nell Arbour. "There is no version of this in which that is my work."

"I know that. I have not come to ask you to do it."

"You have come to ask me to do a thing next to doing it." She put the pen down. "Say the rest of it, because I have known you nine years and I know what your face does when you have already asked."

Owen Rusk had come twenty minutes' walk upriver in the rain to be told no, and he had a request, and he had come to the correct person, and he said:

"I need you to give me one of them. Only one. Just tell me which of the four I should go and ask, and I will do the rest of it and I will not use your name."

It is a small sentence and it took him a second and a half to say and it is the worst thing he has said in a month, and he heard it in the room, and Nell Arbour heard it in the room, and neither of them pretended.

"No," she said.

"That is not—"

"I know what it is not." She did not raise her voice and the kitchen went on being a kitchen. "You have come to me to spend the only thing I have got, which is that when I send somebody, they are mine, and I have never once in six years had to spend that on a man's timetable, and I am not going to start in the fourth week of a rain season because you have got a Friday."

"You could have said no to the four names in the first place."

"I could have," said Nell Arbour, "and I did not, and the reason I did not is a reason you have not earned, and the reason is that a person who needs a far end of a route should be able to find one without an introduction, and if the route can only be walked through a kitchen in Mill Ward then it is not a route, it is a favour with a legal shape."

She squared the ledger.

"Here is the condition, and it has not changed since the seventh day of the month, and I am going to say it now so that you can go up the hill and be certain of it. **I will help with the route and with nothing else.** I am not a witness to your return. I am not an advocate for Lower Lock. I am not yours. If your ward's route ever needs a person standing at the end of it, I will help you look for one and I will not put my name anywhere near it, and if the rest of your case falls over I will not pick it up."

"I have not asked you for any of that."

"No," she said. "You have not. And I have said it anyway, because you have been away four weeks and men come back changed and I would rather tell you where the edge is than have you find out in front of somebody else. And now go and ask somebody, and ask them properly, because you have four names and no practice, and a man who turns up with a wet coat and the word *checked* in his mouth is going to be turned away for the right reason."

---

He asked badly at about eight on the Friday morning of the thirty-second, at a mill above Wexford Landing, and he asked badly because he had spent the whole of the ferry and most of the night rehearsing a version in which he did not ask for anything, which is the standard way a man asks for everything.

Elias Trant was fifty-odd and had a millwright's hands and was in the middle of something when he came out, and Owen stood in the wet yard and got about four seconds in before the man said:

"You have got about four minutes and then I have to go and do a thing with a wheel. What is it."

Owen did the whole thing in one sentence, and it was not a good sentence. He said the Registry, and the ward, and a return, and a person from outside the district, and *the ward has agreed to be checked*, and *I am looking for somebody with no interest in the outcome*, and the word *checked* did all the work and it was the wrong word and he heard it doing the work about half way through.

Trant listened to the end of it.

"No," he said.

"Right."

"You have not asked me the actual question yet."

"I have just asked you the actual question."

"You have asked me whether I will come and look at a piece of paper," said Trant, "which is a thing I do not mind, and the answer to that is no, and it is no kindly, and here it is in one piece so you do not have to stand in my yard finding out. I looked at a mill return once. Sixteen years ago. I was asked to look at it because a man in a district office wanted a second name on a thing and I gave him one, because I thought a name was a small thing, and it was a small thing, and I was a millwright for another nine years afterwards and the only thing anybody remembered me for was the return."

He put his hand on the mill door.

"I will not do it again. That is not a wound, that is a preference, and the reason is that I have got nothing to lose here and you have got a hallful of people's hours on a sheet, and a man with nothing to lose is not a check. He is a tourist. You want somebody who could lose something and does it anyway, and that is a different kind of person and it is not in this yard."

"That is fair," said Owen.

"It is fair and it is also the last time I am going to explain it, so write it down." He looked at Owen's notebook, which was not out. "Write it down. Because you will want to write down that I was rude to you and you are going to be wrong about that, and in a month you are going to tell somebody in a hall that a man at a mill refused a ward, and what actually happened is that a man at a mill told you the truth in a yard and you did not like the shape of it."

---

Wexford Landing is a stone stair and a tide table nailed to a post and a woman who keeps the table in a hut at the top of it. She is not an officer of anything, has never been one, and is not on any list that Owen has ever seen. She is also, and this is worth a paragraph because it has cost him four weeks of carrying four names in a column, not the woman at Coal Hill. Two tide tables. Two landings. Two women, both of them difficult, both of them used, and the only thing Owen had ever done about it was to write one of them down in a line and let the other one be understood, which is a thing a man does with a list and is precisely why nobody has ever thought to ask him about it.

She had the ferry tally in front of her and a dozen crossings in it for the day and she wrote up the last while Owen stood in the door, and when she had finished she put the pen in the tin and said:

"You are the Registry man from the bridge."

"Yes."

"Sit down, you are dripping on the tally."

He sat down. He had rehearsed four things on the boat and threw all four of them away in about ninety seconds, because she had not asked him a single question and he discovered that a person who does not ask you anything has already decided how long this is going to take and it is not the length you want.

She gave him nine minutes. She was exact about the nine and he was exact about it afterwards, and he wrote it in the register that night as *nine minutes, and she counted them, and she did not waste one of them being kind.*

"You want somebody to come and look at a roster," she said, "once, and stand there, and say what she saw. And you have come to me because a woman named Arbour gave you four names and I am one of them, and because I keep a tide table, which is a document that anybody may read and nobody may check, and you have decided that a person who is used to being checked would be good at it."

"Yes."

"I am going to say no," said Ivet Sterne. "And I am going to tell you why, and the why is the useful part, and you may take it or leave it."

She put her hands flat on the table.

"I am not an inspector. I have no office, I cannot be cited, I cannot suspend a thing, I cannot be cited by a court or a ward or your Deputy Registrar, and I have no seal and no mark and nothing in me at all. My whole power is that I was there. That is all of it. I come on a Thursday and I write down what I saw on the Thursday, and somebody who was not there reads it, and that is a check and it is the only kind there is. And the second half of my power is that I can stop. If I do not come on the next Thursday, then the thing is not being checked, it is being witnessed, and there is a difference and everybody in this business knows it and nobody ever says it out loud. And no office in Docketford can take that off me. You cannot buy it, you cannot suspend it, you cannot countersign it away, and you cannot transfer it. That is the entire reason I am careful, and it is the reason I am expensive, and I am not expensive in anything you could offer me. I am expensive in Thursdays."

"You have written things down," said Owen.

"You want to know what I would want. So here is what I would want, and it took me nine years, and I am going to give it to you in a kitchen in Mill Ward and I want it in your book exactly, and I want your ward to see it and not just you."

She put the list on the table in about forty seconds flat.

**One.** I am not a witness to your return and I will not be named on it and I will not sign anything your ward performs. I write my own line or I do not come.

**Two.** I come when I have said I am coming, and not otherwise, and nobody may ask me on the day why I have not.

**Three.** A person of the ward may ask me to come. The Registry may not. If the request comes from that building I will not come at all, and I will not say that at the time.

**Four.** I am paid nothing and I take nothing, and if anybody in that ward offers me a meal or a wage or a bed I will not come again.

**Five.** I read the roster and I do not read the ward's book. The roster is theirs to publish. Their book is theirs.

**Six.** Do not ask me who sent me, and do not write my name in a return, and do not say in a hall that the Registry's man found me.

"That is better than anything I could have written," said Owen, "and I have been writing things like it for four weeks."

"I know. I have read your ward's return. A man on the boat had it. I read it because the woman at Coal Hill told me to read it, and she is the one I have had words with, and I read it because she is usually right and I usually do not do it." She took the list back. "Now. No."

"Right."

"Not yet," said Ivet Sterne. "That is not the same, and you have got until four o'clock this afternoon to find the difference in, so I will give it to you. Your ward has agreed to have its hours read by a stranger. Whose hours?"

"The crew's. The pump. The standpipe rota. The granary count. Four houses and a man of fifty-eight on the standpipe."

"Then what is the stranger for," she said. "You have told me a roster will be checked and you have not told me what a check is worth. I have watched this happen at two other mills. Eight people's hours go onto a sheet, a stranger comes four times, everybody behaves, and at the end of it the wages are exactly what they were, and the sheet goes in a drawer and is read by nobody, and the ward learns that a stranger reading a roster does not feed anybody. And the four people on that standpipe rota are going to find out inside a month that being legible is not the same as being paid, and they are going to be right, and after that no stranger in this delta is going to get a Thursday out of them for free."

"So what do you want."

"I want to know whether the wage is funded."

"It is not."

"Then I will tell you what I have decided," said Ivet Sterne, "and I have not decided it yet, and I am going to go up the stair and think about it, and you are going to come back at four o'clock this afternoon with three answers, and if I like them I will come for one day, and one day is all of it, and I will write you the reason why it is one day in your book so that whoever asks me next year knows I said it out loud the first time. And I am giving you five hours and not three days, and I want you to understand that the five hours is the answer to the question you are about to ask me, which is *why so soon*, and the answer is that a person who gives you three days is buying something, and I am not selling."

---

He got the three answers on the landing at four o'clock in the rain on the Friday afternoon of the thirty-second, and he had come back on the mid-morning boat and sat on the top step out of the wind for the rest of the day with a notebook and a bad sandwich, which is a thing that a man who has asked a woman for a favour spends a day doing.

**One.** What the wage was. The crew's wage, four months in arrears, not a fund and not a grant, an arrear, and the figure, which he said out loud on a landing because a person who will not say a number is a person who wants something.

**Two.** Who had asked her. Not him. The ward. In writing, on the ward's paper, not the Registry's. And this one nearly lost it, and he lost most of an hour on it, and the reason is worth putting down because it is the whole of what he has learned in a month: he had come up the hill with the answer ready, and the answer was a sentence he wanted to be able to say, and the sentence was *the ward has asked you*, and the ward had not asked her. The ward had agreed, in a hall, on the Thursday morning, in front of twenty-two people, to be read by a person from another district on a day of the ward's own choosing — and Marguerite Bray had ruled that minute into the delivery book in her own hand, with her name at the foot of it, and the day had not been named, and nobody had asked her yet, and a Registry man with an armful of answers could have said *asked* and been believed.

What he said instead was: *the ward has agreed to it in a book, on Thursday, in a room with twenty-two people in it, and the line has her name at the bottom of it, and the day is not named and I am not going to name it, because a day named by a clerk in a Registry building is a day you were sent.*

Ivet Sterne read his face for a second and then said, "That is the request. I will take the book and not a letter, and on the day somebody from that ward will hand it to me with both hands, and if it comes out of a coat like yours I will go home."

**Three.** What happened after. Nothing yet, and the honest answer, which was the answer that nearly lost it: if it is not funded then the roster is a list of what people are owed and not a list of what they are paid for, and the room is going to know that by the second week, and the correct thing to do about it is for the ward to say so itself before somebody from a stranger's hall says it for them.

Ivet Sterne listened to all three and then went and got a pencil and wrote four lines on the back of a ferry ticket and gave them to him, and this is the whole of them:

*I will come on the thirty-eighth day of this month from first light to dark, to the pump, to the path, to the hall, and I will write what I saw in my own hand and give it to the ward and not to the office. One day. On the day, a person of that ward hands me the delivery book with both hands, and if the paper comes out of a coat like yours I go home. I will come again on any further day the ward itself names in writing, having asked me, and there is no day after that unless they write again. If the wage is not funded I will say so in my own line, in my own words, and I will not soften it, and the ward must not ask me to.*

Owen Rusk stood on a ferry landing in the rain with a piece of paper in his hand and understood that he had been given a better instrument than anything he had written in a month, by a person who owed him nothing, had met him once, and had spent nine minutes counting.

---

Nell Arbour was on the landing when he came off the ferry. She had come up on the early boat and had not said so and had been standing at the top of the stone stair for most of the day, and she had been in Ivet Sterne's hut for the first five minutes of it and had not said one word in there.

Owen said, "She will come on the Thursday."

"I heard. She told me at the top of the stair and I am not going to repeat it and I am not going to help you with any of it."

"I know. I did not ask you."

"You did not ask me," said Nell, "and you are not arguing, and that is the thing I have been standing on this landing all day waiting to see whether you would do, and I want you to know that I noticed."

She put her left hand in her coat.

"Same condition," she said. "The route and nothing else. I have not helped you with this and you are not to write down that I have, and if your ward asks me in a fortnight I am going to say the same four sentences, and you are not to be hurt about it, and you are not to use it as evidence that I have come round."

"I would not."

"I know you would not. That is the part I have had for four weeks." She looked at the water, which was doing the thing it does in the fourth week. "You have found somebody with no stake in it. That is not what I would have found, and you did not ask me what I would have found, and I want you to notice that that is the first time in nine years that you wanted what I would have done less than you wanted the thing."

Owen Rusk did not have an answer to that and did not manufacture one, and Nell Arbour got on the half past ferry and went down to her own afternoon, and the thing he took upriver with him, on a wet Friday in the fourth week of the rain season, was a ferry ticket with four lines on the back of it, and a return that had come due that same day, and a ward that had set a marker at seven forty that morning and would set it again on Thursday for the sixth week running, and was going to be read, on a day of its own choosing and on its own paper, by a woman who had told him to his face that being legible is not the same as being paid.
