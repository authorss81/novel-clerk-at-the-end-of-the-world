# Chapter 34 — The Circular Names the Review

It came on the Wednesday morning of the forty-fourth day of the month on a runner's tray, sealed, and it was not for the copying floor and it was not for the district office, and it went up to the third landing of the seal stair and stopped there, because a sealed thing goes to a stair and not to a counter, and everybody in the building knew that and nobody has ever written it down.

Owen Rusk was on the second floor with a tray of correspondence at ten to seven and he saw it go past and he went up after it, and the seal-stair clerk had the seal-breakers out by the time he got there.

"You are early," she said. "And you are not on this."

"I am not on anything, and I have watched a thing come up a stair and I have come up after it, and you can tell me to go down."

"I can, and I am not going to, because a man who has come up a stair after a thing is a man who will be in the room, and I would rather have you in the room than have you guessing at it in a corridor." She broke the seal. It was two sheets and a covering, and the covering was the Council's own, and the seal was the Council's, and there was no name on it anywhere. There was a second signature at the foot of the second sheet in a small steady hand that was not the Registrar's, and Owen looked at it for about a second and a half and then deliberately did not look at it again.

"It is the second one this year," said the seal-stair clerk. "The first came on the ninth day of the second month and named a closing date. Read it standing up and do not lean on my rail."

---

*Sheet one.* To the district offices of the Registry of Continuant Works. The Provisional Council notes that a local return in the district of Docketford, entered in the present month under a district file number, is upon its own interval of review; and that the interval has now been renewed more than once without the return having been treated as binding; and that the Council has no jurisdiction over the interval, and does not propose to acquire one.

*Sheet one, continued.* The Council requests, for the information of the Council and not as a finding, to be furnished at the receiving table at Concord Hall within twenty-one days of the date of this circular: (i) the schedule of acts performed by the district in the quarter, in the district's own form, with the names of the persons who performed them and the hours at which the acts were done; (ii) the ground of the second-examination arrangement in force in this district, and the authority by which it is in force; (iii) the disposition forms used by the district office in the quarter, and the number of times each was used.

*Sheet two.* The Council notes that in the quarter a district of this registry has performed a duty in public, upon a roster, before a person of another district who was not of the office; that the roster was offered for reading by any person; and that the person who performed the duty has recorded in her own hand that a wage attaching to the performance is not funded. The Council does not think that this practice requires discouragement. The Council thinks it requires watching.

Owen Rusk read sheet two three times and then stood on a stair in a Registry building and had to put his hand on the rail, and the seal-stair clerk, who had been watching him and not pretending otherwise, said nothing at all and let him have about forty seconds.

"**Requires watching**," he said.

"It is the Council's own hand and it is not the Registrar's, and I have read that word on one document in nineteen years and it was on a paper about a district works schedule and it was the reason a bridge was load-restricted for a term and never repaired." She squared the sheets. "Read it as a compliment and it is a compliment. Read it as a sentence about what a thing may become and it is a sentence about what a thing may become, and I would put it on the wall of this building if I were permitted to put anything on the walls of this building, and I am not."

"It names the review."

"It names the review, and not the petition, and not the order, and not the crossing, and that is the sentence in the whole document." She turned the covering sheet round and read a line off it. "Issued on the day the Docketford hearing application was entered. Day forty-three. Yesterday." She put the covering down. "Which means somebody in that building was told about it the day it was entered, or the day before, and I would not give you odds."

---

He spent the rest of the morning and the whole of the afternoon on the second floor, and what he spent it on was watching a careful office become a carefuler one, and it is worth doing properly, because it is the least dramatic thing that will happen to anybody this week and the one that changes most.

It happened in four small pieces and he saw all four.

The first was at about half past ten at the countersignature desk. A clerk came out of the district office with a folded slip and went to the second desk and put it in front of Junia Pell and said four words, and Owen, who was forty feet away at a tray, could not hear them and did not need to. Pell read the slip, did not look up from it, and said something back that made the clerk stop where she was standing. Then the clerk came back about ten minutes later with a second slip, and Pell signed that one, and Owen understood that the office had asked her for her separation of interest in advance of a renewal, in writing, with a day on it, which is a thing no office in this building had done to her in the three years and four months she had been on the roll.

The second was at about one. A runner came up the second floor with a request, and it was for the ward, and it was for Lower Lock, and it came off the district office's own letter paper and it asked the ward for its schedule of performed acts, by a day, in the form the ward used, and it gave a reason, and the reason was written in the box that says *purpose of request*, and the reason was: *that it may be furnished to the Council within the period named in the circular of this date.* It took four minutes to arrive and it was the first request anybody in that building had sent to that ward in six weeks, and Owen read it twice and then went and stood at the end of the second desk and looked at the tray.

The third was at about half past two. The reviewing officer's attendance for the Friday was confirmed in writing, with an hour, and a room, and the room was not the review room with the six chairs and the clock. It was the long room at the end of the second floor with the high windows, and the reason given in the covering line, in a clerk's hand, was: *in which papers may be produced and read.* And that was new. The Friday review had been a review. On Friday it was going to be a room in which a ward's own papers could be produced and read, and the difference between those two things is about twenty feet of length in the room and about four hundred years of practice, and it had been arranged in about two hours by a person who had not eaten.

The fourth was the one that frightened him, and it came at about four.

Pell came off the tray at four o'clock with a bundle of the quarter's ordinary work and put it down and sat on the end of the bench, which she does, and said:

"They have begun producing things early."

"What things."

"Things nobody has asked for." She had her hand on the bundle and was not opening it. "I have had four of my own comparisons sent up to the record room this afternoon, unasked, with a note saying they were produced early for the convenience of a review. I have not asked for a review of anything. Nobody has asked for a review of my comparisons. Somebody upstairs has decided that this month is the month in which papers are produced early, and no reason has been given to me, and I am not going to ask for one, because if I ask for one I will be told that the office produces papers early when it is in the interest of accuracy, and that will be true, and it will have been said."

Owen stood at the end of the bench and understood the mechanism exactly, and it took him about four seconds, and it was the fourth time in six weeks that understanding something had been a bad thing.

"It is not accuracy," he said. "It is the circular. The office has been named in a document about a review it has no jurisdiction over, and a named office does not stop being careful. It stops being *indifferent*. And an indifferent office is slow, and a slow office loses a quarter, and a careful office under a Council circular produces its papers early and asks its officers to separate in writing before they are asked and books a room with high windows so that a ward can be read at length, and every single thing it has done today has made the ward's case *easier to examine*."

"Yes," said Pell.

"That is what I am frightened of," said Owen Rusk. "Not that they will be unfair. That they will be extremely fair, at length, in a room with a clock, in front of a ward that has three of five parts of a thing about a child and a wage of four months and a bin count coming down on Wednesday."

She capped her pen.

"Then you have understood it," she said, "and I am going to say the rest of it, and then I am going to go and do a tray, and I am not going to say this again in a way you can quote at anybody. **An office that is watched performs better, and better is not the same as fair, and fair is not the same as yours.** Those are three different things and a man who has spent six weeks learning the first one is the most likely person in the building to stop being able to tell them apart. Go home. I have a tray."

---

The ward hall on the Wednesday night at half past seven had the stove going and about thirty people in it, and the reason it had thirty people in it was that the channel had been cleared at the top of the weir path two hours before and the granary count had been done in the bins, and the room was warm and smelled of wet coats and paraffin and grain dust, and everybody was in a good mood in the specific way that a room is in when the water has dropped and nothing has gone wrong.

Owen Rusk came in with two things: a sealed circular that had been broken, in a folded sheet, and a request on the district office's letter paper.

"I am going to read this out in full," he said. "All of it, including the part that is a compliment, and I am not going to summarise it, and Mrs Bray is going to read it back after me because I have been told once in six weeks that the reading is the entire instrument and I have thought about that sentence every day since."

He read it.

He read the first sheet, and the three requests, and the sentence about the Council having no jurisdiction over the interval and not proposing to acquire one, and the covering line, and the date, which was the day before. And then he read sheet two, all of it, including the words *requires watching*, and he read them in the same voice he had used for the rest, and he did not stop at them and he did not slow down, and the room sat and listened to the entire document the way a room listens to a roster.

He did not ask for a second reader. He had been asked for one six weeks ago, in this room, by two women, and he had learned the reason that week, and the reason was that a reading is not a document, it is a man choosing what a sentence was, and the only instrument the ward has for that is a stranger, and there is no stranger in the hall tonight.

Then there was about a minute and a half of a silence, which is what thirty people do with a thing nobody has expected.

"**The Council has asked us for the roster**," said Ada Rusk.

She was at the end of the long table with a slate under her arm, and she had said the five terms in this room on the thirty-first of the month and the fright out loud in front of twenty-four people on the thirty-eighth, and she said this one without any of that.

"The Council has asked for the roster. In its own form. With the names on it and the hours on it." She put the slate down. "We have been putting a sheet of paper with everybody's hours on it into a hall in this ward for a fortnight, on a calendar, on purpose, so that a stranger could come and read it, and we did it because a woman of sixty-one wrote *and the wage* in a book and a woman from a landing upriver told us being legible is not the same as being paid, and none of us has been paid and all of us knew it and we did it anyway. And the Council of this country has read about that somewhere and has written down that it requires watching."

"It is a compliment," said somebody at the back.

"It is a compliment," Ada agreed. "And I am going to say the next part and I would like Mrs Bray to write it down because I think it is the most frightening document any of us will read this year and it is two lines long. If the Council requires watching, then what we are doing is *being watched*, and everything we have done since the seventh day has been done in public on a calendar, and a thing done in public on a calendar is a thing a person can be asked about, and I do not mind being asked about it. I have said since the seventh day that I would rather be legible than be kind. I still say it. So the first half of my feeling about this is that the ward should be pleased, and I would like somebody to write *pleased* down, with the day on it, because in three months one of us is going to need to remember that we were pleased."

"And the second half?" said Wenna Sarr.

"The second half is that the Council has asked for three lists and every one of the three is a list this ward has just made one of." Ada Rusk counted them off on her fingers without any emphasis at all, which was worse than emphasis. "The roster. The ground of the second examination — which is Mr Rusk's, and which is in a book in the district office, and which is four hundred and forty-three years old and has never been amended and whose reasons are *right*, and I have read them and I am not going to pretend they are not right. And the disposition forms the office used in the quarter." She put her hand down. "Three lists. The ward made three lists this month without being asked to make one of them, and this morning a man from the capital wrote down that it wants all three. Mr Rusk, in six weeks I have not once been asked by anybody in that building what this ward *wants*, and this morning somebody wanted to know what this ward *does*, at the hours it does it, with the names on it, and put it in writing with a day on it, and that has not happened in my lifetime and my mother did the same week's work before me."

Owen Rusk stood at the end of the long table in a ward hall and listened to a woman he had spent six weeks overruling be glad about something, and be frightened about the same thing, in the same breath, in the same voice, in front of thirty people, and both of the halves were correct, and he had spent the whole of the sixth week of the rain season working out that being right in a room and being useful in a room are two different products.

He had been right about the circular. He had said, in a stairwell, on a Sunday, that a document in the right room is the only thing in this city that cannot be quietly corrected.

He had not thought, once, about a document being *wanted*.

"Somebody had to want it first," he said.

"Yes," said Ada Rusk. "That is the whole of what I have been trying to say to you since the seventh day and you have only just got here."

Marguerite Bray wrote it in the delivery book. She wrote it in her own ruled hand on the back of a page, in pencil, with the day, and the hour, and the words *the ward is pleased and frightened of this document and both are correct*, and she read it back to the room, and Ada Rusk said *that is the whole of it* and nothing else, and thirty people in a warm hall with grain dust in it made the noise a room makes when it has been handed something it did not know it had been working towards.

And then, at the door, in about nine seconds, with a folded sheet of district office letter paper in her hand, Ada said the other thing.

"Mr Rusk. They have asked us for the roster. In our own form, by a day, with a reason in the box." She held the paper up without unfolding it. "So we are not going to send them the roster. We are going to send them a **schedule**, and the schedule is going to have a covering page and two witnesses' names on it and a line in it about a person being able to take their own branch out of it, and it is going to be in our book first and in their building second, and you are going to carry it up the stair, and you are not going to write a word of it."

"I know," said Owen Rusk.

"I know you know. I said it in the shed on Saturday and I am saying it again in a hall with thirty people in it so that the thirty people have heard it twice." She put the paper in her coat. "Get your coat. It is still raining and I am not asking you to stay."
