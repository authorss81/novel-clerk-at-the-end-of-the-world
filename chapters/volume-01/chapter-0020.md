# Chapter 20 — Who Sent It

The copying floor on a Saturday is the loneliest room Owen Rusk has ever been paid to stand in, and he went into it at nine in the morning on the twenty-sixth day of the month with the ward's copy of the transfer under his arm, and there was nobody in the building at all.

The press was cold. The lamps were out. Sixteen hundred sheets of clean copying paper were drying on the racks in the middle of the floor in a room with no roof noise over it, and the light came in through the high windows in bars, and the whole of the Registry of Continuant Works in Docketford was a large cold room with a smell of dust and ink in it and one man in it doing arithmetic on the ninth of a month eleven months ago.

He had done this arithmetic before. He had done it on the first day of the month, at a desk, and it had taken him about ninety seconds, and it had given him a margin notice and a memorandum and a broken run and a notebook.

It gave him something different this time, and the difference was not in the numbers.

The ninth. The eleventh. Two days, and the schedule says the crossing moved on the ninth, and the counter where it was handed in did not stamp it until the eleventh, and there is no version of the world in which a thing can be received before it is received.

He had known that for a month. What he had never done was ask the other question, which is not *when was this dated* but *by whom, and for what*.

And the answer took him about four seconds, standing at a rack of drying paper, and it was so ordinary that he had to put his hand on the rack.

The ninth was not a date a man chose in order to conceal anything. The ninth was the date a man wrote in because the ninth was the day he had asked.

There was no other reason on earth for a piece of paper to carry the ninth. If you are concealing something you date it later, or you date it nothing, or you leave the line off and let the receiving office fill it in. You do not reach for a day two days before the day the document exists. Nobody dates a document two days early to hide it. People date a document two days early when they have been somewhere and been told no, and they have come away with a date in their head, and they have asked again, and they have been told no again, and the date has gone on the form because the form is where the date goes.

Owen stood in the empty hall and worked out that he had given away, on the sixth day of the month, in a warm room, in front of two people, the entire content of the whole business.

Not a date. A man. A man who had been refused, twice more, and had come back.

---

The ward hall at eight on the Sunday morning had a stove going and a table cleared and two lamps, and Marguerite Bray was there at half past seven because she had known he was coming, because on Saturday afternoon he had stood in her hall for forty minutes and asked her for two things and been given one of them.

"I will show you the book," she said. "You will not have the book. Sit there and look at it over my arm and if you put a wet thumb on a page I will put you out."

"Right."

"The book has a column for what comes in and a column for what goes back out, and I have ruled the second one myself for thirty-six years, ever since I was twenty-five and somebody had to be the first one, because a ward gets more paper than it sends, and everybody forgets to keep track of the paper that comes *back*, and paper that comes back is the paper that somebody has answered." She opened the ledger on the table with a brass weight on it, and turned it round, and put her own hand flat on the page, which is a thing she does instead of pointing. "Your fortnight. The ninth, the tenth, the eleventh, the twelfth, the thirteenth, the fourteenth."

The inward column was where he remembered it. Granary weights. A pump part. A rate notice. A widow's name. A roof notice with a different stamp, and then, near the bottom of the month, the entry he had read in the second week of the month in a good coat with his coat still streaming: *Received 14th day, C.Y. 486. One folded sheet, being the entry and its two leaves, sealed. Brought by T. Sarr of this ward, in person, at the door.*

"Second column," said Bray. "Down here."

The second column was in her hand and it was in pencil and it was short, and it had three lines in it, and the dates on those three lines were the sixteenth, the seventeenth and the eighteenth.

*Returned notice, one sheet. Addressed to a house in Fetter Lane, not to the hall. Came with the post. He said it was for him and I said I would put it in the drawer and he said leave it, and I put it in the drawer.*

*Same.*

*Same. Third of three. Brought by a boy from the counter because the first two came by the post and the third had a different stamp on it.*

"They came back to his house," said Owen. "Not to the hall."

"They came back to the address he gave them, and he gave them a house in Fetter Lane because that is where he lives, and the Office does not send a notice to a ward that has no supported district." Bray turned three pages back and unhooked a paperclip from the corner of a flyleaf, and behind the flyleaf there were three sheets of Registry paper, folded once each, in the standard form, and they were grey with age and one of them had been folded and unfolded and folded again a great many times by somebody who was not careful with paper and was careful with everything else.

She put the first one on the table face up. Owen had seen a form like it in a book on the ground floor of his own building a month ago and had copied three lines of it into a notebook at the ironmonger on Fetter Lane.

*Refused. No supported district at the named place.*

"Yes," he said. "That is one of them."

"It is all three of them. They are the same notice. Somebody wrote it three times." Bray turned the sheet over.

And on the back of the first notice, in pencil, in the ruled-and-slanted hand of a woman who has kept a hand roll for thirty-six years and who has never in her life thrown away a piece of paper anybody sent to her, there were four words.

*Ward public works — schedule wanted.*

Owen looked at the back of the second one.

*Ward public works — schedule wanted.*

And the third.

*Ward public works — schedule wanted.*

And under the third, in the same pencil, and pressed harder, and with two words underlined twice:

*Can one house consent for a ward?*

---

Nobody said anything for a while. The stove ticked. Outside it was the third week of the rain season and the rain had come back overnight and was doing the thing it did.

"Say the rest of it," said Marguerite Bray. "You have got that face on. I have seen it on a Sunday morning once before and it was on my own husband and it is the face a man makes when he has been told the thing he already knew by a person he cannot argue with."

So Owen Rusk told her the rest of it, standing at her table with a wet coat on, in the order the Office keeps in its registers, because there was no other order available to him and because a man reconstructing a year-old morning out of a delivery book should keep the Office's own order.

"A form. Form twelve-B. A request by a person for a district consolidation schedule to be sent out, naming the public works in his district."

"That is what is on the back of three notices."

"That is what is on the back of three notices. Now here is the order of it, and it is the order in a book I have read four times. On the ninth of that month a person in this ward walked two miles upriver to the county intake sub-table at Fetter Bend and asked for a consolidation schedule for this ward's public works. It was refused, and the form of the refusal is on that sheet in your hand, and the ninth is the ninth. He was told there was no supported district at the named place. He came back on the tenth and asked twice more and was refused twice more, and on the back of the second he wrote down *can one house consent for a ward*, and somebody at that counter read that sentence and did not answer it."

"And then?"

"And then he went back a fourth time, on the eleventh, with a sheet. Sealed, signed, witnessed, with a schedule attached. The sub-table received it for intake and forwarded it the same day to the Docketford district office at the Mill Ward annex, and the counter there received it and stamped it on the eleventh, and entered it at the receiving register, and the schedule on the sheet is dated the ninth, and the ninth is the day of the first of the three refusals, and that is why it is the ninth." Owen put his hand flat on the table, on a piece of paper that was eleven months old and had been folded and unfolded by a man who could not answer a question about his own granddaughter. "He was not hiding a date, Mrs Bray. He was the only person in this business who had any idea what the date meant, and it meant *the day I asked and was refused*, and he wanted it in the document because it was the only piece of paper anybody had ever given him that said he had asked."

And then he had to put his hand on the table again, because there was one more part and it was his own.

On the Sunday before, he had had that day-book open in a public room under a slope of glass with his coat still on, and the ninth had been on the left-hand page and the eleventh on the right-hand page and the tenth empty between them, and he had looked straight at the empty day, and written the word *nothing* in his own book, and underlined it, and gone upriver to a woman with a basket. The two lines that filled it had been in the book the whole time, in a hand he had already read, on a page he had already turned, in a room with a door on it, on a Sunday morning, with nobody standing behind him.

He had not been unable to get it. He had not been told no. He had read a page of somebody else's register properly and had gone away, and a week later a woman with a pencil had done the same reading in a worse light and had got the thing he had walked past, and the difference between the two of them was not the book.

Marguerite Bray took her hand off the table and put it flat on the delivery book instead, and looked at the three grey sheets for a while, and then she said the thing that Owen had known was coming and had not known how to make smaller.

"Right," she said. "Then you tell me what a district office is."

"A machine that answers."

"It is not. It is a machine that *receives*." She said it without heat. "Look at the shape of it, boy. He asked for a schedule three times and was refused three times, and the refusal was *no supported district at the named place* — that is a sentence about a bridge and it was correct, and it was correct in exactly the same way as the sentence on the second leaf, and it was correct for the same reason. And then a man walked up to a counter with a sealed sheet in his hand. And the thing on the sealed sheet was a schedule. And a schedule is the thing he had been refused three times." She squared the three sheets. "So the counter did not refuse him and did not answer him. It received a document. And a document is a thing an office is built to receive, and once it is in the book it is answered, and every question behind it is closed, and that is how a refusal becomes a consent without anybody in the room deciding anything at all."

"Nobody in the room decided anything," said Owen. "That is what makes it the same as the scope."

"It is the same shape one level down," said Bray, "and you know it is, and you knew it a month ago, and you gave it away on purpose, and I was in the room when you did it, and I watched you do it, and I have not said one word about it in three weeks and I am saying it now." She looked up. "What were the two days worth."

That was the question Ada had asked him at his own front door in the dark in the second week of the month, and had been passed on, and had been sitting in this ward ever since, and he had said an answer to it once, and it had been a good answer, and it had been an answer about not knowing.

Owen looked at the three grey sheets on the table.

"Three refusals," he said, "and a man who asked three times, and a sheet that came back signed instead of refused, and eleven months."

Marguerite Bray looked at him for a long moment. Then she put the three notices back behind the flyleaf, and closed the ledger, and put the brass weight on it, and she did not write anything down.

---

He told the ward at eleven, in the hall, standing at the long table with Ada on one side of him and the stove behind him, and about twenty-six people in the room, and he told it in the order the Office keeps in its registers because there was no other order, and he told all of it, including the part where he had given it away.

He got about four minutes in and a man at the back said, "So the date was right," and Owen said, "The date was exactly right. The ninth is the day he asked. It was never a mistake and it was never a lie and there is nothing in it anybody could have put right in eleven months, and the only reason I ever thought there was something in it to put right is that I am a person who looks for a date that is wrong."

"And you never found out," said Ada.

"I gave up finding out on the sixth day of the month," said Owen, "in this room, on purpose, and I said out loud what I was paying and Ada asked me if I could live with it and I said yes. And I have been living with it for twenty-one days. And I want to say the other half of it, because a room that only hears one half of a thing is being done to as badly as the ward was in October." He turned round and put his hand on the table on a page of the delivery book, on nothing, on the wood. "If I had kept it, the Office would have corrected a date. That is what would have happened, in nine days, with a memorandum, and the memorandum would have said the schedule was amended to the date of receipt, and the three duties would have stayed in Mill Ward, and the thirteen names would have stayed on the second leaf, and I would have spent a year getting a date changed by two days in a month nobody will look at again. So I was right to give it up."

"Then you have got it back and you are going to put it in."

"No," said Owen.

The room waited.

"I have got it back," he said, "and I am not putting it back in, and I want the reason to be written in that book tonight, because it is the only interesting thing I have done this month and it is going to sound like cowardice. A charge withdrawn by the man who withdrew it cannot be re-entered by him. Not cannot *should* not — cannot. It is a rule about the man, and it is the only rule in this Office that exists for the protection of the person and not the document, and it is the rule that stops a clerk from re-opening a case for his own reasons in his own name in the fourth week of the month because the fourth week is when he found out he was right." He shut his hand. "And the charge that matters is scope, and scope is untouched by any of it, and I have said in a filing on a docket that the entry is validly made and the complaint is of its scope, and I am not going back upstairs to unwrite my own sentence because a woman kept a delivery book for thirty-six years."

Marguerite Bray had the pencil out by then and she put it on the table, and Owen understood what was happening, and he waited.

Mrs Bray wrote one line in the delivery book, in pencil, with the day and the hour, and then she put the pencil down and did not say anything at all, and Owen understood that she had done exactly what he had asked and not one thing more.

---

He walked down the hill at half past twelve in the rain, for the fourth or fifth time in a month with something in his head that he had got out of a room in Docketford, and he got about two hundred yards.

The thing he had learned was not about Tal Sarr.

He had spent a month — he understood that now, standing in a lane above a river, in the rain, at thirty-two years of age, with a cut on one finger and a good deal of wear in the other one — he had spent a month building a case on the proposition that the Registry of Continuant Works was careless.

Not dishonest. He had never said that and he had never believed it, and Pell had been in that building three months and had never said it either, and Ada had said the true version of it on the seventh day of the month while she was voting against a document. *Somebody didn't lie, and that is worse.* He had written that down. He had had it in a notebook for three weeks.

It was not the proposition. The Registry of Continuant Works was not careless. It was the most careful body of clerks in the delta and it had a form for everything, including a form for a person who cannot answer, and that form was sealed on a landing and the plate on the drawer was correct, and there was a requirements sheet for it and he had read it standing up at arm's length while a woman watched him and did not pretend she had not.

Carelessness was not the problem. Carelessness would have been easier. Carelessness would have been a man in a hurry in an office in November, and a man in a hurry in an office in November can be found, and can be spoken to, and can be put in front of a ward meeting in ten minutes and made to say what he did.

The problem was that the office had answered a man three times, correctly, in the correct form, about a place, and had then received a document, and the receiving had closed the question, and the closing had cost a ward a crossing for eleven months, and no person in the chain had done anything at all except answer, refuse, and receive, in that order, in the correct form, on the correct day.

Owen Rusk walked down a lane in the rain and found that he was not in love with the Registry and was not its enemy and never had been either, and that the only description of himself that survived the morning was the plainest one available: he was a man in it. Nine years at a desk. A trestle table with an assisting clerk's card on it. A docket stub in the left pocket with a number on it that he had been permitted to write down. A private book that left the building and a hand that had been cut on a press tray and had not healed.

He had spent a month proving that the Office was careful, and what he had actually done was learn that careful and right are not the same and never have been, and that a building can be run by careful people for four hundred and eighty-seven years and get exactly as wrong as a careless one, and will do it in a better hand.

He got to the bottom of the lane and turned round and looked back up it, at four streets, and then at the hill, and then at the whole of the rest of it, and did the arithmetic of what the ward actually had, because a man who has just learned something ought to be able to say what it is worth.

He had this.

District Return 487-LL, entered on the fifteenth, eight branches and a recorded dissent of four and a woman of seventy-one in a drawer on a shelf with nothing else in it. Renewed for review and not bound, on terms a ward can meet, every seven days, by a reviewing officer with a clock on the wall who told the truth about where he had got his answer. A stability order signed on the twenty-fourth by a careful man who would not sign something he meant to bury, naming the ward sixth of sixteen, and suspended with fifteen others, and public, and on a docket, appealable to the Glass Stair on application. A crossing closed to loads under a separate instrument with a real appeal window, and a repair at four thousand one hundred and sixty that nobody is funding, and an invitation on a notice at the weir path in bigger letters to any district that can do the job better. Eight pages that said what the ward does, and one page that said who said no, and a woman's own hand under a box in a form that a man in a review room had read out loud before he gave them his finding. A hand-primed pump, nine times and once again, and a granary going to three weeks at the end of the month, and a marker set at seven forty on a wet Friday by two people on the top path.

He did not have the pump grant. The grant stayed suspended and it is suspended this morning and it will be suspended in a month and it is not made whole anywhere inside the year.

He did not have six protective returns. He had a list of four names on a page in a notebook, and three of the five parts, and the two that are not anybody's to pay for are the two that are missing, and a woman of seventy-one who is in a drawer in a ward hall and a woman of twenty-two who has written her interest down against herself and is not thanked for it by anybody including him.

He did not have Cobb Rennie. He had a man who carried a child home at night because nobody else was going to, and who was not on anything, and who was the most independent person available for a document, and whose pension wants a record that places him at two rooms on Fetter Lane, and there is no such record, and there has not been for two years, and nothing filed in this case reaches him, and nothing filed in this case is going to.

He did not have the drawer. It was shut and it had a cord on it and a brass tab, and the number was in his book and the shape of the tab was in his book, and Alder Row was four streets from his own front door, and there was an intake number in the same series, and an index card in a public room under a slope of glass that said in a printed word with a space in it that it had never been opened, and he was not going to open it, and he was not going to stop.

And he had a date, in a piece of paper on a docket, in the last week of a season that nobody had fixed the end of, which could not be missed and could not be wrong.

Down on the Fetter Lane side, four doors down from Marguerite Bray's front door, Nessa Kray came out at one o'clock in the rain and put her seed trays out on the boards in the correct order, and a woman she had never spoken to said good morning to her, and she said good morning back, and neither of them said anything else, and that was the whole of the twenty-sixth day of the month.

On the ward's own book, in a hand Owen Rusk had not written and would not have permitted if he could, there were three lines for the twenty-seventh day of the month and nothing else in the column. The first said that a man had asked three times and been told no three times. The second said that a sheet had come back signed. The third was a sentence he had dictated and Marguerite Bray had set down exactly, about a charge that cannot be re-entered by the man who withdrew it, with the hour ruled under it and nothing else. And above all three, from the month before, in the same ruled hand and a different life: a folded sheet, being the entry and its two leaves, sealed, brought by T. Sarr of this ward, in person, at the door. Between that line and these three there was nothing at all for eleven months.

He did not write anything under them. He had wanted to since the twentieth, when he had read a day-book in a public room and written *nothing* in his own book beside a day that was empty and gone upriver to a woman with a basket. On the twenty-seventh day he understood that the ward's book was not the place for it, and that the reason was not procedure, and that the reason was that Marguerite Bray had ruled a delivery book for thirty-six years precisely so that a ward would have one thing in this city that nobody had to explain to anybody.

He put his notebook away at the bottom of the hill and went in to his dinner, and the crossing was still closed, and the pump still ran by hand on the worst nights, and eight pages said what the ward did and one page said who said no, and nobody in Docketford knew yet what the Registry had done to a man's mother in a flood-year quarter, in the same batch, in a different register, and the brass tab in the tray on the ground floor was still shut.
