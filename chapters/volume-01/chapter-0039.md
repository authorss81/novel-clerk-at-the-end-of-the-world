# Chapter 39 — The Ward Files Its Own Case

The bin went on the Monday morning of the forty-ninth day of the month, which was not a thing anybody had planned, and it took about nine minutes to deal with and the whole of the ward came to look at it, because in a ward of two hundred and forty people a bin going is an event and because everybody in the room had been eating out of it in some sense for a month.

It was at the back, on the floor, against the wall under the low window, and it was meal, and it had gone damp in the corner where the wall sweats, and it was not spoiled all the way through; it was spoiled in about a sixth of itself, and the rest of it was good, and Nessa Kray said that from the door without coming in, and got it right, and the granary committee stood round it for a while and then did the thing which is the only thing anybody in Lower Lock knows how to do with a bad bin, which is to write it down.

Marguerite Bray wrote it in the pump book on the page where the figure lives, in pencil, in her own hand, with the day, and then she ruled the figure again underneath it.

It came out at **just over two weeks**.

Two weeks and a bit had been the figure on the Thursday, and the difference between that and what was on the board on the Monday was a sixth of a bin and about three days, and nobody said anything about that at all, and Owen Rusk stood at the end of the long table with a folder of nothing under his arm and understood that this was the second time in four days that a number in a pump book had fallen by an amount that nobody would have called a fall.

"Nobody has asked me this," said Marguerite Bray, to the room, not to him, "and I am writing it down because if nobody asks me I will forget it in a month and then it will be a week and a bit, and I have been keeping this book for thirty-six years and the only thing I have ever been asked for by anybody in that building is a thing to be got out of it."

---

They worked on the three documents on the Monday and the Tuesday, and the working was not writing. That is the part of it that nobody in Docketford has any way of understanding: four hours a day for two days, twenty-odd people in a hall, and not one of them at a desk.

The three were: the **schedule of performed acts**, which is what the Council asked for; the **ward's statement in the petition**, which is what the hearing is about; and the ward's **answer to the three questions the Council asked**, which is a document nobody has ever had to write and which the ward could not have written a month ago.

And on the Monday morning, at about eleven, before anybody had done anything, Ada Rusk stood at the end of the long table and stopped the room, and said the thing that the whole of the sixth week of the rain season turns on, and she said it without raising her voice, and about twenty-two people were in the hall and heard all of it.

"Listen to me for a minute, because we are about to write three pieces of paper and I want the reason we are writing them written down first, and I want it written down by me, and not afterwards by Mr Rusk in a room in Docketford where nobody can be asked about it.

"We have got a good week. I am not going to pretend otherwise and I am not going to be modest about it either, because a modest bridge mechanic is a thing I have never been and it is not going to start now. We performed on a calendar in front of a stranger and the act is in the return and the return went up on the Friday and it was renewed for the fourth time in six weeks and it is due again on that Friday. There is a woman from a landing upriver who read our hours out loud and wrote in her own hand that the wage is not funded. There is a sheet on a table in a hall in Docketford with a hundred and nine lines on it and a queue in front of it. There is a Council paper that asks us for our own sheet. And on Friday a man with a clock read out that a claim to a status is not a claim to a duty, in a room with windows, and did not pretend he had worked it out himself.

"That is six weeks. It is more than anybody in this ward has had since I was a girl.

"And I am telling you the whole of it now because I can see the shape of the next two days and I have seen this shape before, in August, in this room, and it starts with somebody saying *shall we put the crossing in it*." She put her hand flat on the table. "The crossing is the only thing anybody in Docketford will read. If we put the crossing in these papers then these papers are a claim about a bridge, and they will be read as a claim about a bridge, and in a month the Office will say that a district performing in public is a district claiming the status of a work, and it will be correct, and there is a sentence in a reviewing officer's own file that will let them say it in four words.

"And the roster stops being the best document this ward has ever made. That is what I want said. The roster is the only paper any of us has ever produced that nobody can call an error, because every line in it is an hour somebody was there, and a roster is not a claim, a roster is a *fact*, and a fact can be read by anybody and cannot be argued with. The moment we put the crossing in, it becomes a claim. And a claim is what the transfer was. It is what Tal Sarr walked two miles upriver four times with. It is a thing one person signs for a place."

She looked round the room, and every single person in it was looking at her, and not one of them looked at Owen Rusk, and he noticed that and understood that it had been arranged without anybody arranging it.

"Mr Rusk asked me on Friday night what I wanted to do about it, and I told him I would tell him this morning, and I am telling him in front of the room and not to him, and that is the difference between a person who is asked and a person who is answered.

"So: hours, and who was there, and the days we did it, and the people who watched. **Not the crossing.** We claim no status. We disclaim safety, condition and use, in the same sentence as always, and I want that sentence in the schedule as well as in the return, and I want it in our answer to the Council, and I want it at the bottom of every page in the bundle so that a person cannot turn over a page without reading it."

"And the day of the hearing?" said somebody.

"Goes in," said Ada Rusk. "The day goes in, the room goes in, the hour goes in. What we say in that room is a duty. What we do not say in that room is anything about that bridge, and if somebody stands up in that room and asks me about the crack I am going to say *that question is not before you* and mean it."

---

The honest answer to the Council's second question was Ada Rusk's and she gave it out loud before she wrote it down, which is the thing she does.

They asked for the ground of the second examination in this district, and the ground is at item 14 of a schedule in a book in the district office, with recitals, four hundred and forty-three years old, amended never, and the recitals are *right* and everybody who has read them has said so and Ada Rusk said so in a hall on the Wednesday night with thirty people in it.

And then she said the other half.

"The district cannot be examined by anybody who lives in it."

She said it standing at the end of the table and then she wrote it in, because she had told the room on the Wednesday that the frightening part was the one that got written down, and she was not going to hand a ward a sentence in her own hand that she had not read out first.

"Two hundred and forty people, and there is no person in this ward on a district-exclusion roll in this county, and there is not going to be one while the two observers we carry are a Registry officer on the second floor of a building in the city and a counter clerk in the district that took our crossing off us. That is a fact about where we are. It is not about anybody's conduct and it is not a complaint and it does not belong in a document to the Council, and I am putting it in because the Council asked us a question about examinations and the true answer is that we have never had one, and if I leave that out then somebody in Concord Hall will write *the district has examined itself* on a piece of paper in a year and it will be true on its face and false underneath.

"And the district is glad of its observers. Both of them have written the worst true thing about themselves in their own ink, in their own hands, more than once, and one of them did it against herself before anybody asked her. We are glad. We would also like it recorded that glad is not the same as enough, and we are not going to say the second half in a way that sounds like we are asking for something, because we are not, and I will not have a piece of paper out of this hall that a clerk can use to make us sound ungrateful."

Marguerite Bray wrote it in the delivery book and read it back and made her put the word *ungrateful* in a different pencil because Ada had said it in a different voice.

And the ward's answer to the Council's third question was the thing that made about nine people in the hall laugh on the Tuesday morning, in a room with the door shut, at about eleven, which was not a thing Owen had expected to hear in six weeks.

"The Council asks the disposition forms used by the district office in the quarter, and the number of times each was used," Ada Rusk said. "We have got three."

"Three," said somebody.

"We have got three and we have kept them, and Mrs Bray has got two of them in a drawer behind a flyleaf where she has kept everything anybody has ever sent her for thirty-six years, and one of them is nailed to a post at the end of a path in letters big enough to read at forty feet, and there are two more in the same drawer and neither of the two is a disposition." She held up a sheet. "Three refusals in the same form from a county sub-table, one load notice under an engineer's finding, one rate notice that came back through the post in ten days. Five documents, in six weeks, in a ward of two hundred and forty people, and every one of them is a thing somebody was told, and not one of them says why."

She put it down on the table.

"And here is the line the district is going to send with them: *The district holds three. A district office in this county has held a hundred and nine in one quarter. The district does not know the reasons for the five it has seen. The district has asked seven questions in its own hall, one at a time, of persons who may be affected, and has not been answered, and records the office's position that a sub-table's refusals are not refusals of that office, and does not accept that position, and does not ask the Council to overrule it.*"

"Why don't we ask the Council to overrule it?" said Bram Tillet, from the wall, where he had been sitting for two days doing nothing and saying four sentences.

"Because we would be asking a thing in Concord Hall to tell a county sub-table what its own register means," said Ada Rusk, "and the day we do that is the day this ward stops being a ward and becomes a complaint, and I have read what we are writing. It is three pages. It is about hours."

---

Marguerite Bray asked the first of her seven on the Tuesday morning at ten past nine, and she asked it in the hall, in the way she had ruled in the delivery book on the thirty-seventh of the month, which is: one at a time, in this room, to anybody who chooses to come, asked by her.

She had the nine lines on a sheet and she had a list of seven numbers and she read the first one out.

"*Household 4, a court off Bell Row, intake year, the second of the nine.*"

A woman of about fifty-two at the back said, "That's mine."

"Is it."

"That's mine. We were at the two rooms at the top of Bell Row and we have been at the bottom since the flood and I have never been on a roll since I was nineteen."

"What did you go and ask them for?"

The woman looked at the floor for a second.

"I asked them whether a person could be put back on a roll if a whole street asked them," she said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. Two of us went, because the girl said if two of us went it was a street. And the woman at the counter said no district office competent and gave us a piece of paper back, and we did not go again, and I have never told anybody about it, and I have had nineteen years."

Nobody said anything. The stove ticked.

"Thank you," said Marguerite Bray, and wrote it in the delivery book with the day and the hour under it, and read back what she had written, which was the number and not the answer, because an answer is not a fact about a ward and a number is, and she has known that since she was twenty-five.

Then she said: "That is the first. There are six and I will ask them the same way and the same room, and anybody who wants to be here for one of them is welcome and anybody who does not is not obliged to be, and I am not going to chase anybody, and I would be obliged if nobody repeats what has been said in this room this morning to anybody in that building, and I am not able to prevent it and I am asking."

Twenty-six people said nothing, which Owen Rusk understood, standing at the end of the table, to be the whole of the answer.

---

They filed it on the Tuesday afternoon, and the filing was a physical act in a hall and it took about nine minutes and there was no Registry in it at all.

It is the ward's own roster, which is a sheet with a heading and columns under the heading, with a covering page added at the front. The covering page has four things on it: the name of the work, the interval, the two observers' names in their own hands, and a withdrawal right in Ada Rusk's capitals that says a person may take their own branch out of this schedule and the rest of it does not fall over, and that the schedule is performed whether the return does or not.

The middle has the hours, bin by bin, act by act, day by day, four weeks of them, and the fourth week is the week a stranger read it.

The back has four blank pages with the names of Ada Rusk, Bram Tillet, Holm Ferris and Wenna Sarr at the top of them in Marguerite Bray's hand and nothing else on any of them, and Ada Rusk, who had been asked about it twice, said: "Leave them blank. If anybody wants to write on them they can write on them and I will rule whatever they write, in the book, in this room, whatever it is. And if nobody writes on them then they are four blank pages in a document we sent to a Council, and that is also correct, and I want that on the record and I want it in the schedule, because a blank page that is sent on purpose is not a hole. It is a page."

The hearing statement was four lines long and three of them were Ada Rusk's, and the fourth was the day and the room and the hour, and Owen Rusk's paragraph with the hundred and eighty-one and the twenty-two in it was not in it anywhere, and Marguerite Bray had ruled both sentences in the delivery book the same evening in the same page, so that the ward had the number and the argument was the ward's own.

"Owen Rusk," said Ada Rusk, at about four o'clock on the Tuesday afternoon, with the bundle tied. "You are the covering. You are going to take it up the stair on Wednesday morning and you are going to sign for it and you are not going to write a word of it. Now read the whole thing back to this room from the copy, and put your hand on the page, and I am going to stop you, and you are going to let me, and you are not going to look at the clock."

He read it back.

He got about four lines in and Marguerite Bray stopped him, because he had said *for seven days*, and the term of the covering page is not seven days, it is *for as long as the return is performed*, which is a different clock and the ward chose it deliberately on the thirty-first of the month.

"That is not what it says," she said. "It says *for as long as the return is performed*, and the return is renewed every seven days and is not the same thing as seven days, and I want everybody in this room to have heard a Registry clerk get that wrong out loud in a hall."

"I got it wrong."

"You got it wrong the way you got the stranger's sentence wrong the other week, and I have stopped you twice now for the same fault and I am going to stop you twice more before this year is out." She let him go on. "From the top of that page again."

He got to the second page about a minute later and Marguerite Bray stopped him again, and this one was worse, because he had read *any person not of the district and not of this office may read this schedule* as *any person may read this schedule*, and the difference between those two is the whole of the third term Ada Rusk negotiated in a hall on the thirty-first of the month, and if it goes out wrong then a person who lives in this ward and is not in this schedule would have the right to read it, and one of those people is a woman who sold seed for forty-one years and is the reason three streets of this ward can still say their own names.

"Again," said Ada Rusk.

"Third time," said Owen Rusk, and read it correctly, and got to the end, and read the four blank pages out as four blank pages, which was the last thing and which took about four seconds and which nobody in the room said anything about at all, and which was the only part of the whole afternoon that Owen Rusk understood to be the point of the entire six weeks.

---

He carried it up the stair on the Wednesday morning, and before he slept on the Tuesday night he sat at his own table with the plate on it and the lamp on and worked out the thing he felt, and then he worked out that it was the wrong thing to feel about a ward's day, and then he worked out that it was the correct thing to feel about his own, and he did not write it in the register, because there is a difference between a fact about a document and a fact about a man and he has been caught once.

The return had been renewed on the Friday for the fourth time in six weeks and it was due again on that Friday. The roll entry of the fourteenth stood and had not been amended. The load restriction of the tenth was in force and untouched. The grant of the Fetter Lane pump was suspended and would be suspended in a month. The repair was four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and unfunded. The crew's wage was four months in arrears and was written on a page of a ward's delivery book in two hands as a condition of agreeing to be read in public, and it was not funded anywhere in this city that anybody had found.

The pump grant was not restored. The crossing was not open. The sixteen were still sixteen.

And on the Tuesday afternoon, in a hall in Lower Lock, in the ward's own book, in the ward's own words, a district had handed a document to the capital.

And the thing Owen Rusk felt about it, at his own table, at about half past ten at night on the fiftieth day of the month, was relief, and it was the wrong thing to feel about a ward's day, and it was the correct thing to feel about his own, and it was relief because for six weeks the whole of what he had been able to do was carry, and on the Tuesday afternoon in a hall in Lower Lock he had been handed a bundle and told he was the covering, and there was not one word in it in his hand.
