# Chapter 1 — The Fourth River Desk

The batch came up from the seal stair at a quarter past three, and Owen knew before he had unbuckled his case that it was short.

Thirty-one sheets should have been thirty-one. The Fourth Desk produced the Docketford weekly continuance in the order the roll took it: by ward, then by household, then by name, and the whole thing a single afternoon if nobody argued and nobody had to be fetched. He had run ten weeks clean. He knew the weight of a full batch in his forearm the way a cooper knows a stave, and this one was light by about the weight of a folded sheet and a half.

He set the case on the desk rail, took out the three dull pencils he worked with, and did not take out the sharp one. Then he counted, because counting is cheaper than guessing.

It was the twenty-second sheet, Lower Lock, that was wrong.

He read it twice before he understood what kind of wrong it was, and understanding took the shape of a small cold place behind his sternum that he had been there exactly twice before, both times in flood years, both times about three hours before somebody drowned.

Lower Lock was not late. Nothing in the sheet said late. In its place was a transfer entry, and a transfer entry is not a hole in a roll. It is a thing the Office did, with a number, a receiving office, and a witness or two, and it takes the duty out of one return and sets it down inside another where a different set of people can be told about it later.

*Maintenance duties of the public crossing at the Ninth Weir, the Fetter Lane pump, and the ward granary, transferred to the Mill Ward district return, administered at the Mill Ward annex.*

Three acts. The ward maintained four things and he knew all four without looking, because he had grown up thirty yards from the granary door: the crossing, the pump, the granary, and Kray's seed stall. Three of the four had just been picked up and moved downriver to a room with a counter and a queue.

Below that, the receipt block. Signed. Witnessed by two Registry officers whose names were written in the small square hand the Office used when the witness was an officer and not a private party. Sealed. And the receiving line, which is the part that matters, because a receipt is not a promise and not a proposal but a thing that has already happened somewhere else and has been delivered here:

*Received at the Mill Ward annex counter, and entered at the receiving register, on the date the stamp bears.*

The stamp was on the sheet. He turned it to the window light without standing up, because standing up for a stamp is a habit of junior clerks, and the stamp was genuine, and it was a real date in the real autumn of last year, eleven months back, when the Office had received this.

So it was not late. It had arrived.

He went back to the start of the sheet and did the work in the order the rules required, which is the order in which a thing stops being arguable. Subject first: the crossing, the pump, the granary, all named, all specific, no broad language anywhere, because broad subjects get sent back and a sent-back sheet is a sheet you do not get paid for twice. Then the act. Then the witness. Then the scope. Then the term.

The subject was clean. The act was a receipt, performed at a counter, by somebody, on a day. The witnesses were two officers of this Office standing in a room in this building, which meant the act was real and the two of them had seen it, and their interests in it were the interests of a hierarchy, which is thin but is not nothing.

The scope was where his eyes kept going back to, and not because it was wrong. It was narrow. It named three duties and a receiving return. It did not say the ward had ceased. It did not say the ward's people had moved. It said the duties went.

He had two working days' worth of a habit sitting in him, and the habit said: find the error. Find the slip. Somebody has mis-keyed a ward number or a receiving office and the whole sheet is a compositor's error and in an hour it will be a memorandum and by Friday it will be nothing. He went looking for the slip with real appetite, because the alternative was that this was exactly what it appeared to be.

He found it on the leaf stapled behind the entry.

The annex had two leaves. He dealt with the first because his batch turned on dates and the first leaf was the one with the dates on it, and the second leaf was somebody else's problem, and it was not his job to know what was on a leaf he did not need. He would find out later, at dawn, with rain on it.

The first leaf was dated, as dated leaves are, and it carried the effective dates of the three acts the transfer moved. Crossing effective the ninth. Pump effective the tenth. Granary effective the eleventh, in the ordinary way, one duty after another, in the order a receiving office would take them up.

And the entry itself had been received on the eleventh.

He looked at the ninth for a while.

There was a plain arithmetic to it and he did it twice, because it was the kind of arithmetic that undoes a man's whole afternoon. A duty cannot be received before it is received. That is not a Registry rule, it is what a receipt is. The Mill Ward annex stamped this entry on the eleventh of the month, eleven months ago. The annex schedule says the crossing moved on the ninth. Two days before the counter where it was handed in existed as a completed fact.

He reached for the receiving register, which sat in a pigeonhole behind the desk rail and which he had personally keyed for ten weeks, and he turned to the annex's number and put his finger on the line. The stamp. The entry. Both on the eleventh. Both in the same hand as four hundred other entries, unremarkable, initialled at the bottom by whoever had the counter that week.

He had the two documents side by side without deciding to. The schedule on the left saying the ninth. The register on the right saying the eleventh. The page between them doing something he had no vocabulary for, and the something was very small, and it was printed in the ordinary ink of the Office, in the ordinary margin where nobody looked.

> **MARGIN NOTICE**
> Subject: transfer entry, Mill Ward district return.
> Stated effective date: earlier than the act performed.
> The notice reports no further matter.

He read it twice. He checked it against what he could see with his own eyes, because he did not trust a thing he could not corroborate, and the corroboration was on the desk in two places: the ninth and the eleventh. Yes. The notice was correct. It was also almost nothing. It did not say who had written the ninth. It did not say whether it was a mistake or a decision or a slip of a pen in a hurry in a room where nobody had time. It did not touch the second leaf, which he had not read, and it made no claim at all about the scope, which was the part that had moved three duties out of a ward of two hundred and forty people and would not move them back because two days of arithmetic had come loose.

He read it a third time anyway, because a man who has been handed a small true thing and no use for it will go back to it, looking for the door out.

There wasn't one.

The batch wanted his mark at the foot of it. That was the whole of what was asked of him this afternoon: his own hand in the box that says the Fourth Desk's copy agrees with what the Office holds, which is not the same as saying the thing is right, and which every clerk in the hall understood to be the same, and which is why the mark is cheap and the copying is the work. He had put that mark under nine hundred batches without reading one, and he would have put it under this one too, and the sheet would have gone up the stair to the seal press before the lamps were lit, and ten weeks clean would have held.

He took the dull pencil. He put it on the entry, on the receiving line, in the space the form leaves for an officer who is not satisfied, and he wrote the six words the form allows and no others.

*Referred for local confirmation.*

Then he left the box at the foot empty.

The rule was one refusal per batch and he had spent it. The other thirty sheets could still go up if the Fourth Desk's copy went up without the Lower Lock sheet, with a memorandum saying which sheet and why; and a memorandum is slower than a batch and slower still is the last week of a quarterly run, which is the thing a man on the Fourth Desk is actually paid in.

So he wrote the memorandum. It took him longer than the referral mark had, because a referral is six words and a memorandum has to survive being read by somebody who does not care, and when he had finished it he had also written, at the top of his private page, a line he did not have to write and which he did not describe, because a man who tells you what he felt about the worst afternoon of his working life has also told you that it was the worst.

He wrote his own list under the batch, because he made lists when he was frightened and he was not going to pretend otherwise:

*— crossing dates: 9th / receipt 11th. Two days. Copy both.*
*— three duties moved, seed stall not among them. Ask why? No. Note it.*
*— two Registry witnesses. Office officers. No party from the ward.*
*— second leaf, unread. Read it.*
*— annex schedule vs stamp: who wrote the 9th.*

Then he did the thing he had told himself he would do before he left, which was to prove the backdating properly, in ink, in a morning's work, so that it could not be argued with.

The request register was the nearest thing the Office has to a confession. Any person may put a request into a counter anywhere in Docketford, and the counter must write down that it came in and what was done with it, and the register is kept by date and never thrown out, because the Office's defence against its own past is that it wrote it all down. He had never needed it. He went down to the ground-floor counter and asked for that fortnight of the autumn, and the clerk on duty handed him the volume without a question, which is how a Registry treats a clerk who asks correctly.

Ten days. Three requests in the register.

Three. In two days, in a ward of thirteen households, three separate people had walked into a counter somewhere in Docketford and asked the Office for something, and been refused. The refusals were one line each, in the standard form, initialled at the foot.

*Refused. No supported district at the named place.*

That was the first. The second, the same form, the same initials. The third, identical. Three refusals inside the two days the annex schedule was already pretending were over.

He sat with that longer than he sat with anything else in the whole afternoon, and what he understood out of it was only this: whoever wrote the ninth had written a date on which the ward was already moved, and in those two days three people had gone to a counter and been told there was no supported district at the place they were asking about. Which meant somebody was asking. The Office had not invented the ward's absence and then had to go looking for it. People had come in on their own feet, before the sheet said they should have needed to, and had been turned away in the correct form.

He could not learn what the three requests concerned. The register carried the refusal, the date, the counter, and the initials, and the initials were a single letter and a stroke in a different hand each time, and the letter belonged to an office and the stroke belonged to whoever was sitting at that counter, and the Office did not keep a list of who had sat at which counter on a given morning a year ago. He asked the ground-floor clerk whether the counter rotation was kept. The clerk said no, and then said, with the particular cheerfulness of a man who has decided a question is not his, that requests like that were usually about a place, a bridge, a ferry, water, a name.

"Usually," Owen said.

"Places," said the clerk, and put the register back.

Owen wrote all three refusals out in his own book. Not the Office's book. The Office's book was the Office's, and what was in it was what the Office had decided to keep, and he had spent ten weeks learning that a clean run is a thing you maintain by not asking questions in a building where the questions are logged. So he bought a pocket notebook at the ironmonger on Fetter Lane in the spring, cheap, sewn binding, and he began in it that afternoon with a heading he disliked and could not improve on.

*River Desk — private.*

He wrote the transfer's number. He wrote the date the Office received it and the date the annex schedule said it took effect, and underneath, the two-day gap, and beside that the three refusals with their dates and their initials, and beside that a note in his own hand: *these are the same two days. I do not yet know why that matters and I am not going to pretend it does not.*

He looked at the last two dull pencils on the desk rail, and at the case with the sharpened one lying in it where it had lain all afternoon, and he thought, in the flat voice he used on himself when he was being accurate, that there was no version of the rest of this afternoon in which he went up the stair and sealed a sheet that said the ward's crossing had moved on a day two before anyone could have handed it over.

The lamps were lit along the hall by then, one at a time, down the numbered desks, and the stair to the seal press had a queue on it of clerks who had got their batches in on time.

Owen copied the rest of the batch. It took him an hour and a half and it was the best work he did that week, and at the end of it he squared the sheets, tied them, wrote the cover, and did not take them up the stair.

He had a ward to walk to.

It was half past six and the rain had started, the second week of the season, the kind of rain that does not fall so much as stand in the air, and Lower Lock was a twenty-minute walk upriver and the rest of his life.
