# Chapter 55 — The Boat and the Tent

They came up the river on the Thursday morning of the eightieth day of the month in a flat-bottomed survey boat with a mast, a tarpaulin over the afterhold, eleven people, and a wooden case lashed under the wheelhouse that Owen Rusk could see from the bank at Fetter Bend because he was on the bank at Fetter Bend at about a quarter past seven in the morning on a Thursday for no reason that he was ever going to be able to give anybody.

He had not gone down to the water to meet anybody. He had gone down to the water because the pump does not take on Mondays and had not taken on Tuesday either, and Ada Rusk had been priming at four and had asked him to be on the standpipe at seven with a bucket, which is a thing a man in his ward may be asked to do and is the only office anybody in Lower Lock has ever needed him to fill.

They came up at about half past seven. The mast was a sawn tree and the tarpaulin was new and there were two **flags** at the bow, and the upper one was a blue field with a white device on it that Owen Rusk had seen twice in his life, on a form and on a wall in the Glass Stair, and had never seen on a river.

The Census Guard was constituted in the Concord Year 351, has been reconstituted twice, and answers to the Provisional Mandate and to nobody in this county. The device is a **stacked stone**: three bars, and on them a flat oval, which is the whole of the symbolism and is a thing people in this county find faintly insulting and never say so.

He watched the boat come up and thought, standing on a wet bank at a quarter past seven in the morning, that he had been in this city thirty-two years and had never once seen the flags on the water.

---

The tent was up and the board was up and the ledger was on the trestle table by eight o'clock, and the man of sixty from Wednesday had a slate and a lamp and the wooden case open on the table, and the stone was out.

The stone is the plainest object Owen Rusk had ever handled anything like, and he did not handle it. It is a hand's breadth across, flat on one face, rounded on the other, worn grey on the flat, and it has been pressed on more instruments in this county than there are houses in Docketford, and it belongs to nobody, and the person who uses it does not own it and cannot keep it, and it goes back in the case at the end of every settlement and comes out at the beginning of the next one.

"Nobody has a name on the board," said Owen Rusk, "and I asked the woman with the slate yesterday and she said it was for the settlements and not for the staff, and she was right, and I want to say that I think she was right."

"You may think it," said a voice behind him. "It is also the rule, and the rule is item six, and the rule is right for a better reason than the one she gave you."

Owen Rusk turned round.

She was about the height of Ada Rusk and a little older, in a field coat with the sleeves turned back about four inches, and she had a ledger under her arm and a slate and a pencil, and there was river mud on the side of one boot about four inches high, which told Owen Rusk more about her morning than the whole of the first page of her file would have.

"I am not going to be a while about this," she said, "and I am not going to do it standing up in a field at a quarter past eight in the morning, because you have a bucket and a standpipe and a pump that does not take and I can see it does not take from here, and there is a woman of twenty-four at your elbow who is going to tell me within a minute that she primed it on Tuesday and it took on the second stroke. So I am going to say who I am, because you have not asked and you are going to, and then I am going to be in this field all day and you can ask me anything you like at any hour of it, and I will not remember it tomorrow and neither will you, and that is the arrangement I work under and I have worked under it in eleven districts."

"Warden Oress Kade," said Owen Rusk.

"Yes."

"I know the name. There is a file about your office the length of a coat on a shelf behind me and I have not read it."

"That is the correct position for a man in your office to be in and I would think less of you if you had read it," said Warden Kade, "and I will tell you what is in the first paragraph, which is that this service was reconstituted in the Concord Year 402 and that a person who cannot answer for their own premises is not to be counted and is not to be left to be counted by somebody else, and that the second half of that is the part people forget, and it is mine, and I did not write it and I would not have written it as generously."

She put the ledger on the table and opened it and turned it round so that it was the right way up for him, which is a thing that people who have been an officer of something for a long time do and people who have not, do not.

"You have a question," she said. "Everybody who comes up this bank has one and they have had it since the tent went up, and it is the same one, and I would rather you asked it in the first ten minutes than the fourth day. Ask it."

"The first one is who decided," said Owen Rusk.

"The first one is not who decided," said Warden Kade. "The first one is *is the method lawful*, and the answer is yes, and I will give you the whole of it in four sentences and then you can ask me the real one."

"Go on."

"A person in a settlement that cannot be reconciled has a status that cannot be relied on, and this city has a law about how long a status can be relied on, and the law is **fourteen days** for a person in severe unfiling, and after fourteen days a supported restoration is no longer available, and after **thirty** it is impossible, and there is no office in this county that can extend either figure by a day." She did not say this as a recital. She said it the way a doctor says a dosage. "At the end of the fourteen, a **count correction** is made by the Provincial Counting Office and it is attested by me. That is the whole of the service. There is no second instrument. There is no discretion. There is a form, a schedule of premises attached to the form, a date, and a reason, and the reason is a figure, because a figure is the only thing that can be written in a column that has to mean the same thing in Concord Hall as it means in a field behind the tanneries."

"And what is the correction for."

"The draw," said Warden Kade. "The water a settlement would have taken goes back into the district's main. And with it goes its share of the maintenance fund, because the fund is a share of the count. And with that goes the right of the people living in it to be witnessed at an address on a district act, which is the thing nobody outside this service ever puts on a table, and which is the reason I have spent nineteen years in fields instead of a building, and which I would give up tomorrow for an office if the office would put it on a table."

"And the fourteen days," said Owen Rusk. "Are they ever enough."

"No," said Warden Kade.

---

The silence after that lasted about two seconds and it was the only silence of the morning.

"Fourteen days is not enough," she said. "Fourteen days is what the law allows and I work inside the law and I have never once in nineteen seasons gone outside it and I have been asked to and I have said no twice, and both times I was right and both times somebody told me afterwards that I had been slow. Fourteen days is not enough to find a house for a family that has lost nine of its premises to a bend that moved. It is not enough to get a child into a school. It is enough — and I say this without any pleasure at all — to keep a settlement's premises from being counted in a way that takes the count out of a neighbouring district, and that is what it is for, and that is nearly all it is for, and I have known that since my second season and I have not found a way to make the form say it.

"That is the answer. That is what this service is. It is a thing that stops a bad count becoming a worse city, at the price of some people being counted out of it, and I do the arithmetic eleven times this season and I do it honestly, and I have never once had to lie about it, and I have never once been able to explain it to a person in a field either."

"Then let me ask the real one," said Owen Rusk, and his voice was not steady and he did not try to make it. "Who wrote the schedule."

"The District Counting Office wrote the schedule," said Warden Kade, "on the returns received for the Count of the Concord Year 487 and on the notices returned in that season, and the schedule was printed for notice on the twenty-eighth day of the month before last, and I received it on a river post with a quota of eleven on the same sheet, and the quota and the schedule are both dated before I knew the name of one of the eleven and I do not know the name of two of them now."

"You did not make it."

"I did not make it and I cannot unmake it and I have tried," said Warden Kade. "I have written four letters. Three went to Concord Hall and were answered in eleven weeks, correctly, and the answer to each of them was that a schedule of settlements to be reconciled is made by the office that holds the returns, and that the Warden reconciles the settlements named in it, and that a Warden who is not satisfied with a schedule may return it and may not amend it. I returned it in the second week. It came back with the same eleven on it and a date four days later, and nothing else, and I have not written a fifth letter and I will not, because the fifth letter would be the same letter and I have a season to run."

"Then who at the office — "

"I do not know," said Warden Kade, "and that is not modesty, and I will tell you exactly what I mean by that, because I have found that people hear *I do not know* and write *she is careful* in their own books, and both of those are wrong. I mean the column wants a number. I could write a schedule tomorrow. I have sat in a tent and drawn up a list of settlements that I would reconcile first, and I have done it in my head on the water coming up, and if I had drawn it up on paper I would have had eleven names and two columns, and the second column would be a number, and **I could not tell you where my number would come from.** Every figure I have ever reconciled has come out of a return somebody else filled in. That is the service. If I had to make the column I would be inventing it, and I would not know that I was inventing it, and that is the part of this work that I think nobody in Concord Hall has ever once considered."

She shut the ledger.

"Your real question is not the schedule," she said. "Your real question is whether a man who certified a crossing he lives behind is the right person to be the officer of the record of this district's returns. I have had that file since the spring. I read it in March. I have read the transcript of the hearing and the account of the narrowing and the amendment of your mother's record and the four pages and the twenty-one."

"And you have not put us on your list."

"There is nothing on my list to put you on," said Warden Kade. "My list is settlements. My quota is eleven. Docketford district is a district and not a settlement, and the district of Docketford has a return, and the return of the district of Docketford is **good** — I have read it, it is signed, it is current, and there are three dozen districts in this county and yours is the fourth best I have ever seen, and I say that as a person who reconciles the other sixty. You are on a board behind the tanneries because the board is a board, and a district is a settlement like anything else on it, and I have not read anything into it and I would advise you not to either, and now I am going to tell you the thing that will make you not able to put this down at night, and then I am going to go and do a settlement at Wexford Landing because a woman there is seventy-nine and has not been asked anything since the year before last."

---

"Your list is eleven settlements and the quota is eleven," said Owen Rusk, "and eleven settlements reconciled in eleven days is one settlement a day, and the season opens on the first of the month."

"Yes."

"Which means that every one of the eleven has to be reconciled into an answer, or a correction, or a review, inside the season, or the quota is not met and the district is reported short."

"Yes. The district is reported short. The district was reported short in the Concord Year 484 and again in 486, and a district reported short is a district whose count is qualified, and a qualified count goes up to the province and a qualified province is a thing that happens, and I have never once been in a district that was reported short and been told what it meant, and I have stopped asking." She looked at him. "You have worked that out in about a minute. It took me four seasons and I did it standing in a wet field in a different county. I am not going to congratulate you, because it does not help."

"Then the first one is not a choice," he said. "The first one is the one you have to do."

"The first one is Nether Sill," said Warden Kade, "and I have not been there and I have not sent anybody, and I do not go to a settlement first. I go second. There is a reason for that and the reason is that a warden who goes first is a warden who is told what the settlement wants to hear, and I have been told what a settlement wanted to hear twice and both times it cost somebody a district that did not know it had been represented."

"Second."

"I go second, to a settlement that has already been asked its questions by somebody who is not me, and I do the reconciliation myself, in the room, with the stone, and I write the figure in the column myself, in my own hand, and I am the only person in the service who writes the figure and the only person in the service who is not allowed to write anything else."

She turned to go, and then stopped, and came about two paces back, and this was the only time in the whole of that day that she was not three moves ahead of him.

"If you find out who wrote the schedule," said Warden Kade, "write to me and not to the Council, and I will tell you why. I will do nothing with it. I will not suspend anything and I will not refer it and I will not put it in front of a room. I have a season and eleven settlements and a quota and if I start a paper fight in the ninth month I will be somewhere else in the middle of the tenth, and the eleven will not be reconciled, and the eleven are the whole of my job." She looked at him levelly. "And I will not pretend to. That is the second half of writing to me and it is the half that matters. Every person who has ever come to me about a schedule has wanted me to pretend to, and I am telling you now, on a Thursday morning, in a field, before I have met a single one of the eleven, that I will not. If I find out who wrote it I will read the finding to you and I will go and do a settlement at Wexford Landing, and that is the entire extent of what I am able to do about it, and you may put that in whatever book you keep, which I know you keep, because every person in your office keeps one and none of them puts it on a table."

And she walked off toward the west gate, and the man of sixty was waiting at the trestle table with the slate, and the woman of about thirty had four figures on her slate and was not looking at either of them.

---

He did not get a chance to say anything else, because the man of sixty said *two settlements this morning* and it turned out to be true, and because at about eleven o'clock a woman came out of the lane behind the tanneries with a **fire pump on a cart**, and this turned out to be the first thing that happened all season that Owen Rusk did not understand immediately.

The woman was about sixty and the cart was a good cart and the pump was a good pump and there were four men with it. She came into the field and stopped about ten feet off the board and read the schedule the way you read a price list, and then she said, to the woman with the slate:

"Is Ossery on that."

"It is. Ninth row. Eleven premises of record and the year before last."

"Then tell the Warden I have brought the pump."

The woman with the slate wrote something on the slate and went into the tent, and came out in about ninety seconds, and Warden Kade came out behind her, and looked at the cart, and then at the four men, and then at the man of about sixty.

"Inspected this morning," she said to him.

"Inspected this morning, and there is a sheet on it with a seal on it, and the seal is the district works seal and the district works seal is a work of record, and a fire pump that was inspected this morning and put on a cart and brought to a field is a work of record that was maintained this morning." She had a slate out. "What is your name."

"Maud Tray."

"Maud Tray, I am going to say a thing and you are not going to understand why I am saying it, and then I am going to give you back a count. You have drawn water at Ossery off a fire pump since the spring because your district's draw was removed from premises that went off the schedule, and you have drawn it on your own hook, and your own hook is not an instrument, and every person in Ossery is a person in severe unfiling today and has been since March and none of you has been suspended, because the fire pump was inspected on Tuesday and put on a cart and brought to a field, and that is a current act by a named work, and a current act by a work keeps the premises of record of the settlement standing for the season."

"That is not fair," said Maud Tray.

"It is not fair at all," said Warden Kade. "It is a rule, and the rule is that a work of record that has been maintained within the season carries the premises it serves, and nobody in your district has claimed that fire pump in four years because the fire pump is a fire pump and not a count, and I have just claimed it for you, and it is legal, and I am going to have to write it in the second column with a figure against it, and the figure is a figure about a pump and not a figure about your twelve houses, and the form does not care which, and the twelve houses draw this season."

The four men did not move for a second or two. Then one of them said, "Well," and started untying the pump off the cart.

Owen Rusk stood at the west gate of Cutter's Fields at about a quarter past eleven in the morning on the Thursday of the eightieth day of the month and watched a settlement get its water back for a season because a fire pump had been inspected on the Tuesday and put on a cart, and he worked out, standing up, in a field, in the smell of the tanneries, at about the fourth minute after he had worked it out, that **he would not have found that, and that a form in a building would not have found it, and that the whole of the difficulty with the count is that it is a column and a column has no line in it for a thing somebody did because they thought it was right.**

And then, immediately after, because he is honest with himself before he is honest with anybody else: he also worked out that Maud Tray's four men had brought the pump up from a hamlet forty minutes off the water, unasked, on a cart, on a Thursday, and that they had done that before anybody at that field had asked them to, and that the Warden had not known they were coming, and that she had turned round a water draw in about ninety seconds using a rule that nobody had thought to use, and that if she had not come out of that tent when the woman with the slate said her name, the rule would not have been used and Ossery would have been suspended in the ninth month of the year.

And he could not decide, then or afterwards, whether that was the service working or one competent person rescuing a rule, and he never did decide it, and it is the first thing in this volume he carried without ever putting it down.

---

The first settlement was not touched that week. They did a place on the Friday with two premises and a working saw, and a place on the Saturday with a hard winter and nine households, and a place on the Monday with nine premises of record and four of them in a building that had been cut in two by a road in the Concord Year 459 and still had two numbers and one door.

Owen Rusk was in the field for all of it and read all of it and carried no papers on two of the three days because he was told on the first day that a person who carries papers into a reconciliation may be asked to produce them and cannot be asked to explain them, and he thought about the sentence for two hours on a river post and decided that it was not a rule and was a habit, and that the habit was honest.

On the Monday afternoon of the eighty-fourth day of the month, the eighth day of the notice, the boat came past the field with four men in it and a **pilot** who was a girl of about seventeen from a landing below the mill, and the girl said, without being asked, that there was a bend two hours up where a woman kept a sluice and there were eleven houses and half of them were in a mill, and Owen Rusk said the name of the settlement out loud before she finished the sentence.

"Nether Sill," she said. "You know it?"

"My office keeps the figures for it," said Owen Rusk. "Nine premises of record, last reconciled in the Concord Year 486."

The girl looked at him.

"It is nine on the county sheet," she said. "It has been nine for years. There are four buildings you could put a door on and a mill and a sluice house, and the mill is not a house and the sluice house is a house of one, and I have lived here."

"Then it is nine on the county sheet," said Owen Rusk, "and there are four on the ground, and that is a difference of five, and I am going up on Sunday, and I would be glad of the boat."

---

He got back down the hill to Lower Lock at about half past five on the Monday and told Ada Rusk three things: that a warden had come up the river with a stone and a quota of eleven, that the season opened in ten days, and that he was going up to Nether Sill on the Sunday in a survey boat with a girl of seventeen from a landing below the mill.

"You are going up to a place with four doors and a county sheet with nine on it," said Ada Rusk.

"Yes, and I am going up on Saturday, in the survey boat, with the girl who told me."

"Are you going up because a girl of seventeen told you about it on a river post, or because the board is nailed up in a field and you cannot leave a thing alone once you have read it."

"Both," said Owen Rusk. "And because a woman at Arbour Kitchen asked me to read a form, and because I have been in this building for eight years and I did not go and look at a column, and because in ten days a thing happens to eleven places on this river and four of them are in this district and one of them is four streets from this hall, and I do not know what to do about that and I have not got anybody to ask."

"You have got me," said Ada Rusk. "I am not anybody. I am a woman of twenty-four with a crew and a slate and a sister who cannot leave a pump. If you go up there and somebody asks you a question you cannot answer, come back and say so in this hall and do not come back and tell us it is being handled."

"That is the fourth time you have said that."

"It is the fifth time you have not listened to it," said Ada Rusk, "and it is the first time I have said it to you in a hall with nobody else in it, so you can go."
