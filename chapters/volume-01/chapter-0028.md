# Chapter 28 — The Name

It came back on the Tuesday. That was the thing. It came back inside two seconds, on the same envelope, on the same morning, and Owen Rusk wrote the routing slip with his right hand and did not take the notebook out of his coat, and went on with the day's work, and went home upriver in the rain and told nobody.

The name was **Vane, H.**

Three letters and a comma and a full stop, and it was the way his own building had written a man for nine years.

A covering slip is what a clerk signs when a bundle goes to a counter, and a covering slip has three columns, and the third column is the receiving officer, because a bundle that arrives at a counter and cannot be traced to a hand is a bundle nobody will be able to say was ever sent. The Ropewalk district counter took a great deal of the River Desk's business. Owen had covered papers to it four times a quarter for nine years, about ninety sheets a bundle, a third of the district's quarter coming through the assisted trays, and every one of those sheets had carried the same three letters in the office's small print in the third column.

So the name had come to him about four hundred times a quarter. He did the arithmetic on the Tuesday night, in the register, honestly, because a man who cannot even do the arithmetic is a man who has started to make things up. It came to him about once every hour and a quarter of a working week, and it had come to him for nine years, and he had never once had to stop and get it, and he had never once written it out in a sentence, and it had never once been a thing he knew.

That is the shape of it. Not a fact about a man. A *retrieval*. And a retrieval that has happened four hundred times a quarter for nine years is not a memory; it is a groove, and a groove is not a thing you can be proud of or sorry about, it is a thing a person has worn into themselves by doing a job, and on the Monday night of the thirty-fifth day of the month the groove was gone and the string was on an envelope in front of him and there was nothing where it should have been.

---

The third time was the Tuesday afternoon, at about two o'clock, and that one was shorter and worse, and he got past it by writing *Ropewalk* and a stop and nothing else, because a covering slip takes a counter.

The fourth time was the Wednesday morning.

He had been putting the same three letters on covering slips since half past seven, and at ten past eight he was on the tenth one and he had stopped, and the pen was on the paper, and there was a shape in his mouth and no word in it. That was two days and a night and he had spent two days and a night on it, and there was no crisis in it and no cause in it, and the days do not stop for this, and the copying floor does not know, and the second desk does not stop, and the hall went on at half past six the way it goes on.

That is the whole of the symptom. There is no horror in it. There is a pen on a sheet of paper and a third column and a man's hand waiting to be a man's hand, and nothing arrives, and there is no noise, and nothing at all happens that anybody in a hall of sixty people could see or record or be blamed for.

Owen Rusk did not panic. He wrote *Ropewalk district counter* in the third column, because a covering slip takes a counter and not a man and he had four hundred instances of the practice, and he signed it, and he put it in the bundle, and he said to himself, out loud, in a flat voice, at the end of the second desk, where two people could hear him and neither of them looked up:

"That is not the same as the Monday. That is a name and not a retrieval. Write the counter."

He wrote the counter. It went up. The bundle was covered and went to the stair and came back at half past eleven with a seal on it.

And the second thing that happened on the Wednesday, at about half past eleven, is that nothing happened. That is the sentence. **Nothing happened.** He stood at the end of a board in a hall where two hundred and eleven sheets were being compared, with a name-shaped absence in him, and the press went, and a door went, and somebody asked a colleague a question about a continuation, and nobody noticed anything at all, because there is nothing to notice, and because the absence looks *exactly* like a clerk who did not read that line today.

Which is the horror, and it is not frightening. It is **boring.** That is the part. He had spent a month being frightened of his own precision, and every shape his fear had taken in four weeks had had teeth in it, and this one has none: it is a man in a hall writing a counter where a man used to be, and the only person on earth who could tell that anything has happened is the man himself, and there is no form of it, and there is nobody to be angry at, and it does not go away.

---

He found it at the tray, at half past nine on the Wednesday morning of the thirty-seventh day of the month, from the ninth item in the unrouteable correspondence, an hour and a half after the fourth time and while there was still a bundle of sheets to cover for the stair.

The unrouteable correspondence goes into a tray, and the tray is a tray, and a tray is not a drawer and cannot be locked and is not a record of anything. Owen had a piece of luck on that Wednesday morning that he will be embarrassed about for the rest of his life: on top of the stack, under a bundle of returned rate notices, was an envelope with a **glass window** in it, the kind that is put on a notice so that a person can see whether there is a letter waiting for them, and the window had come away at one corner, and the county's own routing hand was visible through the tear.

*Returned, Ropewalk district. Not known at the address.*

Owen Rusk put his hand on the board.

He stood at the tray for about a minute and a half and then he read the envelope the way a clerk reads a piece of evidence, and it took him a while, because the envelope was addressed in a county hand and not his office's, and the address was the whole of it.

*H. Vane, 4 Lantern Walk.*

Three letters and a comma, in a hand about forty years old, written by somebody who was copying another hand, or copying out of a book, and the point about it — the point that took him a minute and a half and nearly took his legs — is that the county had his *initials* and no more, and had had them for about forty years, and had addressed nine hundred days of quarterly notices to three letters and a surname, and the envelope had come back twice, and the two returns were pinned to it, and the mark on both was the same three words in the same hand.

*Not known at the address.*

Owen Rusk held an envelope that told him three things.

One: the name had been in his hands four hundred times a quarter for nine years and it belonged to a man at four Lantern Walk whom a district office had failed to find, twice, in a quarter, and the returns had come back to the tray where they live and had been put on the stack, and the stack is not a record, and nobody is paid to look at a stack.

Two: the notice inside was a district works notice. Not a demand for money and not a threat. The ordinary quarterly notice that goes to the holder of a duty on a public work, telling the holder what the office is doing with the work. There were four hundred and six of them in that quarter, and this one was the one in his hand, and it had been returned twice, and he had never known it existed.

Three, and this is the part that made him sit down on the arm of the stool at the end of the board, which he was not supposed to do: **the name came back and it was worse.**

Because a name that does not come is a thing that has happened to him, in him, and there is no witness and no paper and nobody to be told, and he can go on working. A name that comes back on a returned envelope with two pins on it is not his at all. It is a fact about a man at four Lantern Walk that this city has failed to establish for nine years, four times a quarter, and the failure is on a piece of paper, and the piece of paper is in a tray, and the tray is his, and the tray has no schedule, and there is no first name for the man in any register in Docketford because nobody has ever needed one.

He had spent a month looking for a way to be told something terrible and the thing that arrived on the Wednesday morning was a returned letter.

He did not write the name in the notebook. He understood within about a second why he was not going to, and the reason is not honour and it is not the promise he made to himself in a warm room: the notebook does not have it *already*. He copied the wording of a destroyed statement into that book in the small hours of a Tuesday because a book is a place where a thing is kept, and he has never once written a person's name in it who he has not spoken to, and he was not going to start at thirty-two over a window, and that is the whole of the reason and it took him about a second and it is the correct answer and he was not comforted by it.

He put the envelope back on the stack.

---

He did not tell the ward. He did not tell Pell. He did not write a line about it anywhere, and the not-writing was now a thing he recognised, and he had a name for it, and the name was *a habit*.

At about nine he asked Pell a routine question about a bundle going to the stair and she answered it and he answered her and neither of them noticed anything, because there is nothing to notice, and that was the fourth and worst part of it: not that it is invisible to other people, but that it is *invisible to the person*, most of the time, in the way a missing tooth is not a missing tooth on the day.

He got through the rest of the day. He got through it because there is a return that comes due on the thirty-ninth and a ward that is going to be read on the Thursday by a woman from a landing upriver who has told him to his face that being legible is not the same as being paid, and because two hundred and eleven sheets had to be compared, and because a man with a name-shaped hole in him and a tray of unrouteable correspondence in front of him is, above all things, an ordinary man on an ordinary day.

He went up the hill at six and stood in the doorway of the shed where the marker boards are kept, which is a corrugated lean-to at the top of Fetter Lane with a bench in it and a smell of creosot and wet rope, and Ada Rusk was in it doing something to a lamp with a spanner, and she has watched him for a month with the specific attention of a woman who is waiting to be told something.

"You have stopped writing things down," said Ada Rusk.

He had the notebook out. He had it out because he had taken it out of his coat on the way up, which is a thing he has done every day for a month, and it was open in his hand at the page with the four lines, and it was open at a page with two lines on it that had been four lines on Friday.

"I have not stopped writing things down," said Owen.

"No, you have not," said Ada, without looking up from the lamp. "You have written *less*, and that is worse, and I want you to hear the difference out loud, because you have not heard it and I have watched you for a month not hearing it. Less is not the same as stopping. A man who stops is unwell. A man who writes less has decided that there is less worth having, and that is a *judgement*, Mr Rusk, and a judgement is a thing a man does about the world, and you have been letting a clerk at a desk do it for you and calling it a day."

"I have not decided anything."

"You decided it this week," said Ada Rusk, "and you have not written down what it was, and I am not going to ask you what it was, and I want that on the record as well, because I have been in four rooms this month where a person was asked to account for a thing that was not theirs to account for and I am not going to be one of them."

She put the spanner down.

"I am not going to ask. But I am going to tell you what I have noticed, and then I am going back to the lamp. You have not written anything down since the end of last week. Not the crew's hours, which you used to copy whether I asked you or not. Not the pump. Not the roster. And I do not mind about the roster, because the roster is mine and I have it in a book. What I mind about is that the thing that has gone quiet in your book is *you*."

He stood in the doorway of a shed in the wet with a notebook open in his hand at a page with two lines on it and did not say anything, and Ada Rusk picked the spanner up again and went back to the lamp and let him go, which she had never done before.

He went home upriver. He did not eat. He sat at the table with his mother's good plate on it and a lamp on it, and he did the arithmetic, because it was the only thing he had that was true, and he wrote one line in the register at the bottom of a page, and the line is the last true sentence he wrote that week and he has never been able to improve it.

*Four hundred a quarter for nine years. It came back on a returned letter with two pins on it and the pins said not known, and that is worse, because a name that does not come is a thing that happened to me and a name that comes back on a letter is a thing that happened to him, at 4 Lantern Walk, and I have copied it about fifteen thousand times and I never once knew it was a man.*

He did not sleep that night. He got up at four and went down to the copying floor and did two hundred sheets because there were two hundred sheets, and he did not tell anybody what had happened and he was not going to tell anybody what had happened, and it was not going to go away, and there was no form for it, and no office in the city was going to be asked about it, and no clerk in the building would have noticed, and there was no part of his record that had changed in any way anybody could put on a card.

He was still a copy clerk. The card on the trestle table still had four forbidden lines on it and his name under them, and he had signed a box, and the box was the whole of his authority, and none of it had gone up, and none of it had come down, and he had learned in the fourth week of the rain season that a duty has a price, and that the price is not paid in money, and is not paid in sleep, and is not paid in standing, and is not recoverable by anybody in any office, and is not to be told to anybody, ever, because the moment it is told it becomes a thing somebody can be careful with him about, and the one thing he wanted in the world was to go on being a person it was safe to be careful about.

The return came due on the thirty-ninth and the ward was going to be read on the Thursday, and the returned letter was back on the top of the stack where it had been, in the tray, under nine other pieces of paper that had been returned by people who could not be found, and Owen Rusk came in at ten to seven and put his hand on the board and did not open the notebook, and did not need to, because he had it, and it was going to cost him that for the rest of his life and there was nobody to sue and nothing to appeal and no term on it at all.
