# Chapter 52 — The List Comes Off

The thing about a tray of unfiled correspondence is that it is not a punishment, and that nobody tells you that, and that you can spend a month finding out.

Owen Rusk had spent six working days finding out. The tray holds four kinds of thing: notices that came back *not known at the address*, petitions returned for want of a name, correspondence addressed to a person the Office has a surname and an initial for and nothing else, and a fifth kind that has no name of its own, which is correspondence addressed to a **place** that the Office cannot find, which arrives because somebody posted it and cannot be returned because returning it needs an address.

The first four kinds are about people. He had known that in the first week. The fifth kind had been on the tray twice while he worked it and he had put it at the bottom of the pile each time, the way you put a thing at the bottom of a pile that is not yet your business.

On the Wednesday morning of the seventy-second day of the month it came up on the top, because about a third of the way down there was a second one of the same kind with the same postmark and the same handwriting on the envelope and a date four months earlier, and Owen Rusk is a man who cannot leave two identical things on two different days and has never been able to.

It was a **quarterly notice of the Count's preliminary return for Docketford district**, sent to every premises of record in the district, and it was addressed to a **culvert house at Pegler's Cut**, and the address was good, and the address was forty years old, and the culvert house had been a real building with a real roof and a door and a man who opened it when the water came, and it had been taken down in the spring by a district works crew and the rubble had gone down the river and the water still came through the culvert every year and stood in a field by the mill road and drained away, and the field was in a schedule of premises and the culvert house was not in anything at all.

Three of them had come back. The first two said *not known at the address* in a clerk's hand at a counter. The third said the same thing in the same clerk's hand and had **demolished** written across the line in a second hand, in pencil, by somebody who was not a clerk, and the second hand was not a careful hand.

Owen Rusk stood at the end of the second desk on the Wednesday morning of the seventy-second day of the month and looked at a form that had come back three times, and read the sentence at the foot of it that he had read about four hundred times in six weeks.

*This notice is sent to premises of record. If you consider that a premises of record has been wrongly omitted, or wrongly included, you may make enquiry of the district office, second counter.*

There is no form on which a person may say *the water still comes*. There is no column for a building that is not there and a thing that is. There is a schedule of premises, and the culvert house was struck out of it in the spring, correctly, because it is a heap of stone in a river, and the field it drained was not struck out, and the field is in the schedule, and the field is now the thing that the notice is asking about, and the field cannot be asked anything.

He put the three returns in a bundle with a string on it, because a thing you have worked out cannot be put back in an unsorted tray, and he wrote in his own register — the one that does not leave the building and is not the one that does — one line: *Pegler's Cut, culvert house, demolished in the spring, the drain still runs, three returns, no form.*

And then, because he is who he is, he did the second thing, which is the thing that costs, and which he has been forbidden from doing in public for seven weeks by his own discipline, and which is: he went and found out who demolished it.

It took him eleven minutes. It was in the district works return for the spring, on the ground floor, in a column headed *removed from schedule*, in a clerk's hand, with a day on it and no reason, because the district works returns keep removals and not reasons, and are not required to keep reasons, and the Office's own published position on a different matter in a public hall in the last month was five clauses and a courtesy saying exactly that.

The day was the eighteenth of the month. The entry said *culvert house, Pegler's Cut — removed from schedule, not reinstated*. Eight words and a date. There was a *not reinstated* in it, which meant somebody had thought about it, and Owen Rusk stood and looked at that for a while, and worked out that *not reinstated* is what a column says when the person filling it in was told the answer, and that there was no second question in the district works return, and that the second question had been somewhere else in this city, or had not been asked.

He wrote the eighteen of the month in his register too, and put the book away, and went and did the other two hundred and thirty-odd sheets on the tray, and was forty minutes late, and was forty minutes late again on the Thursday.

---

He went up the hill on the Tuesday morning of the seventy-first day of the month with five lines in his head and no sheet, because a man who reads out five things from a sheet is a man who has a sheet.

It was about nine in the morning and there were four people in the hall and no stove, and Marguerite Bray was at the end of the long table with the delivery book open in front of her and the pencil in her hand, which on a Tuesday morning in the second week of a dry season is how you know the hall is open for business and not for shelter.

He read them out.

He read them out in the order they were written in, in the ordinary voice, at the pace of a man reading a menu, and he did not say *item one* and he did not say *firstly*, and when he got to the third one he nearly did say *the third item* and did not, and the not-saying-it took him about a second and a half.

*One. The pension of Cobb Rennie, of two rooms in this district, who is of no roll in this city and has been for two years, and whom nothing in this matter reaches.*

Bray wrote it in the second column and ruled the day and the hour.

*Two. Six persons of this district named on a schedule held in the intake year 486, of whom three are children, and for whom no protective return is entered, three of the five required parts of which exist and two of which are Registry functions nobody in this city is paid to provide.*

Nobody said anything. There is a woman at the back of that hall who has been in it since the third week of the month and who did not move.

*Three. Four pages in a bundle, of which one carries a refusal, one is blank on purpose and two are unanswered.*

A day after the hour of the seal, two of them are answered. He said so, because he always checks, and the checking is the only professional habit he has that has ever been any use to anybody. One carries a refusal in a cook's hand. One carries a duty in a watchman's hand. One carries four sentences in a bridge mechanic's capitals. And **one is blank on purpose**, and the woman whose name is at the top of it is standing in this hall with her basket at her feet and has said out loud that she would rather hand over a page with her name on it and nothing on it than a page with her name on it saying nothing.

*Four. The repair of the abutment, four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds, unfunded, with a notice at the weir path inviting any district able to perform the repair to perform it.*

And the hall did a thing he had not expected, which was that four people said the number out loud at the same time. It is a number this ward has said in public now so many times that it has stopped being a number and become a noise, and the noise of it in that hall on the Tuesday morning of the seventy-first day was not grief and was not anger. It was four people keeping a thing in the air.

*Five. The receipt of the district and of its witnesses, promised in the answer of the nineteenth day of this month, on a day to be named and an hour to be named, and not given.*

The receipt is not in that hall. The receipt is on the ground floor of the River Desk in this city, standing on a spike, with a heading on it, and nobody has taken it off, and it is the fifth of five and it is the only one of the five that is nobody's fault, and it is the only one of the five that has a name on the spike.

Bray read the five back in her own voice without being asked, which is what she does, and ruled them into the delivery book under the line she had ruled the previous evening, in the second column, with the day and the hour, and put the pencil down.

Then she ruled one more line, and she ruled it before anybody asked her to, and she said it out loud as she wrote it because she has never once in thirty-six years of ruling that book written anything she had not said first.

"**And under all five of these, in this hand: no person in this room is under any of them, and the man who read them out is not under any of them, and the person who was under them until four o'clock on Monday afternoon was under them for seven days and put them down on Monday afternoon because a form said seven days and not one day more.**"

"And rule," said Ada Rusk from the wall, "that nobody is to be got at about it, and rule it in capitals, because in about three weeks somebody in a good mood is going to try to be kind to one of us about that receipt."

"It is ruled," said Marguerite Bray.

Nobody thanked anybody. Cobb Rennie's name went into the second column of a book in a ward hall two miles upriver from a building in which there is no column for it, and that is what a column is.

---

He came down the hill at about half past ten on the Tuesday with the thing done, and the thing he felt on the walk down was not relief and was not the understanding and was something he had not got a word for, which is the feeling of putting a heavy thing down in a field in the dark and finding that your arms are shaking and that the field is the same field it was this morning.

He had carried five things for seven days. He had held them, which is a skill, and which is a strange thing to have been taught by a form, and the reason he was shaking was that the skill did not go away when the reason for it did, and the arms do not know that.

On the Wednesday morning he was at the ground floor at about a quarter past nine for a bundle that had come up from the intake room, and he was passing the countersignature desk on the way back to the tray, and he stopped, because the spike was not where it had been.

It was still there. It was the same spike, in the same corner, by the same door, and there were two things on it and there had been one thing on it.

The receipt was still on it, at the back, behind a quarterly works notice and a sub-table return, and it was where he had last seen it, and he could see the corner of it, and the heading on it said *Request for a receipt, in petition 487-GS-19, of the steward and of the witnesses and of such persons as the petitioner names.*

The new thing was a printed card, wired to the spike, and it was in the size of a hand and it was in the ordinary hand of this office, and it said:

**RETENTION OF INSTRUMENTS ON SPIKE. Paper on this spike is retained for the period stated on the sheet it belongs to. On the expiry of that period the sheet is disposed of by the standing rule. The rule is item nine of the district office's Schedule of Instruments in Force. The rule does not require a person to be told.**

Owen Rusk read it twice, and then he did the arithmetic, standing at a spike on the ground floor of a building where he is a stranger, at about a quarter past nine in the morning on the Wednesday of the seventy-second day of the month.

The receipt belonged to a petition. The petition was answered on the nineteenth day of the month, and the answer promised a receipt on a day to be named at an hour to be named. The petition's answer had come and gone. The hearing had been held on the fifty-ninth and had gone and come back again on the sixty-sixth. And on the sixty-third day of the month a **certification** had been sealed in this building on a form on the third landing, and that form had a term on it, and the term had ended at about half past three on Monday afternoon, and an instrument that has run its term and is superseded by a line in a column of a book is an instrument that has been retained for its period.

And the period on a petition's papers in this city, for a matter concluded, is **two years** from the answer, or, where no answer has ever been made, **fourteen days from the making of the last entry** — and the receipt was the district's request for a receipt, and the district's request was made in answer to a promise, and no answer had ever been made to the request, and the request was therefore, in the ordinary way this office holds things, un-answered since the day it was written.

Fourteen days from the last entry. The last entry on that request was the day it was written, which was the twentieth day of the month, the day after the answer, when the ward had gone in and asked for the receipt it had been promised and had been given the form for asking on it.

So the thing standing on that spike with a heading on it, the fifth of five lines, the only one of the five that nobody was ever going to be blamed for, was in its fourteenth day on a Wednesday, and there was no person in this city who was required to be told about it, and the rule that said so was item nine of a schedule, and item fourteen of the same schedule was four hundred and forty-three years old and had never been amended, and both of them were right.

Owen Rusk stood at a spike for about a minute and a half.

Then he did a thing he had not planned and would not have been able to justify, which was that he took the receipt off the spike, and carried it up the stairs, and put it flat on the tray on the second desk under a weight, which is not what a tray of unfiled correspondence is for and which he did in full view of a building in which he is a stranger.

He wrote in his own register, on the Wednesday morning, standing up, the following, and it is the whole of the entry and there is no second line:

*Receipt of petition 487-GS-19 is on this desk and not on the spike. I have taken it off the spike. If it is disposed of on the fourteenth day, I will be able to say that I took it off, and the ward will be able to say that it was taken off, and neither of those is the ward being given a receipt. It is still owed. It is the fifth of five. Nobody is to be told about this for about a week and then I will have to tell somebody.*
