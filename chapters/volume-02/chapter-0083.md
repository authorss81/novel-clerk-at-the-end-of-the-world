# Chapter 83 — A District May Survey Its Own Book

The room is on the second floor at the back, off a corridor that smells of damp and has not had a window opened in this building's memory, and the door has a key and the key is on a board with four other keys and Owen Rusk has had it for nine years, and he has been in the room about nine times in nine years, and the reason he has the key is that the room holds the district's own bound book of premises of record and the book is heavy and the clerk who used to carry it up had a bad back.

He went up on the Saturday morning of the fifteenth day of the third month at about half past nine with a sheet of foolscap and a pencil and the return for the season of 489, and he shut the door behind him, and the room has no window, and there is one light in it, and it is a bulb on a flex and the flex has been taped twice.

**The district's book of premises of record. Four columns. Bound in a canvas ledger about two inches thick, with a brass corner on each cover and no name on the spine, because the name of the district is on the first page and has been on the first page since the book was started.**

*One. The premises of record, with the settlement they stand in and the street of record.*
*Two. The water by instrument.*
*Three. The season to which the entry belongs.*
*Four. Struck, or not struck.*

He had been in the room about nine times in nine years and he had never once gone down the third column, and he had never once gone down the fourth.

**There is no column in this book for when a person was at the door.** That was the first thing and it took him about four seconds to establish and about twenty minutes to believe. The book is the district's own record of what it has got, and it has got four columns, and three of them are about the water and the season and the striking, and one of them is a list of doors, and **nothing in nine years and two hundred yards of a bound book has ever recorded that anybody ever stood in front of one of those doors and looked at it.**

He went down the settlement rows until he came to the ten that are named in the schedule printed in the last week of the month before last, and it took him about an hour and a half, and the ten are the ten and the figures are the figures and there is nothing whatever in nine feet of canvas and two hundred yards of a bound book that a person in a village could learn.

**Wexford Landing, four. Gallants Reach, six. Lower Lock, eleven. Quill Bank, nine. Rush Halt, seven. Witherow, three. Ossery, eleven. Otter Stair, nine. Ashlocks, six.**

And the tenth row of the settlement rows in this book, which is the row that has no village in it, because the book is the district office's book and the district office is a place with a street of record and six doors and a standpipe in it and a stove and a yard and a person who washes in the yard, and it is entered in the district's own book of premises of record on the same page and in the same hand as a mill at Rush Halt.

**And one of the six is the room he was standing in.**

He put his hand flat on the page and did not write anything about it, because the moment he wrote a thing about the tenth row of a district book he had a document about a choice of places, and the only permitted act with that is nothing, and it is not even a secret any more; the whole of this county has read that row off a frame in a field and about thirty people read it out loud on the seventh of the month.

**A line against which a quantity is authorised is a line.** He had written that out himself on the back of a form in the first month of the year and it had come back in five clauses with a number on it, and it was true, and he had written *I do not argue this and I am not permitted to argue it and I would be ashamed of arguing it* under a ground that was true, and he had not read the room again since and he was not going to now.

---

He did it properly, which took him the best part of the morning and is the only desk act of his working life and he is not proud of it.

He went down the third column. It is the column that matters, because it is the one that gets carried forward, and it is the column that makes the return what the return is. He went down it line by line to the settlement rows for the ten named in the schedule of the twenty-eighth of the second month, and he read the season to which each entry belongs, and he read the fourth column, and then he did the thing that made the whole of the afternoon.

**Every entry in the third column that belongs to a settlement of this district says 486 or says 479.** Not because anybody decided in 479 and 486. Because the season is carried forward every year by a machine and a person initials the machine, and the entry belongs to the season it was first entered and it stays in that season forever, and so **the season column does not say when anybody looked. It says when somebody first wrote the line down, which is a different fact, and it is the only fact the book has, and it is the reason the fifth column of the return is empty and is going to be empty in June.**

So he took a sheet of foolscap and ruled it into ten rows and wrote in it the date on which he had gone down the third column of the district's own book of premises of record, having been in the room he has the key to, alone, with a bulb on a flex.

**The fifteenth day of the third month of the Concord Year 488, which is a Saturday.**

Ten rows, ten dates, the same date, and a heading over them that he wrote out in full because a heading is the whole of it:

**Year of last survey of the premises of record of each settlement named in the schedule of settlements to be reconciled for the season of the Concord Year 489. Surveyed by the district office in respect of the district's own book of premises of record, in a room in the district office at Fetter Bend, and not in the settlement. The fifteenth day of the third month.**

He read it back twice before he signed it, and he dated it, and he signed it *Owen Rusk, officer of the record of the district returns*, and then he sat looking at it for about a quarter of an hour in a room with no window.

**It is an invention with a date on it.** Not a lie. A survey of the district office's own book, performed by an officer of that office, on a day, in a room, and the day is the day it was done, and the year that would go in the fifth column is the year a man in a room in Fetter Bend read a book and wrote a line down, **and the year is a Saturday in the third month in a room two miles up the hill from a ward of eleven doors, and it is going to be better protected than the truth, because it is dated and it is signed, and nobody will be able to prove a single thing about it in about four years, and the man who put it there will be a man in a building somewhere who was asked a question and answered it honestly in a form.**

Then he wrote out the sentence he had already worked out and then some, in his own hand, on the back of it, and it is this:

**The fifteenth day of the third month is a day on which a person read a book in a room in Fetter Bend and did not go to a settlement. It is not a year in which Lower Lock was surveyed. It is not a year in which anything was looked at. And it would be entered in ten cells of the fifth column of the district return for the season of 489 and it would be the truest thing on that return and it would mean that nobody went anywhere, and it would be the second year in a row that the fifth column has been filled in by a person in an office and not by a person at a door, and the year before last it was a man in a water office with a bad knee who wrote not known ninety times and was thanked by nobody.**

He read that back too, and then he did not tear it up, because a torn sheet about a date is not a record and he has learned that twice.

---

Here is the whole of why he did not send it, and it is two things and he is not going to be able to decide for the rest of his life which of them was the reason, and that is the honest shape of it.

**The first is that it is his.** The return for the season of 490 is going to be completed out of a water office's answer to a fourteen-day request, and about ninety of the hundred and ninety-four lines on that answer say *not known* in a man's honest hand, and in about a year that hand is going into ten cells as a year somebody looked at something, and that man will be asked about it and he will say that he answered a form. He will be right. **And if Owen Rusk sends a return in this month with a date in it that he wrote himself, in his own hand, on purpose, knowing exactly what it is, then in about a year there will be two offices in this county manufacturing surveys, and one of them will be his, and it will be the better documented of the two because he is the better clerk.**

**The second is the determination.**

The determination of the twenty-fifth stands: no reconciliation in Docketford district in reliance upon the district return unless and until that return is completed and current. The moment that return is completed and current, that determination is spent, and the district may reconcile anything in reliance upon it, and anybody anywhere may bring a challenge that a paper is not current, and stop it, and the fourteen days will run on a place.

**And Owen Rusk worked that out in a room in Concord Hall on the Sunday morning of the twenty-fifth of the second month, in about four seconds, and wrote it down, and sent it up the stairs anyway, because Junia Pell told him he had handed a body a written instruction to be cured and had not read it.**

He had been carrying the answer to his own question for eighteen days, and eighteen days is how long it takes a man to work out that the thing he has just been praised for and the thing he has just done are the same act.

**If he completes the fifth column, he ends the only instrument in this county that is currently stopping a season's work on his own district. And the man who ends it is him, and he will do it in the district's own hand with a date on it, and the file will show a Continuance Examiner of the Count who brought a challenge about a paper in the second month and made that same paper current in the third month, and both of those facts are in the same hand and they are four weeks apart and neither one is a lie.**

He put the sheet down on the desk of the room he has the key to, and squared it, and then picked it up again and put it inside the back cover of the district's own book of premises of record, which is the one place in that building where a piece of paper is not noticed, and which is where things have been put in this office for a hundred and forty years by people who did not want them noticed, and **he knows that, and he has known it for nine years, and it is the reason the room has a key on a board and not a person.**

---

Halden Pike came up the corridor at about half past four on the Monday afternoon of the seventeenth day of the third month with a tray of nothing under one arm, which is a thing a Deputy Registrar of Docketford is allowed to do and almost never does.

He is forty-seven. He has been the deputy registrar of this district for eleven years and he has been offered the registrar's chair twice and has said no both times in writing, and the register of contested closures is the reason and everybody in the building knows it is the reason. He is not a rival to anybody. He is a man who is good at a thing and has decided to keep doing it.

He put the tray down on the shelf and stood with his back to the door, because Owen Rusk was in the room with the light on and there is one light in it and it had been on since half past nine.

"You have had the light on in here all day," he said.

"I have."

Pike came and looked at the book on the desk, and then at the foolscap that was not there any more because it was in the back cover, and he did not open the book, and he worked out in about a second and a half that the back cover was where it was.

"**Then you have done it,**" he said.

"I have."

"You have put a year in ten cells."

"**I have not put a year in ten cells. I have written ten dates on a sheet of foolscap in a room with a bulb on a flex and I have not sent it up the stairs, and the sheet is in the back of a book that has been on that shelf since the Concord Year 348, and you are going to tell me in about nine seconds whether you would have done it, and then I am going to go down the hill.**"

"I would have done it in 471," said Halden Pike, "and I did, and it took an afternoon, and it is the reason I asked for the register in a room of thirty people three years later and it is the reason I have said no to a chair twice, **and I have never told anybody either of those two things, and I have just told you both of them in about six seconds, and you may make of that whatever you like and I would rather you did not.**"

Owen Rusk did not make anything of it.

"The Office has no view on it," he said. "It has been put to me in writing and in a form and the form is correct. A district may survey its own book at any time it likes and nothing in force stops it."

"That is not because the Office thinks it is proper," said Pike. "**That is because the Office has never had to have a view on it, and there is a difference, and you have not worked out which side of that sentence you are standing on and I have not either.**"

"A date is a fact about a room," said Owen Rusk. "That is all a date is. Once it is in a column it stops being a fact about a room and starts being a fact about a place, and there is nobody in this county who can tell the two apart afterwards, and that is not a scandal, it is a filing."

Pike looked at him for about three seconds, which from a man who is quick with a tray is a considerable thing.

"**You have been in this building nine years and that is the first sentence anybody has said in it that I have not had a better version of,**" he said. "And I am going to tell you the better version now and then we are going to stand here and not say anything else about it for the rest of the season."

"All right."

"A date is not a fact about a room. A date is a fact about a room, **and it is a promise about a place.** The moment you write it in a column you have said to a settlement, in a document, without saying it, *there is somebody who looks at you on this day.*" Pike shifted his weight. "**And there is not one person in this county who has ever been lied to by a date, and I want that said, because everybody in Concord Hall thinks they have. Dates are not false. They are kept by people who have stopped, and the keeping is the whole of the injury, and the worst of it is that the people who keep them are not the ones who stopped.**"

The bulb on the flex buzzed.

"You read the third column in October," said Owen Rusk.

"I read the third column in October in a room in this building with about nine other people in it and I asked one question about a draw in a field afterwards in front of about thirty people, and I asked the man of the Water Office not to be named, and he would not have told you if you had asked him, and you did not ask him, and I have not said thank you for that and I am not going to, because you did not do me a favour. You did the ordinary thing."

"I did the ordinary thing because if I had asked him you would have known I knew."

"Yes," said Halden Pike. "**Which is the same sentence twice and both halves of it are yours.**"

He put his hand on the shelf.

"And you asked him not to be named, and he has not been named, and I have never known his name, and you are the deputy registrar of this district and you have a water office in your district and you could have had his name out of that room in about a fortnight by asking a person in a corridor, and you asked him for it in a field in front of thirty people and you asked him to be quiet about it."

"**I asked him in a field in front of about thirty people, on a Sunday, in the cold, because a question asked in a room is a conversation and a question asked in a field is a record,**" said Pike. "**And he said no, and the no was correct, and I have never asked him again, and that is the whole of my interest in the man of fifty-five, and I am not going to tell you his name and you are not going to ask me and if you do ask me I will say no at a counter, because that is where a person says no in this county.**"

"I am not going to ask you."

"I know you are not," said Pike. "**I have watched you not ask two people this year and I would like it noticed that I have watched, and that I have not told anybody, and that a register of ninety-one contested closures going back a century has three names in it from this district and the first of the three is mine, and the second is a man of about sixty who signed a return in the year 458 and is dead, and the third is a woman who is alive in this county and whom I have not been able to establish the name of in eleven years, and that is the register's business and not mine, and the register is printed once a year and it goes to the Council's counter and about eleven people read it.**"

---

Owen Rusk came down off the second floor at about five and Junia Pell was at the end of the second desk with her hand on a stack of assisted correspondence, and she had been waiting for him, and she is very good at waiting for a person in a way that consists entirely of not doing it visibly.

"You have been up there since half past nine," she said.

"Yes."

"With the light on."

"Yes."

"**And there is a sheet of foolscap in the back of a book that has been on that shelf since the Concord Year 348.**"

Owen Rusk stopped in the middle of the floor of the second floor of the district office of Docketford.

"I did not tell you that."

"You did not," said Junia Pell. "**I have been in this building two months and there is one room on the top of that stair with a key on a board and a bulb on a flex and I have known about it since the second week, and I have never been in it, and I have never asked, and I am telling you now that I have never asked, and that is the whole of what I have come to say, and I have said it in about nine seconds and I would like to go back to the assisted tray.**"

"Then say the other thing and go."

"The other thing is this." She took her hand off the correspondence. "**On the twenty-fifth of the month before last a woman with a slate told you that the answer to a challenge about a paper is that somebody stops, and that the fourteen days will run on a place because a man in a building noticed a date. And you wrote it down and sent it up. And you have had twenty-two days to think about what you would do with it and you have gone and done the thing that stops the most, and you have done it to yourself, and nobody in this county is going to warn you about that, and I am not going to warn you about that, and if you have been sitting up there all day telling yourself that somebody is going to come and stop you, there is nobody coming, and you worked it out in about nine seconds in a room in another city and you sent the answer up the stairs yourself.**"

The bulb on the flex buzzed on the second floor above them.

"**That is not a comfort,**" said Junia Pell. "I want to be exact, because you are about to go and be pleased with yourself for about four minutes. It is not a comfort. It is a district that has decided, in the only quiet way available to it, to be the district that nobody can look at, and to get there by being right twice, and about four years from now a person in Concord Hall is going to read two documents in this district's file that have your name on them and they are going to be about a challenge and a cure, and they are four weeks apart, and the one that cured it will not say why."

She picked the correspondence back up.

"I am not going to be thanked for that either," she said. "**Put it on the same page as everything else on it this season.**"

He went and did the tray and it was on at a quarter past six and it was empty, and had been since about nine, and a sheet of foolscap with ten identical dates on it was in the back of a book on a shelf that has been on a shelf since the Concord Year 348, and the fifth column of the district return for the season of 489 was still empty on all ten, and it is going to be empty in June, and in June of the season of 490 it is going to be full of a year a man in a water office with a bad knee put there honestly, and about a year after that somebody in this building is going to find that out and is going to be the man in the room, and he already knows whose hand it is.
