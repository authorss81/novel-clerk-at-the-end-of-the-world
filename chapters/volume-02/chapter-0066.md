# Chapter 66 — The Hand It Is Written In

A kitchen is not a room, and the difference is that a room is a thing the county counts.

That was the whole of the argument and Nell Arbour made it on the Monday to a woman of about fifty-four who had come up the hill to Fetter Lane to ask for the Continuance Examiner of the Count, and she made it in four words and then went and washed her hands, and Owen Rusk has thought since that those four words were worth more than the four she had said on the Tuesday about the doors.

**Forty** people came on the Wednesday morning of the last day of the first month of the Concord Year 488, at about ten, to a claimant kitchen in Docketford's Mill Ward with a stove in it and a long table that took sixteen at a stretch and two more along the wall, and **thirty-one of the forty came off the mud flats at Witherow**, in boots, and two of the thirty-one were under sixteen and had been told to stay by the door and stayed by the door for an hour and a half and did not move.

Nobody was in a queue. That is the thing about the Arbour Kitchen and it is not a small thing: there is no rail in Docketford outside the count room, and inside the count room the rail has three sides and a public stand on it, and the rail is why nine people come, and every one of the nine is a clerk. In a kitchen the rail is the door and the door is open and a person comes because it is on a door and not because it is on a letter.

Nell Arbour had put it on the door on the Saturday. It said a settlement, a matter, an hour, and **the officer of the count would sit at the table**, and under that, in her own capitals, *this is a kitchen and you may say so at the door if anybody stops you.*

---

The officer came at ten and did not look at the room for about as long as it takes to know that a room is forty people, and then she sat down at the end of the table with her back to the wall and put a plain flat stone on the table in front of her, four inches across with a Registry punch on the side of it, which is the officer's own stone and is for taking minutes and not for attesting anything.

"I want to say one thing before anybody speaks," she said, "and then I am going to hear this properly and I am going to take a long time, because there is no clock in this building and I have noticed that, and I am not going to be the person who brings one."

The stove ticked. Somebody at the back laughed and stopped.

"My name is not on this. My name is on the determination and not on the notice on the door, and that is not modesty, it is that in about four years somebody is going to want to know whether this hearing had a room or a kitchen, and if it had a room it was not heard properly, and if it had a kitchen it was not a room at all, and I am not going to be a person in a file about either of those. **There is no room in this county for a review of a settlement of three premises. I have said so in writing to a form. The form is correct.** So the question before me this morning is not whether this is a proper room. It is whether a review can be heard properly in a place that is not a room, and I am going to decide that, and I am deciding it in front of you, and if I decide it wrongly you will all be able to tell in about nine years exactly what I did and where."

"Then we will be able to tell you now," said Nell Arbour from the stove, "because there are forty of us and you are one."

"Yes," said the officer. "I had worked that out."

---

**Witherow.** Three premises of record. Eleven households. Last reconciliation 479, which is the year on a printed sheet put up in a field behind the tanneries on the twenty-eighth of the month before last, and a year that is nine years old, and a figure of three that is a correct figure of the doors of Witherow and possibly a figure of nothing else.

The review was requested by a person of the district, in her own name, on the fourth day of this month, and the name is on a public file in Concord Hall in two names, hers and Owen Rusk's, because the form has a line for the applicant and a line for the person of the district and nobody in Concord Hall has ever had the imagination to leave the second one blank.

The officer turned the request over twice and then put it down and did not read it out.

"I am going to ask four questions and they are the same four I asked in a room with high windows in this county on the twenty-fourth of the month, and I am going to ask them in this order, and the first one is the only one that matters this morning."

**"The request must show a current local act performed by the settlement, this season, at a place of their own choosing, recorded, and not an act claimed for them from two miles away. Show me the act."**

Mavis Rudge stood up at about a quarter past ten and she was fifty-four and she had walked from the flats and she had a stick she did not need and she did not sit down again for about an hour and a half.

"Warden's got nothing to do with us," she said. "We had a count in the year the sheet says and I did not go to it and I am not going to tell this room that I did not go to it, because I did not go to it and there is a reason and the reason is that the last time anybody came up the flats with a stone in a case they asked me four questions and I answered three of them wrong on purpose and one of them right and they wrote down the one that was right."

There was a noise in the room that was not a noise.

"And I have wanted a document my whole life," said Mavis Rudge, "and I am not going to be a woman in a hall doing the thing where she tells you what she has been through. You have all come because it is on a door. I did not come because it is on a door. I came because a clerk in your district wrote my name on a piece of paper on the fourth of this month and I have never been on a piece of paper in thirty-one years and I would like to know what happens to it."

"That is fair," said the officer. "Show me the act."

---

The act was a pump rota.

That is the whole of it and it took the rest of the morning. **Dorn Lyle**, forty-eight, of the flats, came to the front on the second attempt and did not use the stick, and put a sheet of paper on the table which was a rota for a hand pump on the mud flats in eleven columns, one for each household, with the days down the side and the hours down the side after that, and the names in it in a good round hand that got no worse anywhere on the page, and the date at the top and the date at the bottom and a line under the bottom one.

"It goes up on the door at the pump on a Monday and it comes off on a Sunday," she said. "It is copied out on the Sunday night. It is copied out by me. There are eleven households on this and there are nine names on it because two of the households are a daughter's in Ashlocks and a brother's in Rush Halt and the brother's draws here and the daughter does not. It is done every week. It has been done every week since the Count was not taken."

"How many times have you written it out," said Owen Rusk.

"Eleven years," said Dorn Lyle. "And before me, my mother."

The officer read it. She read it the whole way down, twice, which nobody expected, and the second time she put her finger on the eleven columns and moved it along.

"**This is a good document,**" she said. "This is the best-produced document I have had in front of me in a review in twenty years and I am going to have to write four sentences about it and none of them will help you."

"Then write them anyway," said Mavis Rudge.

"The rota is a record of an act performed by a settlement," said the officer, "and the act is performed by one person."

"There are eleven households on it—"

"The act is performed by one person, at the pump, on a Sunday night, with the pump in front of her," said the officer, "and the office is asking the settlement to do a thing, and the settlement is a word that means eleven houses and there is no such word on the form, and when the form says *the settlement* it means the settlement's premises, and the settlement's premises are three, and a rota kept by one woman is not three premises, it is a woman, and I am not allowed to enter a woman. **If this office wanted a settlement it should have had a box for a settlement and it has not got one, and that is a fact about this office and I am entering it.**"

"You will enter that," said Mavis Rudge.

"I will enter that," said the officer, "and I have entered about four hundred findings in this county and I have entered that sentence four times and every one of the four was written by a man and not by a woman, and I have never had a woman in front of me holding the paper when I wrote it, and that is the whole of what is different about this morning."

---

The second question was what the reconciliation of Witherow rested upon, and the third was whether the paper it rested upon was current, and Owen Rusk answered both, and the answer to the third was that the paper was current and had a date on it and was nine years old, and the officer wrote that down without comment.

The fourth question was put to the room and not to anybody, at about half past eleven, and it was the question she had come for.

**"Is there anything in this county that says what a settlement is, other than a building?"**

Nobody answered for about four seconds. Owen Rusk said that there were two instruments and that neither of them answered it, and that a person could not consent while their facts were dissolving and that the Guard's answer was a person per person and a date, and that the answer was humane and it was correct and it had taken the water off a reach eleven days before a bank came down and that he was not attacking it and could not, because it was right and it had worked.

The officer wrote about ninety seconds and did not read it back.

Then she said: **"Who is taking the record."**

---

"I am," said Owen Rusk.

"You are the applicant."

"I am the applicant and I am the officer of the record of the district returns out of which Witherow is taken, and there is nobody else in this room who is paid to write."

"There is a clerk of this district office behind me," said the officer, "who brought the file up and who has not said anything for two hours."

Junia Pell came up the room. She was twenty-two, she had been in the building three months and four days, and she was the fourth name on a roll of nineteen that had had no vacancy since the spring, and she said the whole of it in one sentence and did not stop in the middle of it.

"**I would be the hand that a kitchen is a room,**" she said, "and in four years somebody in Concord Hall will want to know how many hearings were heard in places that were not rooms, and there will be one name on the answer, and it will be mine, and I will be twenty-six and I will be the reason, and I have been the reason once in this building already and it cost a man a culvert."

"That is four sentences," said Owen Rusk.

"It is one," said Junia Pell, "and you have made it longer by counting, and you have been doing that since the fourth of this month." She put the file down on the table, squared, and stepped back. "Also you would be writing a finding about a settlement of eleven households in a room that is not a room, and the finding is going into a file in Concord Hall with both your names on it, and in about four years a person is going to read it and see a Registry hand and a kitchen and they are going to be the same fact. There is nobody in this county who can make those two facts different. I can decline to be the hand. I cannot make it different."

The officer looked at her for a moment.

"Declined, and thanked, and I am not going to pretend I would not have said yes," she said. "Sit down, please, both of you, and the applicant will write it, and I am going to tell him now the one thing that will make it worse, because you should both have it in advance and I have never given anybody anything in advance in my life."

---

"Then write it," said Mavis Rudge.

"**I cannot write it in the hand of a person of Witherow,**" said the officer, "and I am telling you that before you ask, because you have been standing there for an hour and a half deciding how to ask it and I have watched you do it."

"Then I have been standing here for an hour and a half for nothing."

"You have been standing there for an hour and a half for the first time in thirty-one years," said the officer, "and if I could do it I would do it in a minute, and I cannot, and here is the reason and I would rather you had it from me than worked it out at four in the morning for the rest of your life. **A hand is a person. A person can be found.** The day a document is in a hand, that hand is somewhere, and in nine years there is a file with a name at the top of it and a settlement of three premises under it, and the name at the top is the name of the person who wrote it, and every person in this room knows what happens to a name at the top of a file in this county."

"I know what happens to it," said Mavis Rudge. "I have been on one. Thirty-one years ago and it is still there and it is not about a pump."

"Then you know more about it than anybody in this building," said the officer, "and I am sorry, and it does not alter the hand."

Mavis Rudge stood there for about four seconds and then she said, to Owen Rusk, in the flat voice of a person instructing a clerk:

"**Write it in yours, then, and write it in a good hand, and do not hurry, and read it to me before you sign it and do not change anything I have not heard.**"

And then she sat down, which she had not done for two hours, and put the stick across her knees.

---

He wrote it. It is four lines and it took him about nine minutes and it is in the hand of the officer of the record of the district returns of Docketford district because there is no other hand in the county that is entitled to write it.

**REVIEW REQUESTED. HEARD. RECONCILIATION SUSTAINED. NO CURRENT LOCAL ACT SHOWN.**

**MATERIAL PRODUCED BY THE SETTLEMENT: one sheet, a pump rota for the mud flats at Witherow, in the hand of one person, kept weekly, eleven years and the eleven years before it. The office has no column for a settlement. That is a fact about this office and not about Witherow.**

**THE APPLICANT AND THE REQUESTER ARE NAMED ON THE REQUEST AND ON THIS FILE. There are two names and neither of them may be removed by an officer.**

He read all four lines out loud and Mavis Rudge made him change nothing, and she said so afterward, in front of forty people, twice.

At about twenty past twelve the officer signed it with a plain stone with a Registry punch on the side of it, which is not a seal, and the finding went into a file in Concord Hall, and **the file says, in a hand at a counter, in two names, that a settlement of eleven households at Witherow asked this county for something and the answer was no.**

That is what Mavis Rudge asked for. She got it, in a kitchen, in front of forty people, after thirty-one years, and she did not enjoy one minute of it, and she did not say so, and nobody asked her, and she went out into the Mill Ward at about twenty past twelve and stood in the road for about two minutes with her stick in her hand and then went to find the pump and tell the rota was copied on Sunday as usual.

---

**The two from Ashlocks spoke at about ten past one, at the door, on their way out, and neither of them is the man who apologised in writing.**

"We were told to come and say nothing," said the sister.

"You are not here as anybody," said Owen Rusk. "You are not on a file and nothing you say is being taken down and there is no note of this in any book I have ever kept."

"We know," said the wife. "We came to see whether there was a room."

"There is not," said Nell Arbour.

"Thank you," said the sister, and they went out, and the door shut behind them, and about nine people in that kitchen had heard every word of it.

---

They did it on the yard stones at about half past one, in the cold, for about twenty minutes, and neither of them said one word about the difference between them, and neither of them has ever, and there is not going to be.

"You had it in your mouth," said Nell Arbour. "Nine times this morning. Not the words. The thing underneath them. Eleven settlements, a column, a book nobody opened, a man in October, a draw. You had it and you did not put it in a room."

"If I put it in a room this morning it becomes a document about the count," said Owen Rusk, "and it goes to Concord Hall, and a man with a folder answers it once, and it is answered, and then in nine years there is a file about the count and I am a name on it and nobody is ever able to say that again in a kitchen about a village. **The room is the only place any of it gets written. That is what a room is.**"

"The kitchen is the only place any of it gets decided," said Nell Arbour. "That is what a kitchen is, and I have been in this one for six years and I have never once seen a decision made in a room. You find the person in a kitchen. You find her in the yard, in a queue, in a doorway, in a field behind the tanneries. And you get her to say the thing out loud in front of her own people, and then you carry it to a room yourself, on your own legs, and by the time it gets there it is a piece of paper and the person is not in it. **You had forty people in this room this morning who have just watched a hand not be able to do a thing. That is the raw material. You let it go to Concord Hall on its own and in nine years it is four words on a sheet with two names under it.**"

"I have looked," said Owen Rusk. "I looked this morning for about an hour before they came. I have a card that lets me do two things. I can put a figure in a box and I can ask whether a paper is current. There is no box in this county for what you have just watched happen and there is no clause that lets me ask whether a settlement is a settlement. **Everything you want me to do in a room is a thing my card does not do, and I have been carrying it since the fourth of this month and I know exactly what it is.**"

"I know you know."

"Then what do you want."

"I want you to know the difference between the two," said Nell Arbour, "because you have spent a month finding out that you cannot put a figure in a box, and you have made a religion out of the box, and the box is where the people are not. That is all. I am not asking you to say it. I am telling you that you did not say it and that I noticed and that I will go on noticing."

"Then we do not agree."

"No," said Nell Arbour. "We have not agreed about anything all month and I have not missed it once."

---

He came up Fetter Lane at about ten past two on the Wednesday and did the tray and was on it at a quarter past six, and on the Thursday morning of the second month of the Concord Year 488 he sat at the end of the long table in a ward hall with the stove on and about twenty people in it and heard Marguerite Bray rule three things into the delivery book, which is the most she has ever ruled in one sitting and two of them were about a pump rota in another village.

Then Ada Rusk, from the wall, without turning round, in the voice she uses for a roster:

"**The fourth week was done on Monday, and this is the third morning of the fifth, and the sixth is printed on the same sheet behind it, and there is no form in this county on which anybody may move a name from one week to another, and the Warden may return a schedule and may not amend it, and there is no seventh week printed anywhere, and I am not going to stand in this hall and ask her whether she will write one.** So there are two weeks left on that sheet and neither of them is ours, and there is no day this month and no week after it that anybody has given us, and I want that read into the book tonight and not on the day, and I want it read as it is, which is that a ward asked for a week in a printed column and got one, and spent it in a room at the Glass Stair about a correction from the year four hundred and eighty-four, and there is nobody in this hall who did that and there is nobody in this hall who could have stopped it, and I am not going to stand here and pretend that either of those is a comfort."

Nobody said anything.

"Also," said Ada Rusk, "the granary was counted on the Sunday of the twenty-seventh and there were ten days in it, and there were nine yesterday, and today is the first of the month and there are six, and on the seventh of next month there is nothing, and I have not told anybody in this hall to go and buy anything and nobody is to go and buy anything."

At about four in the afternoon of the same day Owen Rusk was at the counter of the count room at the Glass Stair, in a public room anybody may go into, where a woman who keeps the board had come in out of the weather with a scraper in her hand, and he asked her the question he had been carrying up a hill for a week.

"There is no column on that board of anything a person drinks," he said. "**Do you know where the water figures come from.**"

She put the scraper down on the counter.

"**Two floors up in another building, and it is not this office,**" she said, "and you have asked me that in a field and I have watched you not ask it in a room for a month. **I do not know who fills in the third column and I have been asked that twice in nine years and I have never found out, and it is not me, and it is not the count, and that is the whole of what I have got.** Well. Come Friday, because they shut at three and he is the only one who is there in the morning, and take a pencil, because he will not give you the sheet and you are entitled to the sheet and he is not entitled to keep it, and he does not know that, and nobody has ever told him, because in nine years nobody has ever come."

He was on the boat at eight on the Friday morning with a pencil in his coat and no paper, and the sun was coming up over the flat above the weirs, and the granary had five days in it, and there was a printed sheet in a field behind the tanneries with a fourth column in it that nobody in this county had ever read, and there was nothing on it anywhere of anything a person drinks, and the man who fills in the figures that are not on it went up at eight o'clock every morning and had done for twenty-two years and had never once been asked why.
