# Chapter 50 — One Page, and a Drawer

The rain season ended on the Wednesday of the sixty-fifth day of the month, or would have, and by the Monday of the seventieth there had been five days of it in which nothing came out of the sky, and the water at the top step had gone from two feet to a foot and a half to a foot to about nine inches and had stopped, and the weir path was out and firm and the bar was not in it and the channelling bar was found on the Wednesday afternoon a hundred yards below the second bend by two people who are not in this case and who have never been asked about anything.

The posting came down on the Friday morning and it was in an envelope with no letter in it, because postings in this building do not have letters in them.

*O. Rusk, copy clerk, River Desk. To the Fourth Desk, unfiled correspondence tray, and to the record of the district returns. The tray is a posting and not a qualification and does not travel with him. The card of the tray is unchanged.*

That is a lateral move. Owen Rusk read it twice on the Friday and understood it completely on the first pass and did not enjoy a second of it, and the reason he did not enjoy it is that a man who has spent seven weeks holding a list of five unresolved things in a district of two hundred and forty people has been put back on a tray of correspondence that cannot be routed, in a building where he is a stranger, with a card that still has four lines on it that he may not sign.

He had not asked for any of it. That was the part he found hardest. Nobody had done him a favour and nobody had given him a step and there was no meeting at which it had been decided, and the envelope had come down from a floor above his own on a Friday morning the way postings do.

And the work itself is better. He should be honest about that in the same sentence. The tray of unfiled correspondence is a job with an end to it, and he knows how to do it, and it does not require him to be anything, and on the Monday morning there were two hundred and forty sheets on it and he did a hundred and ninety of them before lunch and the hundred and ninety were correct.

---

He came down to the ground floor at about twenty past one in the afternoon and asked for the file, and the file came up out of the district office's own drawer in about four minutes, and it came up with an officer's hand on a slip that said it was produced to a named officer of the record and not to a party, and the officer was named as a copy clerk of the River Desk of the district named in it, which was this district, and which is not a thing anybody in that building has ever been in a position to be before.

"One page," he said, when it was on the table. "Is it one page."

"The return is one page. The schedule of performed acts is behind it and that is four weeks. The four pages are behind that. The list is at the back of the certification and that is a separate instrument with a separate number and it is not attached to this." He turned the file round. "Mr Rusk, I am going to say a thing to you as a body with hands and not as a favour, and then I am going to go back to my tray. You are on the file. You are on the second page of the certification and you are in the third box of the third page in your own hand, and nobody in this office has said one word about that, and nobody is going to, because a person who is of a district may certify and may not examine, and it is on the form, and that is the whole of the arrangement.

"You are not on the return. I have looked. A person who is a named officer of the record of an instrument is not a witness to it and does not become one by asking."

"Understood," said Owen Rusk.

And the clerk went away, and Owen Rusk stood at a table on the ground floor of the River Desk with District Return 487-LL in front of him, one page, and read it.

---

**The scope**, at the top, in a bridge mechanic's capitals, four words, and he read them and did not have anything to say about them and had said almost nothing about them for five days:

**Duty, and no status.**

And under it, printed, in the small print that this building has taught him to read before anything else: *This return claims no right, and gives no warranty, as to the safety, the condition or the use of the Ninth Weir public crossing. No witness in it is competent to speak to any of them, and none has been asked.*

Then the four duties, held by six named persons, one to a name, each of them a thing one person can do alone on a Tuesday in February: the marker, the channel report, the count, the reading, the bins, the copy.

Then the six names.

Then the term, and the review, which is **every seven days from the day of the seal and not before**, and the thing a hold cannot ride on, which is the interval.

Then the objection class: *the eight persons whose branches are in this return, and any person the district office names in writing.* Eight. It has been eight since the twenty-fourth of the month and it will be eight on Thursday, and no instrument in this city has ever named anybody in writing, and the ward has not asked.

Then the two observers, in their own hands, and a shelf of written statements about their own interest in the file behind them, most of them requested by this office, one of them written against herself before anybody asked her, and a line in the margin in a reviewing officer's hand that says *a separation made once does not carry forward, and a separation made once has never yet carried forward in this district.*

Then the appeal route, which is a place and a name and a standing, and the standing is fifteen words, and the name is Hester Lund of Coal Hill, nineteen years on that table, and it is on the first page of a return in a public file and it is the only name in this case on a first page that is not the name of somebody in Lower Lock, and it got there because a woman who keeps a tide table was asked in the right words by a person of a ward on the ward's own paper out of the ward's own bag from the hall door, and not out of a coat.

Then the withdrawal right, in capitals:

**A PERSON MAY TAKE THEIR OWN BRANCH OUT OF THIS RETURN AND THE REST OF IT DOES NOT FALL OVER.**

And then, at the foot, the witness schedule, which is a sheet, and which is the only part of the instrument he had never seen in his life.

---

It is a single sheet and it has a column of names and a column of what each person is on the return as, and the column of what each person is there as is the whole document's conscience.

He did the arithmetic twice, standing at a table on the ground floor of a building in which he is a stranger, with a pencil he was not supposed to use on that floor, and it came out the same both times.

**Eight branches.** Nine adults consented in the hall on the seventh day of the month and one of them — Nessa Kray, seventy-one, in her own words, standing up, unasked — withdrew on the twenty-third, and the return narrowed by one page and stood. Eight adults, one to a page, in eight hands, no shared line anywhere, no proxy clause, no witness line a householder could fill. He read four of the eight names and did not read the other four, because there are eight pages and he is a named officer of the record of an instrument he did not draft, and a page is a page.

**Two observers.** Junia Pell, of this building, and Wend Voss, of the Mill Ward annex, which is the district that received the receipt that took the crossing off this ward in the first place. Between them a shelf of written statements about their own interest, in their own hands, one of them written against herself before anybody asked her, and a line in a reviewing officer's margin that says a separation made once does not carry forward.

**Four named objectors**, holding their own papers: **A. Rusk, B. Tillet, H. Ferris, W. Sarr.** Not branches. Not parties. Four adults who voted no on the seventh day of the month and are named in the ward's own book as having been wrong, in two hands, and who were offered a separate branch and did not get one, and one of whom has written a refusal on his own page in a cook's hand and one of whom has written a duty and one of whom has written a sentence that is the opposite of a pardon and one of whom has written nothing and said out loud why.

**Six persons whose acts are recorded and who are not branches.** Bess Crale. Tam Ockey. Enid Salter. Ruth Mapp. Sully Dace. Wil Pomfret. Six ordinary people of a ward of two hundred and forty, on a public file, one duty each, because a district that had given up a crew and put in six names had understood that a crew is a word and a person is a person.

**One steward.** Marguerite Bray, sixty-one, who has ruled the second column of a delivery book for thirty-six years in a book that has been in her family for fifty-one, and who read four weeks of hours out of it in a long room in a city two miles and a river away, in the ordinary voice, including the standpipe rota, and who said the number and not the answer to six people in a hall, one at a time, and was not answered once.

Eight. Two. Four. Six. One.

Twenty-one living witnesses, and not one of them is Owen Rusk.

He stood at a table and looked at the three names that people would look for and that are not on it, and each of them is not on it for a reason that is on the same file.

**Cobb Rennie, fifty-eight, of two rooms in this district.** His hours came off the return on the sixtieth day of the month because a district that claims a duty it cannot afford to keep has a duty taken from it in about four minutes by a careful man in a room, and the ward chose the night hours itself rather than have them taken. They are on the roster. They are at the weir path, on a board in a tin under two nails, and any person in this county may read it on any day without notice and take a copy away. And there is no column in this city for nineteen years at an address a man can point at, and his pension is not cured and was not cured and is not going to be, and naming him again is worth less than money and more than nothing.

**Ivy Sarr, twelve, of this district.** On a standpipe rota at four in the morning in the dark. In a schedule of performed acts, at a counter, readable, copyable. Three of the five required parts of a protective return exist, and the two that do not are Registry functions, and the word is not written beside any of the four people in that ward who could be asked, and the number five hours a week is on a page in a ward hall in a steward's hand with the day and the hour under it, and that is a number on a page and not a custodian and not a protected child and not an entry.

**Hester Lund, of Coal Hill.** Not on it, by the seventh of seven conditions she gave on a wet landing, nineteen years after she started keeping that table, and the seventh is this: *if my name goes on a page in a return, the standing ends that day, and nobody asks me why, and I do not say that word to either of you again.* She is at the far end of the route and she is not on the instrument, and she stood in a room in Docketford on the fifty-ninth day of the month and said out loud, in front of strangers, that the ward's schedule did not claim a seed stall and that a woman of seventy-one had lost one and that she could not speak for her and the ward was right.

Three names that people will look for, and three absences, and every one of them costs somebody something and not one of them is a mistake.

---

He read the four pages behind the schedule and they were the four pages.

One carries a refusal in the hand of a man of forty-six who is a ferry cook at Wexford Landing, with the reason and the date, and the reason is that being read by somebody who can stop is not the same as being read by somebody who can stay.

One carries a duty, in the hand of a man of fifty-eight who keeps the night watch: the copy, at the weir path, on the day the count is written up, hung where anybody coming down can read it, and brought in again in the wet, with the sentence *I will complain in pencil in Mrs Bray's book if it cannot be done.*

One carries four sentences in the capitals of a woman of twenty-four, which are the second sentence of which is the whole of what a separate branch is not.

And one is blank, and it has been sent blank five times, and the woman whose name is at the top of it has said out loud in a hall that she would rather hand them a page with her name on it and nothing on it than a page with her name on it saying nothing, and nobody has chased her, and nobody is going to, and a blank page that is sent on purpose is not a hole.

It is a page.

---

Owen Rusk stood at a table on the ground floor of the River Desk at about twenty past one in the afternoon on the seventieth day of the month of the last week of a rain season that has ended, holding the thing he had spent seven weeks and a flood and a hearing and a Saturday morning with a bucket chain for, and the entire content of it was a page with four words at the top of it.

And he worked out, standing up, at about ten past two, that the volume of his life had not been about the page, and that it had been about a sentence he had been living inside since the fourth week of the month without knowing that it was the sentence.

*A man who keeps a thing alive by holding on to it has no business telling a ward what it may perform.*

A woman of twenty-four said that to him in a hall in front of twenty-two people, in the fourth week of the month, on a Thursday, and he had not defended himself, and he had gone on holding the file, and every single thing that had happened since had been the sentence working.

The ward had been saved by becoming legally responsible for itself, and it had become legally responsible for itself by taking things off, and the things it took off were its own hours and a bridge and a man of fifty-eight and a girl of twelve and its own best act.

The people who did the work were never the people he had been carrying. That was the second half of it and it was Ada Rusk's half and she had given it to him in a shed at the top of Fetter Lane in the first week with a bucket in her hands, and he had taken seven weeks to understand that a copy clerk of the River Desk is not a person anybody in Lower Lock is waiting for at four in the morning, and that the only office anybody in that ward ever needed him to fill was the one where he stands in a hall and reads out what a checker wrote, and lets somebody stop him.

What he had wanted was to be the person who carried it. And the whole of what he had instead was a tray of correspondence that cannot be routed, in a building where he is a stranger, with a card with four lines on it he may not sign, and a certification with about an hour left on it, and a list of five lines he has to begin again tomorrow.

And the reason that is not a worse life than the one he had at the start of the month is that the one at the start of the month had been a man who believed that if he carried the case correctly nobody else could harm it, and the one at the end of it is a man who has found out that the case is a page with four words on it that a woman of twenty-four wrote, and that his whole function is to be answerable for the five things underneath it and to say them, in order, out loud, in a room, without a sheet, and to come back the next morning and say them again.

---

Nothing underneath any of it had been restored. He went and checked, because he always checks, because it is the only professional habit he has that was ever any use to anybody.

The Fetter Lane pump grant is suspended and is not restored and its own review is not named, and the four hundred pounds a year that the Registry's own precedent once entered against a named work in the same quarter, in writing, on the same day, on a published schedule, is not in this file and is not going to be. The pump still runs by hand at four in the morning on the worst nights, and it took on the first stroke on Thursday, and it will not always.

The crossing is closed to loads. It was closed to loads on the first day of this month of rain and it is closed to loads now, and the load-restriction suspension of the tenth stands and has not been amended, and the crack in the abutment has moved and nobody in this city has a duty to look at it this month.

The repair is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and unfunded, and the notice nailed to a post at the top of the weir path says that any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it, and the path is out in the weather now and four people a day can read it.

The crew's wage is four months in arrears and is a written condition of the ward's own roster in two hands and is not funded anywhere in this volume.

The sixteen are sixteen. Stability Order 114 of the twenty-fourth day of this month is on a public docket, is in public appeal, is not withdrawn, is not a finding upon the safety, condition or use of any work, and one of the sixteen is still suspended and one of the sixteen is still Lower Lock, and the Deputy Registrar who signed it is alive and unpromoted and is this morning the first name in a register of contested closures that he asked for himself in a room of thirty people, and that register is printed once a year and goes to the Council's counter.

The Office's answer about the forty-one is on a table by a door in a public hall, two hundred and twenty-four words, five clauses and a courtesy, and it is a form, and it is correct, and it is still not an answer, and it will be the answer in the next quarter, and the practice is visible and is not stopped.

Six children and non-responders have three of five parts and two of them are functions this city has never paid for. Four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds. Four months of a wage. A receipt promised on the nineteenth of the month, on a day to be named and an hour to be named, that was never given, and that is standing on a spike on the ground floor of this building with a heading on it.

And the name of a man of fifty-one who carries bundles up a stair and puts them on a ledge and says nothing, who has done it for ten years from the water gate at the upriver end, whose name Owen Rusk copied about four times a week for six years out of the third column of a weekly post sheet, and who went on the sixty-fifth day of this month, and who is not VANE, H., and is not anybody, and is not written in his register, and is not on this file, and is not to be told.

---

At about a quarter to three in the afternoon he took his coat off the back of the second desk, because he had been posted back to the Fourth Desk and the Fourth Desk is a place and a man is posted to it, and the coat goes on the back of the chair at the end of the working day, and it had been on the back of a chair in a corridor for eight weeks and it was going to be on the back of a chair at a desk for the rest of his working life and that is the entire promotion he has ever had.

Then he went down the stairs to the ground floor, because there is a thing at the bottom of those stairs that he has stood in front of eight times in seven weeks and has never opened, and there is no reason on the twenty-seventh day after a flood season to open it and there never has been.

The tray of unfiled correspondence is on the ground floor under the slope of the glass, and beside it, in the same run of drawers, there is a drawer with **black braided cord** round the handle, and the cord is a notice and a legal status and not a tie, and there is a brass tab on the face of the drawer with a number and a ward's name and a year cut into it, and behind the tab, in the frame of the drawer, an index card, and on the index card, in a hand that wanted whoever came after to understand that it was a decision and not a failure of filing, five words.

He put his hand on the cord.

Not to open it. He put his hand on it, flat, the way you put your hand on a door of a house you have decided not to go into, and he stood there in a ground floor corridor of the Registry of Continuant Works at about a quarter to three in the afternoon on the seventieth day of the Concord Year 487, and he did not turn the card over, and he did not take the cord off the handle, and he did not ask anybody in that building for permission and he was never going to.

**ALDER ROW — 461.**

A ward of Docketford closed in the Concord Year 461, twenty-six years before this month, four streets from where he sleeps. Intake 486/F-B/10, in the same series as the transfer's own originating entry, received for intake as a number in a column, in the same unbroken column, four weeks after the transfer, and never examined, and the card behind the tab says never opened, and it is the oldest thing in this case and the only thing in it that he has not been able to put into a document and has not wanted to.

He has stood in front of it eight times in seven weeks. It is four feet from where he sleeps. It is a filing and not a mystery and he wrote that in his register a month ago and underlined it once and meant it.

And he is the only person in this city who is in a position to find out what is in it, and he has told nobody in Docketford that he has read a line about it, and there is a woman of sixty-one in a ward hall who has a delivery book with a bad slow hand in the inward column for the seventeenth day of a month in the intake year, and a man of about fifty who walked two miles in the wet to a door and asked two women to sign that they had seen him and was refused, correctly, and went two miles in the wet the other way and put it on a table that receives.

It is a drawer, and it is a filing, and the difference is a cord and a card and a decision somebody made in a corridor in a year nobody wrote down.

He stood there with his hand on the cord for about a minute, and he knew exactly what it was, and he did not know what was in it, and he was not going to open it, and that was a choice and not an omission, and he had made it seven times.

The cord was cold. The brass was cold. The card behind it said five words in a flat hand and there was nothing behind the card he could hear.

Above him on the second floor a press went, and somewhere in the building a clerk said a number out loud to a person at a counter, and the building went on with two hundred sheets of correspondence that cannot be routed, and the season had ended, and the granary was at a fortnight and a bit and the writing-up would be on Thursday afternoon, and on Thursday morning at seven forty there would be three people on a path above a second bend putting a marker in the rain for the eighth week running.

Owen Rusk took his hand off the cord, and went back up the stairs, and sat down at a desk he no longer wanted, at about ten minutes to three, with about an hour left on a certification and a list of five lines and a tray, and did not write the name of a man of fifty-one down anywhere at all.
