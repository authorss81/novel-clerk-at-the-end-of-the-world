# Chapter 47 — One Active Hold

What a certification does to a working life is that it stops being general.

That is the whole of the finding and it took four days to arrive at it and about nine minutes to understand, and Owen Rusk understood it on the Tuesday morning of the sixty-fourth day of the month, standing at the end of the second desk at about ten past eight in the morning with a tray in front of him and a sheet clipped to the back of it that he had not clipped there himself.

A copy clerk holds things in general. That is what a copying floor is: a man at a desk who is handed a thing and is trusted with it in the abstract, and the abstraction is a professional skill, and it is why a building full of clerks can hold a district's entire record in a room with a leaking roof without anybody noticing that anything in it has changed.

He could not do that any more, and the reason he could not do it was on the second page of a form in a file and had been on the second page of a form in a file since Saturday afternoon: *the person making this certification carries the part that is not resolved, and the part that is not resolved is a list.*

And the list was five lines long, and it had been five lines long since Saturday, and on Tuesday morning it came into the copying floor in the most ordinary way in the world, which is that it came in through the ordinary work.

---

The first one came at about ten past nine, from the countersignature desk, and it came in the shape of a question about a man's hours.

A quarter notice had come up from a sub-table for a district at Fetter Bend and one of the household references was a place and not a house, which is the seventh of Marguerite Bray's seven, and the household reference at 4 Lantern Walk is the eighth and is nobody's. The notice was addressed to three letters and a surname and it had come back twice, *not known at the address*, and it was on the top of the stack in the tray of unrouteable correspondence, where it had been since the third week of the month, and where Owen Rusk had told two people in two rooms that it was not his business and that it was not Mrs Bray's business, and had been right both times.

He picked it up on the Tuesday morning because his hand was on the tray, and he read the address on it, and he did not get a name, because there is no first name for the man in any register in this city, because for nine years the city has written to a surname and an initial, and because that is not a mystery and has never been a mystery and is not going to be one.

And the first line of the list came into his head at that moment, entire, with a smell in it.

*Cobb Rennie, of two rooms in this district, who is of no roll in this city and has been for two years, and whom nothing in this matter reaches.*

The smell is oiled cloth. That is the whole of it. It is not a metaphor and it is not clever. Cobb Rennie keeps an oiled coat on a hook behind his door because he has walked a child of twelve home every night in the dark for four months and a coat that has been in that river basin four times a week does not last a season unless it is oiled, and the smell of it is in the room at the back of the ward hall on a Tuesday evening, and it is in Owen Rusk's head at ten past nine on a Tuesday morning in a building four hundred yards from that hall, and it is the smell of a man who is owed a pension and who is not going to get it and who is on nobody's list but this one.

The detail does not aggregate. That is what he had not understood on Saturday.

A list of five is a thing a man can hold. Five is a number and a number is a thing you can put in a box and look at on a bad day. But the moment the person holding it starts doing the ordinary work of his ordinary day, the five stops being five and becomes five *particulars*, and a particular is not a number, and a particular has a smell and a weight and a sound and does not add to anything.

Cobb Rennie is not one of five things. Cobb Rennie is a coat on a hook and a man on a standpipe rota at four in the morning and a sentence in his own voice from about a month ago, that he had never been told by anybody in this city that the thing he was doing every night had a name, and that he did not want it named.

Owen Rusk stood at the end of the second desk with a returned notice in his hand and understood that he was not going to be able to put it down, and that this was going to happen about forty times a day, and that there was nothing at all unusual about it, and that this was the ordinary condition of every man and woman in this ward who had a duty.

---

The second one came at about half past ten and it came as a weight.

It is the **roster board**. The ward keeps a copy of the roster at the weir path, hung on two nails under a tin, and Wil Pomfret is the person who carries it up and hangs it and brings it in again in the wet, and it is one of the six duties on the return now, and one of the six names on the instrument sealed on Saturday, and Owen Rusk has never seen the board.

It weighs nine pounds dry.

Wet, it weighs about thirteen, and it is a thing you cannot carry one-handed in a crosswind at the top of a path above a river, and a ward knows the weight of its own board to about a pound, and the number is not in any instrument and is not going to be.

And a wet board is where Ivy Sarr would be, if she were a branch, if there were a protective return, if there were a custodian and five hours a week from a woman of forty-four who is owed nothing for it and who has a laundry, and if the office had ever in nine hundred years paid anybody to keep a thing for a child who cannot answer for herself.

*Ivy Sarr is not a branch of this. She is on a rota. She is on a roster. She is in a schedule of performed acts that anybody in this county may read at the weir path on any day without notice and take a copy away.*

Three of the five required parts exist. Two of them are Registry functions and this city has never paid for either of them. The word *custodian* is not written beside any of the four people in that ward who could be asked, and it is not going to be written beside any of them, and the number five hours a week is on a page in a ward hall in a steward's ruled hand with the day and the hour under it, and it is a number on a page and not a gap, and a number on a page in a ward hall two miles upriver does not put anybody in a protected return.

Owen Rusk stood at the end of the second desk at about half past ten on the Tuesday morning and understood that **being legible is not the same as being owed anything**, and that a woman of sixty-one had said a version of it in a hall on Monday with a stove going, and that he had heard it, and that hearing it was not the same as carrying it, and that carrying it was what he had signed for on Saturday and what he was going to be doing at about this time every morning for as long as anybody in this city cared to let him.

---

The third came at about one in the afternoon, and it came in a voice.

Not a voice in the room. A voice in his head, in the exact pitch of a woman who sells nothing in particular and is used to being heard over a road, from a wall by a door in a ward hall, on the Friday evening of the sixtieth day of the month.

*"Because the page is for a thing I would do, and I have not found the thing yet, and I would rather hand you a page with my name on it and nothing on it than a page with my name on it saying nothing."*

And behind her voice, immediately, with no gap at all, the sound of a room after somebody has said a thing that cannot be improved: about twenty-six people not saying anything, and a stove ticking, and a man of forty-six getting up out of a chair to go and look at a bin.

Because a page with a name on it and nothing on it is not a hole. It is a page. And a page with a name on it and something on it that does not get done is worse, and everybody in that hall knew it, and one woman said so out loud and went home, and two pages are still untouched because two people have not written on them, and nobody has chased them, and the bundle went to a Council in Concord Hall on Saturday morning for the fourth time with the same four pages in the same order.

---

The fourth came at about four in the afternoon and it came as a number, and it was the only one of the five that came as a number, and he wrote it down on the back of a sheet of copying paper because he could not help it.

**Four thousand one hundred and sixty.**

It is an estimate. It was made in the spring by an engineer behind an office, and it is a good estimate, and it has not moved, and no instrument in this city is going to move it, and there is a notice nailed to a post at the top of a path that says any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it, and the path was under a foot and a half of water on Saturday morning, and on the Tuesday morning the path was out and the notice was legible to about four people a day.

Four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds, and the wage is four months in arrears, and the crew is nine, and the granary came out at a fortnight on Monday, and the pump grant is suspended and will be suspended in a month, and the crossing is closed to loads and is going to be closed to loads in the first week of the coming month when somebody looks at the crack again, and the office has not disputed any of it and has not been asked to.

He wrote the number on the back of a sheet and then, about four seconds later, did the thing he has been not doing for seven weeks and did anyway, and picked up a pencil and put the other four in a column under it, and then stopped with the pencil on the paper.

Because a column of five numbers is a list, and a list of five is a thing a man can hold, and he had signed on Saturday for the part that is not a list.

He put the pencil down and put the sheet in the tray drawer and did not write it in the register, and the reason he did not write it in the register is that a register is a thing a man leaves the building with, and this is a thing he leaves the building with every morning whether he writes it down or not.

---

The fifth one came on the Wednesday morning, and it came as nothing at all, which was how he understood it.

It is a receipt.

The answer of the nineteenth of the month gave the district a receipt — of the steward, of the witnesses, of such persons as the district might name — at the district office, on a day to be named, at an hour to be named. The docket has since given a hearing, at the Glass Stair, in a long room, at ten in the morning, on the fifty-ninth, and a hearing is not a receipt, and the two documents have been apart since the nineteenth day of the month, and a clerk is not permitted to join them.

So on the Wednesday morning of the sixty-fifth day of the month, Owen Rusk walked into a room in a building he works in, in the ordinary way, on the ordinary work, and there was a receipt request form on a spike by the countersignature desk, and it had been on the spike since the nineteenth, and nobody had taken it, because the district office had not offered a day and the ward had gone and got a hearing instead, and a hearing does not discharge a receipt.

He read the heading on it. *Request for a receipt, in petition 487-GS-19, of the steward and the witnesses and of such persons as the petitioner names.*

He put it back on the spike.

That is the entire event of the Wednesday morning, and it took four seconds, and there is no instrument in this city that records that a person stood in a room and read a heading and put a piece of paper back on a spike, and the list on the second page of a form in a file has five lines on it and the fifth one is that, and it is the only one of the five that is not anybody's fault and not anybody's loss and not anybody's fault at all, it is a piece of administration that nobody has performed in six weeks because nobody has been asked, and the person it is owed to is a woman of sixty-one who was told in a corridor on the Friday that she would be asked about it in about four weeks, and she has not been asked.

---

And on the Wednesday afternoon, at about ten past three, on the ground floor of the River Desk, in the tray of unrouteable correspondence, on a weekly post sheet that he had been copying for the second desk since the second week of a year six years ago, the third column said a name and the name did not come.

Owen Rusk had a bundle in his left arm. He was going up the stair with it, because the ward's schedule goes up on a Wednesday and has gone up three times now and he carries it because carrying it is the job.

The post sheet is a weekly thing. It has four columns: the day, the bundles, the number of pounds, and the name of the river-post hand who brought them up from the water gate. It has a line for every delivery in a week. Owen Rusk has copied those sheets for six years, out of a tray, into a big book, four lines at a time, and he has done it at the rate of about a hundred and forty weeks, and there are about two hundred and eighty lines in the big book with a name in the third column of each one that is the same name.

**Hiram Cray.**

Fifty-one, of the water gate, upriver end, thirty years in the post and ten of them on this gate, who carries a bundle up a stair and puts it on a ledge and says nothing, and whose whole job in this city is to be the last pair of hands between a piece of paper and a shelf.

It was in the third column on the Wednesday line, and Owen Rusk's hand went to the third column on the Wednesday line, and there was nothing there at all.

Not a noise. Not a light. Not a gap he could describe. The absence looked exactly like a line of a post sheet with a space in it where a name had not been written, and it looked like that to everybody who has ever copied a post sheet, which is to say it looked like a Tuesday in February when the water gate was iced and the hand stayed down.

He stood on the stair with a bundle in his arm for about four seconds.

Then he did the only thing there is to do, which is the thing he did on the Thursday of the fourth week of the month, and it is the thing he has never once done badly, and it is the reason it cannot be got round.

He put the bundle on the ledge, on the outside, on the flat, where it would be safe, and he took the sheet off the tray and turned it round so that the other way was up, and he read the number of pounds, which was there, and he read the day, which was there, and he read the second column, which was there, and he did not read the third column again.

Then he wrote it out.

He got it out of the big book. That is what the big book is for; that is the entire function of a register in this city and it is the reason nobody has ever been able to argue with him about the thing in the third column, because the string is gone out of his working life and the string was never anywhere except his working life.

**HIRAM CRAY. Fifty-one. Water gate, upriver end. Thirty years in the post, ten of them on that gate. Carries bundles up a stair, puts them on a ledge, says nothing.**

He read it twice. And then he read the sentence that describes the work, because the work is the part that is a fact and the name is the part that is a person, and he had been told on Saturday in a room with high windows that the part that is not resolved is a list, and a list of particulars does not aggregate, and a particular is a name and a piece of work.

Then he copied the post sheet into the big book in his own hand, all four columns, and the third column on the Wednesday line he copied as it stood, which is to say he copied the space.

And he did not write the name anywhere else, and he did not say it out loud, and he did not put it in his register, and he did not put it in a letter, and nobody in this city will ever be told.

---

He carried the bundle up the stair and put it on the ledge and said nothing, because that is a thing a man of fifty-one has been doing on that stair for ten years and it is the whole of what he is for.

Then he went back down and got the tray and copied the other two hundred and fifty sheets on it, and he was about forty minutes late getting home, and the reason he was forty minutes late is that he could not find the smell of oiled cloth anywhere in the building and he looked for it for about ten minutes in three rooms, and at about eight in the evening he got the ferry up the hill and stood in the lane.

"Two," he said, out loud, to a lane in the rain.

Because that was the number. He had lost one in the fourth week of the month, in a room of sixty people, and it had been the receiving officer's string on a covering slip and it had been a near miss, and then it had not been a near miss. And now there was a second one, and the second one was a man of fifty-one with thirty years in the post who carried bundles up a stair and said nothing, and whose name Owen Rusk had written in a big book about four times a week for six years and had last written out in full, in his own hand, into the air of a ground floor corridor at ten past three in the afternoon on the sixty-fifth day of the month of a rain season in the Concord Year 487.

And the finding, which was the same finding as the first one and which he had been told would be the same finding, was that neither of them had been a price anybody charged him.

VANE, H. had not been taken. There was no bill. There was no form with his name on it and no entry in a column and no seal and no instrument at all, and the second one was the same, and there is no office in this city that could produce a document saying that a copy clerk of the River Desk has been charged one name and permitted to keep another, and there is no register anywhere that has both of them in it, and there is nobody he could show it to.

What it actually is, he worked out in a lane, in about ninety seconds, standing up, in the wet, and it is the plainest thing he has thought in seven weeks and it is not a mystery and he was not going to tell anybody:

It is the ordinary Tuesday texture of a thing that cannot be put down.

A list of five particulars arrives, and the particulars do not aggregate, and you cannot hold them in general, so you hold them one at a time and each one is a smell or a weight or a pitch of voice or a number, and the day does not stop. The day is a day. There are two hundred and forty sheets on the tray. The press is cold. A copy clerk of the River Desk, of the district named in a return he certified on Saturday, holds one active preservation, and a preservation does not stop the ordinary work, and the ordinary work is the reason the person holding it is the person holding it.

And the thing he can least put down is a name.

He stood in the lane and let that sit for a while, and then he went up the hill to his own door and did not go into the ward hall, because the hall is shut on a Wednesday unless there is business, and there was no business.

On the Thursday morning of the sixty-sixth day of the month, at twenty minutes to six, the hall was open, and there were about nine people in it and one lamp going and no stove, and Ada Rusk was at the end of the long table with the roster on it, and everybody in that room was going to Docketford and the boat went at six.

"Before we go," she said. "The week, out loud, in this hall, in public, at twenty minutes to six in the morning, because Thursday is the day we do it and I am not going to let a boat be the reason we did not."

And she read it out, all of it, in the voice she uses for six in the evening at twenty minutes to six in the morning, including the standpipe rota and the four names on it and the man of fifty-eight, and at the end of it she said:

"And that is the week, and the pump did not take until the second stroke on Tuesday and Wil has it down, and the second bin is a quarter gone and Sully has written it in the pump book with the day on it, and the notice on the weir path is out in the weather now and anybody can read it, and Mrs Bray is going to rule that the four thousand one hundred and sixty is four thousand one hundred and sixty and has not moved and is not going to move in this month.

"And I would like everybody in this room to understand what this ward is now. It is five things. Four of them are on a piece of paper in a building in Docketford with my four words at the top of it, and the fifth one is a man of fifty-eight in an oiled coat who is owed something and is not going to get it, and there is nothing anybody in this hall can do about any of the five, and we are going to stand in a room at ten o'clock this morning and say what we did, and be told by an office that it is a form."
