# Chapter 70 — What the Ward Did With It

The granary at Lower Lock has been open and empty since the seventh day of the second month and nobody in the ward has bought a quarter of anything since, because Ada Rusk said so in a hall on the first day of the month in front of about twenty people and wrote it into the delivery book in her own capitals with the word **nobody** underlined, and the book is a thing a steward's clerk reads out on a Thursday.

It is the ninth day of the second month that the count is certified and the tenth is a Saturday, and at about eight in the morning a crew of nine is on the weir path with two buckets, a length of chain, a spade, and a bucket of tallow, and they are going to a public crossing to do three things that nobody has paid them for in four months and that they have done every week since before the flood, and the reason they are doing them this morning is not duty and not the return and not a form.

The reason is that the return says they do them, and the return is a thing that exists.

"There is no binding in it," Cobb Rennie said, at the top of the path, to nobody in particular, in the voice of a man who has said this at the top of this path for nineteen years. "Six renewals. For review. It has been for review since the March before the flood and there is not one word in it that binds anybody, including me, and I have read it four times this week because it is on the table in the hall and I am the sort of man who reads what is on the table."

"Renew the marker," said Wenna Sarr.

That is the whole of the exchange and it happens every Saturday and has happened since the March before the flood, and the crew are nine, and two of the nine are on a standpipe rota at four in the morning, and one of the two is a household with a girl of twelve in it, and the wage is four months in arrears and is written on a page of the delivery book in two hands as the condition of agreeing to be read in public, and is not funded anywhere in this city that anybody has found.

The crossing is closed to loaded traffic and to vehicles above four tons under the load-restriction suspension of the tenth day, and the suspension is untouched and is not challenged by the return and the return does not claim anything about the safety, condition or use of the crossing, which no witness in it is competent to speak to. The abutment is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and unfunded. The marker is a post with a board on it, and the board is repainted twice a year in white out of a tin that is not on a return and has not been on a return since the March before the flood, and in a ward where a girl of twelve does the shopping a tin of white is a decision.

The notice is still up at the weir path. It has been up since the flood week, in a frame, under a piece of slate, and it says, in the ordinary form's voice, that **any district able to perform the repair is invited to perform it**, and it gives a figure, and it has been rained on for nine weeks and the ink has run in one line and not in the others, and no district has come.

Owen Rusk was on the path at about ten past eight because he had been asked to come by Ada Rusk, in a hall, on the Friday, in front of nine people, and what she had said was: *"You may come and stand on the path and you may not say anything that is not a fact about a document, and if anybody asks you what you have come for you will say that you have come to read a notice, and I will not have anybody in this ward explaining you to themselves."*

So he read the notice. He read it twice, because a notice is a document and he is the officer of the record of a class of them, and then he stood with his back to the frame while nine people did a morning's work that no instrument in this county compels.

---

They did the marker, and then the channel, and then the pump.

The channel is the honest half. The silt comes up on the north side of the cut every season and every season somebody has to get a spade into it and put it in a barrow, and the barrow has to go somewhere, and there is nowhere on the north bank to put it that is not the bank, and so the crew has been putting it in the mill race for six years and the mill race is not theirs and nobody has ever said so. It is not a duty on the return. It is a thing nine people do on a Saturday because the alternative is that it is there in April.

"You have written that down," said Cobb Rennie, at the mill race, without stopping. "You have written the barrow in."

"I have written the barrow in."

"Then it is on a record now, and I want to know whose record, and I want to know whether anybody has to do anything about it." He straightened up and looked down the reach. "Because I have been putting silt in another man's race since the March before the flood and if a count comes up the reach one day and a person counts a barrowful of this district's ground in a mill's water, somebody is going to be very pleased with themselves."

**"The count is a count of draws and premises and works maintained,"** said Owen Rusk, "and it is taken by a warden with a quota, and a barrow of silt in a mill race is not one of the three and there is no column in this county for it. The reason column is a figure. Nobody is going to count your barrow."

"Good," said Cobb Rennie, and went back to the spade.

That is the whole of what there is for anybody in this county to say about a ward that has been keeping a crossing alive on a Saturday for six years with a wage four months in arrears. There is no column for it. There is no form for it. There is a notice at the weir path that invites a district to do the job, and the notice is not a failure and it is not a favour, and if Owen Rusk were a colder man he would say that the notice is the mechanism by which the repair has not had to be paid for, and he is not a cold man, and he did not say it, and he stood with his back to a frame for an hour and a half and let nine people do a thing he had no power to make anybody do.

Ada Rusk was not on the path. She was at the ward hall with a bin count and a book, and she had sent Ruth Mapp down to say that the stove was lit and that if the officer of the record wished to be in the hall at four he would find twenty people in it and a chair, and that she would not be asking him to speak.

---

The hall at Lower Lock on the Sunday evening of the eleventh day of the second month had a stove in it and about thirty people in it and a smell of wet coats that is the smell of that hall in every season, and the long table had the delivery book on it and the roster card on the end of it and, for the first time since the fourth day of the month, there was nothing in the world that any of them had been asked to sign.

The bin count was read out in Marguerite Bray's hand and it was a bin count. That is the whole of it, and it was read out because the bin at the bottom of Fetter Lane went in the sixth in the month of rain and the ward is the only body in this county that can say what is in a bin, and nobody has ever found a form for it and the ward has never once asked for one.

"**The granary has been open and empty for five days,**" said Marguerite Bray, "and it is empty because it was counted empty on the seventh and the last of it went into a barrow and there is nobody in this hall who has done anything wrong, and I am going to say the rest of it so that nobody stands up. Ada Rusk told this hall on the first of the month that the sixth was the end and that nobody was to go and buy anything, and she was right, and she wrote it in the book, and it is the correct decision and it is a decision that costs this ward about ninety meals a day and it was made by a person who had done the arithmetic out loud in front of me in April and I have watched her do it once since and not twice."

"Would you have done it," said Cobb Rennie.

"No," said Marguerite Bray. "**I would have gone and bought it. And I have a bin to empty and you have a pump to prime and neither of those things is the same as a decision, and I would like the hall to notice that I have just said so out loud, because in about four years somebody is going to write a finding about a granary and I would like it in the book that the steward's clerk would have bought.**"

Nobody said anything for a while, and the stove ticked, and then Owen Rusk stood up at the end of the table and read out the count, because it was his job and because he was the only person in the room who could read a count sheet quickly enough to be useful, and because a document that is going into a ward is better read by the person whose hand is on the record.

He read the nine, and then he read the two, and he read the line that said *not reconciled, and not to be reconciled in this season*, and then he read the paragraph about the district being short by seven and having been short in 484 and in 486, and he read the sentence about a warden suspending her own operations in a field at noon on the Friday, and when he had finished he put the sheet face down on the table, which is not a thing anybody does with a count in a hall, and Ada Rusk said:

"Leave it that way. People will want to look at it and that is the entire use of a piece of paper."

Then nobody thanked him for that either, and he sat down.

---

He did not stay for the end of it. He went out at about half past six into a lane with a pump house in it and stood in the cold for about four minutes, and the thing he was doing was arithmetic, and the arithmetic was this.

**Eleven premises of record, and it is the correct number, and it was the correct number on the seventy-sixth day of the month before last, and a pitch at the foot of Fetter Lane with a woman of seventy-one on it who has had it forty-one years is not in it and cannot be got into it by a district writing it on a form. Six people in that ward cannot be asked and the ward is performing a care act for all six of them at four in the morning, and the line in the district's own record that asks which person is responsible for that work is a line with **none** on it in capitals, and it is dated the sixth of this month, and it is in Owen Rusk's hand, and it is the district's document and not the ward's.**

**So: nothing has moved, and a season has cost this ward a crossing that stays shut, a pump primed by hand on the worst nights, four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds, a bin, a pitch, a wage four months in arrears, a granary, a girl of twelve, and a reconciliation, and it has bought the ward one line on a public board that is two names long and neither of them is a person.**

He worked out that this was correct, and that he had known it was correct since the flood week of the season before and had spent a season proving it, and that a man who proves a thing at that length has not been idle, and that there is no document in this county which records the proving, and that the office would call his record thin, and that he had written *thin* about himself before anybody in Concord Hall had.

**A hold is an answerability and preserves nothing.** He had three. Ivy Sarr's household, of two rooms in Fetter Lane, a person who cannot be asked. Mavis Rudge, of Witherow, who has a document with her name at the top of it in which somebody asked her and the answer was no. Ned Callow, of Quill Bank, who asked for a person in the room and did not get one and will not ask again. Three people, three holds, and not one of the three is a household in Fetter Lane, and the number three is a card and the card is the answer to a question he asked himself in a hall a month ago, and the answer has not changed and neither has anything else.

And then he thought about the five lines in the ward's delivery book, which were the last thing he had written in the month of rain and the first thing he had read in this one, and they were the same five, and four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds of them was still unfunded, and Cobb Rennie's pension was still a pension that is not a pension, and four pages were still four pages, and the receipt was still the one of the five that can never be paid at all.

Then he went up the lane and did the tray and was on it at a quarter past seven.

---

On the Monday morning of the twelfth day of the second month of the Concord Year 488 a form came down to the district office from Concord Hall, and it went to the second desk because it was addressed to the district and not to a person, and Junia Pell brought it round the end of the desk and put it down and said, "That is the season's return for the Count, and it has got a column on it that was not on the last one, and I have read it twice and I do not like it."

**DISTRICT RETURN OF SCHEDULE OF SETTLEMENTS TO BE RECONCILED, FOR THE SEASON OF THE CONCORD YEAR 489. To the district office, in advance of posting. To be completed and returned on or before the twenty-eighth day of this month.**

*Columns. One, settlement. Two, year of last reconciliation. Three, premises of record. Four, authorised draw. **Five, year of last survey.***

*The schedule for the season will be printed in advance and posted at the west gate of Cutter's Fields and at the post at the head of Fetter Lane on the twenty-eighth day of this month, and will not be amended after printing. A Warden may return a schedule and may not amend it. Settlements to be reconciled for the season of the Concord Year 489: ten.*

Owen Rusk read the heading of the fifth column four times and then put the sheet down flat and put both hands flat on the desk, which he does not do.

**Ten.** Nine weeks ago there were eleven. One of them is struck, and the water not drawn off it has gone into the district's main, and the one is not coming back, and the appeal lies to the Glass Stair and will take a season. Ten is the number the Office arrived at on its own, and the Office has not asked anybody, and the Office did not have to, because a settlement that is off the count is not a settlement the count has to reconcile, and eleven minus one is a subtraction any machine in Concord Hall can do at a quarter past three in the afternoon.

And **the fifth column is new, and the fifth column is the answer to a question nobody has asked yet.**

There is no year of last survey in Docketford district. There is no year of last survey in any district in this county that Owen Rusk can name, because a survey is a thing a person goes to a place and does, and nobody has gone anywhere all season except the Census Guard, and the Guard does not survey; the Guard reconciles what it is given, and it was given a schedule, and the schedule was written in the last week of the Concord Year 487 by a department that did not need to look at anything because it already had the figures.

**Somebody in Concord Hall has read a determination from the twenty-fourth of this month, or a report of one, or a count of the settlements in a district where a figure was carried four seasons in a standing extract, and has sent down a column headed *year of last survey*, and it is to be completed by the district, and the district office is the officer of the record, and there is no survey in this district to put a year in any of the ten cells.**

It costs the county nothing. It is a column that can be printed. It is exactly the thing that a fourth column on a board in a field was in the first week of this season, except that a fourth column in a field was Owen Rusk's own and cost him nothing and was the truth, and this one is going into a return with ten blank cells in it that a machine is going to read, and a blank in a machine is a zero, and a zero in a settlement's row is a figure, and a figure in a settlement's row is a reason somebody will put in a box in a hand the size of a hand.

**He may put a figure in a box and he may ask whether a paper is current. That is the whole of his card. He has had it since the fourth day of the month and he has read it about ninety times.**

He did not fill in the fifth column. He wrote **no survey in this district in the season of the Concord Year 489** across the ten cells in his own hand, which is not a figure and is not a year and is a sentence in a form that wants a year, and he is aware while writing it that a sentence in a machine is a fault and a fault in a return is a return that comes back, and that it will come back on the twenty-eighth with a note, and that the note will be correct.

Then he sent it up the stairs to the seal stair, and Junia Pell said, without looking up, "It is Monday, and that goes to the Glass Stair, and I have never once seen a fifth column on a district return in this building in three months and four days, and the man at the top will not read it and he will send it back and you will have to send it again, and I am telling you now so that you do not find out at four o'clock."

"I know," said Owen Rusk. "**Send it up anyway.**"

---

He got back to Fetter Lane at about one and did the tray, and was on it at a quarter past six, and there was nothing on it from anywhere.

There was the copy of the return of authorised draws for the season, which any person of the district may apply for, which he applied for on the Friday of the second in a room across the cut with a man of about fifty-five on it, which is made and delivered within fourteen days, and which comes on the **sixteenth.**

There was the schedule for the season of 489, which will be printed in advance on the twenty-eighth and posted at the west gate of Cutter's Fields and at the post at the head of Fetter Lane, and which will carry ten names, and which he may not examine, and which was written before anybody looked at anything, and which will be printed again in June in the last week of a month by people who have still not been to any of them.

There was the tenth cell of the fifth column, in his own hand, going up the stairs to a man who would not read it.

And there was a quarter past six on the Monday of the twelfth day of the second month of the Concord Year 488, and the granary at the bottom of Fetter Lane was open and had been open for five days, and the wage at the crossing was four months in arrears, and the repair was four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds, and the pump grant was suspended and was not going to be restored by anything anybody in this lane had heard of, and the sixteen were sixteen and Stability Order 114 was on a docket at the Glass Stair in public appeal and had not been withdrawn, and a man at a tray in a building two miles up the hill had a card with two things on it and sixteen days in which to fill in ten cells that nobody in this county had ever surveyed, and on the sixteenth a sheet a yard wide was going to arrive with eleven names on it and the quantities that had chosen them.

He put the pen down at a quarter past six and did not leave until about eight, and he did not go up the reach, and he did not write to anybody, because a warden had told him on the first morning of the season in a tent that she would not read a letter from him until the season was done, and had said it twice, and meant it both times, and the season was over at noon on the Friday.
