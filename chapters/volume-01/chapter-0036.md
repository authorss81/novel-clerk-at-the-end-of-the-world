# Chapter 36 — The Four Who Said No

The return came up on the Friday morning of the forty-sixth day of the month in the long room at the end of the second floor with the high windows, and the room was booked for the morning and the clock in it was a different clock, and about forty people came to stand at the back of it, which is what happens when a reviewing officer books a room in which papers may be produced and read.

The act was one line and Owen Rusk wrote it out in longhand on the form and read it back and then signed the box that said *entry of a filing on this tray*, and nothing else, because there was nothing else on the card to sign.

*On the forty-fifth day of the month the district performed the maintenance of the named work in the manner named in this return, upon a roster produced by the district, before the two observers recorded, the roster being produced at the ward hall and a copy held at the weir path, and the roster was read in public in the ward hall. No person not of the district was present. No person not of the district has been asked, the district having named no further day.*

Nobody in the room said anything about that clause. Owen had written it because it was true and because the reviewing officer had read a stranger's hand into this file a week earlier and the ward's whole position depended on the difference between *nobody came* and *nobody was asked*, and the second one is a fact about the ward and the first one is a fact about the day.

The two observers were re-separated in writing, in their own hands, once for this renewal, as they had been three times before. Pell wrote hers in the room and gave it to the clerk. Voss's had come up from the annex on the Thursday afternoon in an envelope with the mill's postmark on it and a line on the outside in his own hand that said *this is the fourth and not a copy of the third.*

It was **renewed for review**, on the same terms, for the **fourth** time, and the interval ran from today, and the next was due on the fifty-third.

"No grant is restored," the reviewing officer said, without being asked, because by now it was the practice of the room and he read the small print out himself. "The grant of the Fetter Lane pump is not restored and its suspension stands until its own review. The load-restriction suspension of the tenth of the month is not affected and is not superseded. The roll entry of the fourteenth stands and has not been amended. The order of the twenty-fourth stands. The crossing's standing is a duty which the district performs and as nothing else, and I am not going to say that sentence differently this week than I said it three weeks ago, and if anybody in this room is waiting for me to say it differently they may go home."

"Mr Rusk." He had not read anything out of the tray and had not looked at Owen once. "There is a new thing on this instrument and it is a small thing and I have been putting it in the file in one form or another for three renewals and I am now going to put it in the file in words that somebody has to answer."

He turned the sheet round, and there were four lines on it in a reviewing officer's own small hand, and Owen read them upside down from the back of the room and had to walk round to read them the right way up, and the clerk did not stop him.

*The appeal route of this return names a place and a name and stops there. A route with nobody standing at the far end of it is a piece of paper with an address on it. This office cannot supply the person at the far end of this route and is not asked to, and cannot be compelled to, and has no function in this matter corresponding to it. The district is asked, once, in writing, to name a person who will stand at the far end of its own route. This is not a condition of renewal and this renewal is not conditioned on an answer. It is recorded here because a reviewing officer who lets it go four times is a reviewing officer who has decided it does not matter.*

Owen Rusk stood in a room with the high windows and understood four things in about two seconds, in this order.

He could not fill it in. He was not of the ward, and the route was the ward's, and a person who is named at the far end of a district's route has to be a person the district has asked.

He could not suggest a name. He had four in a column in a book and had used none of them and the ward did not know he had them.

He could not carry it. That is a ward act, and a ward act is performed in a hall.

And the fourth thing was that the answer was due before the hearing, and the hearing was now applied for and entered and objected to, and nobody in Docketford had yet been in a room and asked to be a person.

"Right," said Owen Rusk.

"That is all," said the reviewing officer. "It is in the file. Go and do your work."

---

The ward hall at half past six on the Friday evening had a stove going and about twenty-two people in it, and four of them were not there to be helpful.

Owen Rusk had learned in six weeks that there is a particular way four people sit in a room, and it is not the way anybody notices consciously, and it is that they do not move. The other eighteen shifted, and got up, and went to the door, and came back. The four sat where they had put themselves, at three corners and a wall, and they had done that on the seventh day of the month and they were doing it now, and Bram Tillet had a cup in his hand and Holm Ferris had his cap on his knee and Wenna Sarr had her basket at her feet and Ada Rusk was standing at the end of the long table with her arms folded.

"We have read it all," said Ada Rusk.

"Read what."

"All of it." She counted them off, and it was the same hand she had used on the Thursday night with the Council's circular, and it did not slow down. "The roster, which we have read because anybody may read it. The schedule of the precedent, which was read out in this room in the granary by a man from the mill on a Wednesday night a fortnight and a bit ago and never explained to us by anybody in that building. The council's paper, which Mr Rusk read out in this room on Wednesday. The refusal column, which we have all had in our hands since the Saturday and which nobody has ever read out loud in a room, because it is a hundred and nine lines and it takes a woman with a free morning. The hearing statement, which is four lines and three of them are mine. The thing about the route that the man with the clock said out loud this morning in a room with windows."

"Which is not fair," said Owen Rusk. "You were not in that room."

"I was told about it at the double count on Wednesday night, by a woman of sixty-one, in about nine words, and she said *and the clerk cannot fill it in and do not let him try*." Ada Rusk unfolded her arms. "So we have read it all, and we have done what we have been doing all week, which is to look at the four documents that are about this ward and see where we are in them. And we are not in any of them. Not one. And we have not been in one of them since the seventh day of the month, and before the seventh day of the month we were in all of them, and that is the trade, and I made it, and I have said out loud in this room that I made it, and I am not going to be talked out of it by anybody in here including the four of you."

Wenna Sarr spoke. She had been sitting on the wall by the door with her hands in her lap and she spoke at the pitch she spoke at, which is the pitch of a woman who sells nothing in particular and is used to being heard over a road.

"You have all become legible," she said, "and I have not."

Nobody made a noise.

"So say the rest of it," said Ada.

"I have got a niece," said Wenna Sarr. "She is twelve. She is on the standpipe rota on a Thursday at four in the morning and Mr Rusk stood on a step and looked at a sheet of paper with five names on it on the thirty-eighth day of the month, and every one of those five names could be read by any person who came into this hall, and I have been in this hall six weeks and there is a page with a sentence on it about my brother and a mill receipt and a woman who cannot read what the office hands her." She moved her hands. "That page is the only thing anybody has ever written about us in four years. It says we were wrong. It says it in Mrs Bray's hand and Ada's and it is in a book in this hall and any person can read it. That is being legible. That is the worst day of my life and I would not take it back."

"Then that is what you get," said Ada Rusk, "and you are getting it because on the seventh day of the month I asked a hall of twenty-eight people a question and four of them put their hands up, and I asked them to say it out loud, and one of them said it twice, and Mrs Bray wrote it down with the day and the hour on it, and the reason I did it in front of people instead of in a corridor is that a thing said in a corridor can be taken back by the person who said it and a thing said in a room with a witness on it cannot."

"I voted no because we were going to lose," said Wenna Sarr. "I did not vote no because I wanted to be the only one who could say no."

"I know exactly why you voted no," said Ada. "You have never once pretended otherwise and neither have I. Now sit down, because I have read that document four times and I have got a thing to put to the four of you and I am going to do it in front of twenty-two people, and I have thought about doing it in a corridor for two days and I have decided not to, for the reason I have just said."

She put a sheet of paper on the table. It was in her capitals, and it had a heading on it, and there was no Registry stamp on it anywhere, and Mrs Bray had a pencil in her hand before it touched the wood.

"**A separate branch is not a pardon,**" Ada Rusk said. "I am going to say what it is, because half of you have been sitting there since Wednesday working out what it is and getting it wrong, and getting it wrong would be worse for you than not having it.

"It is not a reinstatement. Nothing in this office has ever said that any of the four of us was excluded by a mistake, and if anybody in that building ever does say it we will not want it, and I would like that written down. It is a separate thing. Narrower. It carries its own duty, which is not the bridge and is not the marker and is not the pump, because those are the crew's and the four of us are not the crew. It carries its own two observers, which are not ours and not the office's, and it carries its own withdrawal right, which means one of the four of you can put your own branch out of it and the rest of it does not fall over. And it carries its own exposure, which is the whole of what I have been trying to tell this hall for a fortnight, which is that a document is a document and it can be read by somebody who was not there, and the hours of whichever of you take it will be on a piece of paper that a stranger may read without notice and take a copy away, and you may complain in pencil, unsigned, and I will rule it in the book the same week.

"And here is the part that matters and it is one line. **A branch is added to a return by a district office naming its holder in writing.** Not by the ward. Not by me. By that office, in writing, by name. And I have been sitting with that for two days and I have worked out what it means, and I am going to say it, and Mrs Bray is going to write it down whatever it is."

She turned round and looked at the four of them, one at a time, which took about four seconds and was the longest four seconds of the evening.

"It means the door only opens from their side. If they name the four of us in writing then we are in the objection class of that return and we may object to it, and that is what we would want. **And it opens the other way as well**, because the class of persons who may object to that return is the eight persons whose branches are in it and any person the district office names in writing, and it is eight, and it has been eight since the twenty-fourth of the month, and it is eight because on that day I filled a box in, and I wrote the wider version first, *any adult of the district not a party to this return*, and it was a class broad enough to be a door left open instead of a door shut, and Mrs Bray made me read both versions out in a room with the door shut and then made me use the narrow one, and I used the narrow one, and the four of us went out of it for ever, and nobody in that office has named anybody in writing since, and I have watched for it every week."

"So it is one door," said Wenna Sarr. "And it opens both ways."

"It is one door and it opens both ways and it is the only door there is." Ada put her hand flat on the table. "I am not asking them to open it this week. I am telling you what it is, because I made you a promise on the seventh day in this room and the promise was that you would not be asked again without being told first, and I have never once broken it and I am not starting on a Friday night."

"Who pays for it?" said Wenna Sarr.

Nobody answered for about two seconds.

"Nobody," said Ada Rusk.

"That is what I thought you would say and I wanted to hear you say it in front of the room and not in a corridor." She did not move. "Because I have been in this hall six weeks and the answer to that question about anything has been the same every single time, and you are not going to be the exception, and if you had come in here tonight with a sum I would have asked to see the instrument, and you have not got one, and I would have known you had not."

"I have not got one," said Ada. "I have got a condition in a book in Marguerite Bray's hand that says *and the wage* and *wage not funded*, and that is the closest thing anybody in this ward has come to money in six weeks, and it is a condition and not a wage, and I am not going to stand here in a warm hall and tell four people that a branch is a wage. It is not. It is a place in a document for a duty somebody has already agreed to do, and it is *more* than you have now, and it is *less* than you were offered on the seventh day, and I am not able to make those two things into one thing and I am not going to try in front of you."

Holm Ferris, who is fifty-eight and keeps the night watch in this ward and had not spoken since the Wednesday, said:

"I want to know what the duty is."

"Then help me," said Ada Rusk, "because that is the part I have not got. A branch carries a duty. I have been talking about the *paper* of it for two days because the paper is what I understand and the paper is what I have got, and the paper is not the thing. Somebody has to say what the four of you would do on a Tuesday, in the rain, before somebody who is not us, that is a duty, and it has to be a thing you would actually do, and it has to be a thing that can be done by one of you alone, because a duty that needs all four of us is a duty that will not be done in February."

The room sat with that for a while.

And Owen Rusk, who had been standing at the end of the table since half past six with his hands behind his back, said:

"I think a separate branch is a reinstatement in the return—"

"No," said Wenna Sarr, without turning round.

He stopped.

"A reinstatement is what a person gets," said Wenna Sarr, "when the office says that the leaving out was a mistake. A reinstatement puts you back in the thing you were left out of, on the ground that you should never have been out of it. That is the thing that is supposed to happen when four people are right. And no office in this city has said that about us, and none of them is going to, and if one of them did we would have to think very hard about whether we wanted it, because a reinstatement is a favour and a favour can be withdrawn, and it would put us back inside a document that somebody else drew the lines of." She turned round on the wall. "A separate branch is not that. A separate branch is a smaller thing that we drew ourselves and that somebody in that building has to agree to put in writing. And it is worse. That is what I want said out loud in this room tonight before anybody goes home pleased. It is a smaller thing and it is a thing with our names on it and nobody else's, and it is going to be read without notice, and it does not put us back in anything."

Owen Rusk stood at the end of the table and had been wrong in a room in front of twenty-two people and had been corrected in one sentence by a woman selling nothing in particular from a stall, and he said:

"That is right. I withdraw it."

"Thank you," said Wenna Sarr, and that was the whole of the exchange, and Ada Rusk looked at Owen for a second longer than she usually did, which was a thing about the seventh day of the month and nothing else.

"Mr Rusk is witnessing this," Ada said, to the room, "and not drafting it, and Mrs Bray is ruling it, and if either of them changes a word I will know, and Wenna has already caught one of them out once tonight so they are not doing well and I am not going to pretend they are."

"Proceed," said Owen Rusk.

---

Bram Tillet said no.

He said it at about a quarter to eight, from the corner, without standing up, and it was the last thing in the evening and it was so quiet that Ada had to ask him to say it again.

"Say it again," she said, "louder, because if I have to ask you then it goes in the book wrong."

Bram Tillet was forty-six and had been a ferry cook at Wexford Landing since he was nineteen and he has cooked on the middle crossing since he was nineteen and he voted no on the seventh day of the month with a hand up and a reason he gave at the time in one sentence, and he is not a man who speaks in rooms.

"No," he said, louder. "And I want the reason written down and I want it written down in the same breath as the no, because I have watched four people in this hall do a thing this month and the reason always arrives about a day late and by then everybody has agreed with it."

"Go on."

"The branch has its own two observers." Bram Tillet put his cup on the table. "The roster has one and she came from a landing upriver and she said herself, in this room, on the thirty-eighth, that her whole power is that she was there and that she can stop, and that she is expensive in Thursdays. And that is the best thing anybody has done in this ward in six weeks and it cost that woman a whole day and she was not paid and she will come again only if the ward asks her in writing on the ward's own paper."

He looked round the room.

"So where is the Thursday coming from for a branch? Not from that woman, because we have used her Thursday and a branch is not a day, a branch is for ever. Not from the mill, because the mill has a factor and a factor reads notices. Not from the Registry, because the Registry maintains the forms and there is a name against that duty on a schedule and I would not trust it with the only thing I would have." He picked his cap off his knee. "Which leaves this room. Which means the four people in the branch check the branch. And I have thought about that and I cannot do it, and I want it understood that I am not refusing because of the seventh day and I am not refusing because somebody offered me a way back into a document. I am refusing because I have watched you all, every one of you, agree in this hall to be read on a calendar, and I have watched what it did to you, and I am not going to be the reason a woman of twenty-four has to explain at a double count on a Wednesday night why nobody came to look at our hours."

The room did not make a noise.

"I am not saying the branch is a bad idea," said Bram Tillet. "I am saying it is a good idea with nobody standing at the end of it, and the honest thing about that is that the woman who told you being legible is not the same as being paid has not got to the other half of it, and neither has anybody else. **Being read by somebody who can stop is not the same as being read by somebody who can stay.**"

Nobody said anything for some time.

"Written down," said Ada Rusk.

"It is written down," said Marguerite Bray, who had it written down, and who had the day and the hour under it.

"Mr Rusk. Would you put a branch in a return with the four of us in it and nobody to read it."

"No," said Owen Rusk.

"Then it is not ready," said Ada Rusk, "and neither am I."

---

She did not stop the evening there, and Owen Rusk understood afterwards that this was the whole of the difference between the sixth week of the rain season and the first, and that nobody in the building had taught it to her.

"Right," she said. "Here is what is going to happen and it is not a decision, it is a piece of paper.

"Mrs Bray is going to rule in the book, in her own hand, with the day and the hour, that a separate branch for the four of us has been offered in this hall on this evening, in these terms, by me, and that one of the four has refused it on the evening it was offered, giving his reason, and that three have not answered, and that there is no time limit on their not answering except the one I am about to write down. And after it there are going to be four pages, one for each of us, with our names at the top and nothing else on them, and no box, and no form, and no word printed on them at all. And a branch is not going to be offered again unless and until one of the four of you writes on one of those pages, and I am not going to chase you, and Mr Rusk is not going to chase you, and Mrs Bray is not going to chase you, and the office is not going to be asked to name anybody until one of you has written something."

"And the date," said Wenna Sarr. "You said a time limit."

"The day of the hearing," said Ada Rusk. "Whichever day it turns out to be. And I am not going to be able to help you with that, because it is not in anybody's book yet, and when it is, Mrs Bray will rule the day in the same column and you will be able to see it here in the hall, on the wall, whenever you want to look at it."

Marguerite Bray wrote for about four minutes, in her own hand, and then ruled four lines under it, and then she wrote the four names at the top of four separate pages, one under another, in her own hand, and put the pencil down and read the whole of it back to the room including the part about the day of the hearing, which is the part that made Ada Rusk look at the floor for a second.

And then she said the last thing of the night, in the flat voice she uses for things she means.

"Twenty-two years ago I wrote a man's name in a delivery book because he brought me a paper and told me he could not read it, and it was the correct thing to do, and it did him no good at all." She put her hand flat on the page. "I am not telling you what I think. I am telling you what I have got, and what I have got is four blank pages in my own book with four names at the top of them, and that is a very small thing and it is the only thing I have ever been able to give anybody, and I have given it to four people who have not asked me for it, which is the only way anybody in this ward has ever received anything worth having."

At the door Ada Rusk stopped Owen with two fingers on his sleeve, the way she had done in the third week, and did not let go.

"You will have to answer a request from that office," she said. "For the schedule. It came on the Wednesday. You carried it up the hill in your coat like a favour."

"It came from the district office, in the correct form, with a day on it."

"I know. That is why I am asking." She let go of his sleeve. "Tell me on Monday morning what I want to do about it, and do not bring me an answer on Saturday night. And Mr Rusk — " and she looked at the far end of the table, at four blank pages under four names — "I told that room in the fourth week that a man who keeps a thing alive by holding on to it has no business telling a ward what it may perform. I have just watched a man refuse to fill in a box on a ward's behalf in front of twenty-two people, and the four people who are named on those pages are on the far side of that sentence and not the near side, and I want you to notice that you did that on a Friday night with no idea it was a test."
