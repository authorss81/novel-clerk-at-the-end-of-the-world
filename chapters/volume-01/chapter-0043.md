# Chapter 43 — A Duty for One Household

The requirements came out of the seal stair on the Tuesday morning of the fifty-seventh day of the month as a sheet of copying paper with a heading on it and a requirements box on the other side, and the requirements sheet is the single most cheerful document in the Registry of Continuant Works, because it is the only sheet anybody ever produces that tells a person exactly what has to be true before anything can happen.

Owen Rusk carried it up the hill in the rain at about eleven and put it on the long table in the ward hall, and Ada Rusk read it standing, and then she made everybody else read it standing, which takes four minutes and is the only procedure this ward has ever adopted from the Office without an argument.

A **protective return** may be entered for a child, or for a person who cannot answer for themselves, and it protects the person's status for as long as the care situation lasts, and it cannot bind that person's consent, labour, identity, vote or private life, whichever of those may be in issue, and it is reviewed every seven days. And it requires five things.

**One.** Two unrelated living witnesses who have nothing to gain from the answer.

**Two.** A current care act — an act performed this week, by somebody, for that person, that a person could name.

**Three.** A named local appeal route, with a person at the far end of it.

**Four.** A **custodian** — a named place at which the thing is kept, and a named person who keeps it, and a power in the office to replace that person.

**Five.** Nothing. There is no fifth. There are four, and the sheet says four, and there is a fifth thing on the sheet about review and a sixth about the reservation of consent, and the four are the four.

"Three," said Ada Rusk.

"Three of the four," said Owen Rusk, "and it has been three of the four since the third week of the month and the three are the two witnesses and the care act. The route went into the return on the forty-eighth with a name at the far end of it, conditionally, and the condition is not met yet and I am not going to tell the room that it is met."

"Which leaves the custodian," said Ada Rusk.

"Which leaves the custodian."

And there was about a second and a half of the room not saying anything, and Owen Rusk understood — because he had been in seven rooms in seven weeks in which a person did not say something — that the reason nobody said anything is that all four of the people who might have been asked are in this ward, and three of them are in this room, and one of them is standing at the end of the table with her arms folded.

"A custodian," said Ilse Mern, "is a word in a form. It is not a person. Is it a person."

"It is a named place where the thing is kept and a named person who keeps it," said Owen Rusk, "and the office may replace that person, and the power to replace is in the instrument and not in the ward."

"Then it is a person," said Ilse Mern, "with the difference that if I am wrong anybody in that building can put a different person in my place without asking me, and the person they put in my place is a person I have never met doing a thing I cannot see, about a child."

"Yes."

"Then somebody had better say what it costs," said Ilse Mern, "out loud, in this room, before anybody is asked, because I am the person who is going to be asked and I have worked out what I would say if I were asked and I would rather say it first than be asked."

Nobody stopped her, which is what Ada Rusk does in a hall when a person has the floor and is right.

"A custodian is not a fee," said Ilse Mern. "Nobody is going to pay me and I have known that since the thirty-first of the month, and it is not a feeling either, and I want that said, because in about a month somebody is going to describe this as a burden I have taken on and I will not have it described that way.

"It is **five hours a week.** Not five hours on a Thursday. Five hours a week, in the house, where somebody can come to the door at any hour and ask me where the papers are, and I have to be able to say. Not a locked drawer — a locked drawer is a thing you can say is in a locked drawer. It is a thing I know where it is and can put my hand on it in the dark. Five hours a week, for as long as the care situation lasts, which is the phrase on that sheet, and the care situation for that child is not a thing with an end in it, and I am forty-four and I have got two rooms and a laundry and I am not going to have five hours a week of a thing I cannot refuse in the middle."

"How did you get to five," said Owen Rusk.

"Because I have done it before for a woman in Bell Row whose son was taken and there was nowhere to keep what he had written, and it was not five hours when it was the last year, it was nearly every day, and I was thirty-eight." She moved her hands. "And I will say the other half, and then Ada can rule it and we can all be uncomfortable. The office may replace me. That is the sentence. Whoever holds that thing is the person anybody comes to, and anybody means anybody — a clerk, a constable, a man from the district office with a form, and one day somebody from a building in Concord Hall who has read about us in a circular. And the person they come to is the person I was, and the hours of the person I was do not stop because the person has been replaced. They stop because somebody says so, or they do not."

"Right," said Ada Rusk. "Five hours a week, and the office may replace you, and nobody in this room can pay it, and I am not going to stand here and pretend otherwise in front of the woman. I asked her and she answered and I have nothing to add to what she said."

"What happens if nobody takes it," said Wenna Sarr, from the wall.

"Then the six of them do not have a protective return," said Ada Rusk, "and they do not have one because the instrument needs a place and a person, and there is no such person, and the absence of a custodian is not a hole in a document. It is a child with no address for her own papers."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Then it is not that the ward cannot get a custodian," said Owen Rusk carefully. "It is that a custodian is a Registry function, and the Registry does not pay for one, and this city has not paid for one in — a long time. There are four people in this ward who could be asked and I am not going to write the word beside any of their names, in a book or in a document, and Mrs Bray, put that in the book, because I am saying it in a hall and somebody should be writing it down: **the word is not written beside any of them.**"

---

The letter came at about two in the afternoon on the Tuesday, and it came to the ward hall door, and it came in the ward's bag.

That is the whole of how it arrived and it is the whole of the arrangement, and it had been made on the Thursday of the previous week by a woman who has said for nineteen years that she has stood at the end of another ward's route once and will not say where.

Ada Rusk took it off the step and read it standing in the doorway, and then she went and got Marguerite Bray and made her stand next to her, and then she read it out loud in the hall with her hand on the page, which is a thing this hall has done with a great many things in seven weeks.

It was four lines. It was in a hand that had been on a tide table's glass frame for nineteen years and had not been asked to write a letter in a long time.

*I have read the schedule of performed acts and the four lines that came with it.*

*I will be in the room on the fifty-ninth at ten of the morning, with the document in front of me.*

*I am not a witness to your return and my name is not on it and I sign nothing that your district performs. Bring the sheet and not the book.*

*Do not tell me in advance what you are going to say.*

Nobody in the hall said anything for about four seconds.

"Four lines," said Ada Rusk. "And not one of them is a kindness."

"Those are the best four lines anybody has written to this ward in seven weeks," said Owen Rusk, "and I have spent a month in a building where two hundred and twenty-four words were the answer to a question, and I would like everybody in this room to understand which of those two things I have just compared."

"You are going to tell me what to say in that room," said Ada Rusk, "and you are not going to get the chance, because it is not your sentence and it is not my sentence and it is not Mr Rusk's sentence, and it is going to be whatever Mrs Bray says in the first four minutes after the chair has said what the two matters are, because the far end of that route is a person who will say what this ward did and nothing else, and if anybody in that building tries to make her into anything else we will find out about it in about ninety seconds and it will not be recoverable."

"I am not going to try."

"I know you are not. It is what the seventh line was for."

---

The Wednesday was the day the return had to be made narrow, and the reason it had to be made narrow was not a legal one, and Ada Rusk said so in the first two minutes of the afternoon, with four people in the hall and the fire barely in.

"I am going to say the reason out loud before anybody finds it on their own," she said, "and then I am going to sit down and I am not going to help anybody, because it is my sentence and it has been my sentence since Saturday and I have been carrying it round this ward for three days waiting for a room."

Nobody stopped her.

"On Saturday night a woman of seventy-one lost forty-one years of a pitch. On Sunday a bin in this ward went in a corner and a man on Bell Row has been carrying oakum since dawn and does not want a fuss. On Monday the water stood in this granary for four hours and the count came out at a fortnight. And the repair is four thousand one hundred and sixty pounds and unfunded and the notice is on a path that was under water until Sunday afternoon, and the wage is four months in arrears, and the crossing is shut, and the grant is suspended and will be suspended in a month.

"We are going to the Glass Stair on Thursday morning. And a Deputy Registrar's office is going to look at a district return and ask one question, and the question is going to be: *can this district afford to keep what it has claimed.* And the answer, on the evidence this ward has produced in seven weeks, is that it cannot afford to keep a great deal of it, and that is not a matter of opinion, that is the content of our own schedule, which anybody may read.

"So either we go up there with a claim this ward cannot keep, and somebody in that building is honest and says so, and the return is not certified — or we go up there with a claim this ward can keep, which is a smaller claim, and every person in this room is going to lose something off the paper on Friday, and it is going to be chosen by us, and not by them."

"By us," said Dory Anstruther.

"By us," said Ada Rusk. "I am not going to pretend there is any other way of doing that. The Office will not do it for us. Mr Rusk cannot do it for us, and I am going to say why in a moment and it is going to be said in a hall and not in a corridor. **We are the ones who have to decide which of our own hours are worth a piece of paper.**"

---

She asked him on the Wednesday night, at about nine, in the room at the back, with Marguerite Bray in the room and the delivery book open on the table and a lamp on it, and she did it in about forty seconds and she did not sit down.

"Mr Rusk. I am going to ask you to take a duty for a household, and it is Ivy Sarr's, and it is two people, and it is the smallest instrument anybody in this ward has ever asked anybody for, and it is the heaviest thing in the book, and I want you to hear all of that before you answer me."

"Go on."

"You cannot be the custodian. You are not a Registry officer and you are not paid and there is no power in you to be replaced, so the fourth part of the requirements is not you. You cannot be a witness. You live in this ward and a witness to a return is a person who does not live in the thing, and you have said so yourself to two different people in two different weeks. So there is nothing in you for it.

"There is one thing left and I want it said in a room and not in a corridor, because it is not a small thing to ask and you will want to get up and go to the end of the lane. **You are the person who will be on the page and be answerable for it.** Not the office of it. The person. You would be the one it comes back to."

Marguerite Bray did not look up from the book.

"What does it cost," said Owen Rusk.

"That is the right question," said Ada Rusk, "and I am not going to pretend to anybody that I have got a tidy answer, and I am going to give you the answer I have got in about ninety seconds and then you may say no and I will rule it in the book with the day on it and it will be the same as Mr Tillet's, and I will not hold it against you, and you may leave the room while I say the rest of it if you like."

"Go on."

"A duty is not a fee and it is not a feeling. It takes the unresolved part of that household with it, and the unresolved part of that household is: a child of twelve who is on a standpipe rota at four in the morning in the dark, a woman of about sixty who cannot read what the office hands her, a brother who drowned in the spring and left a receipt that was validly made and used for a place, a girl of nine and a girl of six and three people marked *unable to answer*, one of them with an address instead of a name, and six of them with no custodian and no instrument and no route. A duty of this kind does not close. It has no end date on it, because the end date is the child's, and the child's end date is not anybody's paper in this city.

"And what it cannot close will come to you," said Ada Rusk, "in the ordinary way that unresolved things come to the person who is holding them, which is to say that in three years somebody will be angry with you about it, and in five years you will be the only person left who can remember what the office said on the fourteenth of the month, and there is no room in that building for a thing like this except at your desk or in your own head, and it will not stay in your head, and it will come out at the wrong moment in front of the wrong person, and that is not a warning, that is how it works on everybody who has ever held one."

Owen Rusk stood in the room at the back of a ward hall at nine o'clock at night on the fifty-eighth day of the month of a rain season and did not say anything for about half a minute.

"Ask it properly," he said.

Ada Rusk looked up.

"You have given me the cost of the thing," he said. "I want the question. In the form you want it in. Because I have watched this ward find out three times in seven weeks that a question asked in the wrong words produces a document instead of an answer, and I am not going to be the fourth."

Ada Rusk put her hand flat on the table.

"Mr Rusk. Would you accept, in your own name, outside of any office, the duty of being the person answerable for the return of the household of Ivy Sarr in this ward, for as long as she is a person who cannot answer for herself?"

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

He said it in about a second, and then he said the rest of it, and he said it standing in a room with a woman of sixty-one writing it down, which is the arrangement in this ward and is not an accident.

"I want it recorded that I asked what it costs before I said it, and that I was answered, and that the answer was that it does not close. And I want it recorded why I said yes, because if the reason is not written down then in about a year somebody will write down a better one. **I said yes because nobody else will, and that is not a noble reason, and it is the true one.** Four people in this ward could hold a thing like that and three of them have said in this room in the last month that they cannot afford to, and the fourth is fifty-eight and is owed a pension this city has never paid him and I am not going to be the man who hands him a duty and calls it an honour."

"Mr Rusk," said Ada Rusk, "you are not going to hand Cobb Rennie anything, and you have never once tried to, and I want that on the same line."

"The word is not written beside any of them," said Owen Rusk, "and it is not going to be, and the five hours a week that Ilse Mern said out loud in this room on Tuesday morning is in Mrs Bray's book in her own hand and it is a number on a page and not a gap, and that is all I have been asked to do about it and I have done it."

Marguerite Bray wrote for about two minutes, in her own ruled hand, in the second column, with the day and the hour, and read it back, all of it, including the sentence about the reason, because she has never in thirty-six years read back the part that flatters anybody and omitted the part that does not.

Then Ada Rusk turned round and picked up her slate.

"And now the other thing," she said, "and it is small, and I am doing it because I would rather do it in a hall than have it done to us. On Friday the return comes up. It is the sixth renewal and it is a review and it has never been binding and it is not going to be binding on Friday. And I have not yet decided what comes off it, and I am not going to decide it tonight, and I am not going to decide it tomorrow in a room with four people in it.

"Mr Rusk has spent seven weeks arguing for a return that is as wide as it can honestly be. I am going to have to take it off him. And I want him to hear me say in advance that I know what I am about to do, and that I am going to do it, and that he is not going to be in the room when I do it, and why."

"Why," said Owen Rusk.

"Because on Friday morning a person is going to stand in a hall in Docketford in front of about forty people and say *and you hid them inside a favourable reading*, and I would like the thing I do on Friday afternoon to be mine, and I would like the man who says the hardest sentence anybody has said in this case to be in a building two miles away when I do it." She picked up the chalk. "Go down the hill. Get the ferry. And when somebody asks you tomorrow why a ward of two hundred and forty people gave something up on a Friday afternoon, you tell them it was narrow enough to be true, and do not tell them it was your idea."
