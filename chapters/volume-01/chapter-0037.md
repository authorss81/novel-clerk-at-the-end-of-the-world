# Chapter 37 — Answer in Public

The hall was fuller on the fourteenth day of a sheet than it had been on the first, and Owen Rusk had learned that this is not a thing a person arranges.

On the first day a man read the sheet and went away. On the third, two people read it together. By the fourteenth there was a queue that anybody arriving at the Glass Stair counter walked into without meaning to, and about a third of the people in it came to the counter for something else, and stayed about four minutes longer than they meant to, and there was a habit forming of pointing.

He came in at half past ten on the Saturday morning of the forty-seventh day and stood at the table for about two minutes and then did the thing he had decided on the Thursday in a hall with thirty people in it, which was to stand at the side of it rather than behind it, and to say nothing to anybody who came to read it, and to let the queue find him.

At about eleven the door at the end of the hall opened and an officer of the district office came out with a sheet of paper in his hand and a runner behind him, and Owen Rusk felt the room find out what it was about a second and a half before anybody said anything, because it is a particular sound that thirty people make when a person in a good coat walks into a room holding paper.

The officer was about thirty and did not know Owen and had clearly been told to do exactly what he was about to do.

He put the sheet on the table beside Owen's, squared it, and turned round to the room.

"This is the answer of the district office to a comparison posted at this table," he said. "It is dated the fortieth day of the month. It is posted here so that anybody who has read the comparison may read the answer. It is not a filing and I am not taking anybody's name."

He read it out. He read it in a clear voice and at a reasonable speed and he did it well, and about half the room could follow it, and Owen Rusk stood at the side of the table and heard his own case answered in public by a clerk who had four minutes.

*That a disposition is a decision. That a decision is entered in this office's column with the officer's initials and the date of its making. That a disposition is not a reason and is not a record of a reason. That this office does not maintain a column of reasons and is not required by any instrument in force in this district to maintain one. That the disposition "No district office competent" is a statement of the reach of this office at the date of its making, and is not a finding upon any person, place or work.*

*That the office notes that a district which performs a duty in public may show that performance, and that a schedule of performed acts is not a reason and does not answer the disposition of any entry in the refusal column of this office.*

*The office has answered a comparison posted at a public counter. It does not treat the posting of a comparison as a filing, and will not answer it as one in any other quarter.*

Owen Rusk read the last three lines twice and then stopped reading and started listening to the room, which was the thing he had come to do.

The officer had said the six words *in any other quarter* in the same voice as everything else, which is to say he had not known that he was saying them, and a woman of about fifty near the back of the room had made a small noise, and somebody at the front had said *what does that mean* loud enough for him to answer it.

"It means it is the answer for next quarter as well," said the officer, patiently. "A comparison is not a filing. The office answers a comparison once and does not carry it forward, and if the comparison is posted again the office will answer it again, and there is no form on which a question of this kind may be carried forward."

"Then what is the point of the table," said somebody.

"That is not a question the office can take," said the officer.

"It is a question," said Owen Rusk, "and I asked it, and I am not going to let him leave without an answer to it going on the sheet in his hand, because in three months somebody will say the office was never asked."

The officer looked at him for about a second and a half and Owen understood that he was about to be asked who he was, and understood that the answer mattered, and understood about a second later that it did not matter at all, which was new.

"O. Rusk," he said. "River Desk. Co-petitioner in the matter the comparison names."

"Right," said the officer, and wrote something on the back of the sheet in about four seconds, and did not read it aloud, and squared it again. "That is noted at the counter. It is not a filing. I am sorry that I cannot take a question; there is a person at the counter who takes them and I am not that person."

He went back in and the door shut, and about twenty people stood in the hall and looked at two sheets of paper on a table by a door.

---

Owen Rusk had said nothing for about a minute and a half, and then he said the thing.

He did not write it out first. He had it in about ninety words and he had had it since the Thursday afternoon, assembled out of a hundred and nine entries and a roster and four months of an unfunded wage, and he said it at the side of a table in a public waiting hall at eleven o'clock on a Saturday morning, without a sheet, in the ordinary voice, the way a man reads out a column.

"A district that performs in public can show the face of a duty," he said. "It sets a marker at seven forty in the rain and anybody may come and read the sheet. An office that refuses without a reason cannot show the face of a refusal, because there is nothing to show. And the only people in this city who can bear witness to what an office decides are offices and districts, and the districts are not doing it, because being legible is not the same as being paid. So put the reason in the column. One sentence, four minutes a week."

Nobody said anything, and then the woman of about fifty at the back said: "Say the last part again."

"The part about the districts not doing it?"

"Aye."

"Because the wage is not funded," said Owen Rusk. "That is the whole of it. Four households and a man of fifty-eight are on a standpipe rota at four in the morning, and one of those four households has a girl of twelve in it, and their names are on a roster in a hall in Lower Lock, and any person may read it, and I have been in that hall six weeks. And they are not being paid, and being on the sheet does not pay them, and being read by a stranger does not pay them, and the whole of what that hall has done this month is make a set of unpaid hours legible in a flood year. And you cannot be checked for a thing you are not paid for, and you cannot be examined for it either, and a district that is not paid for its duty is a district nobody can be obliged to do anything, and that is a sentence about this city and not about the Registry."

He stopped.

"Where is the ward?" said somebody.

"Fetter Lane. Two miles upriver."

"Which ward is that?"

"Lower Lock."

"Wasn't that the one on the roll in the news about the crossing?"

"Which news?"

"The one where a man put a thing on a table in here." The woman stood up. She was about fifty and she had a basket and she had come in on the Saturday morning to do something else entirely. "You've been standing next to that table a fortnight."

"Since the thirty-third," said Owen Rusk.

She looked at the two sheets, and then at the queue, and then at him, and then she went out into the rain.

Nobody followed her. That was the part he noticed afterwards, and he noticed it at about eleven twenty and thought about it for the rest of the day, and it was this: a dozen people had heard the argument and one of them had gone out into the rain, and the argument was good, and the queue in front of the table had got about two people longer while he was saying it, and neither of those two facts had anything to do with each other.

---

Pell came in at about half past twelve, on a Saturday, in a wet coat, and stood at the side of the table and read both sheets in about three minutes without touching either of them.

"Two things," she said. "One of them is good and I am not going to be the one who tells you which."

"Go on."

"First. Read the first five clauses against the answer you got at the counter a week ago." She put her finger in the air about a foot from the paper. "Word for word. Same order. Same sheet, with a sixth clause and a line at the foot added by hand on Wednesday, and a different signature at the bottom, and the same five clauses in the same order in the same words as the one that came down on the forty-th in the same building to the same table. I have been in this Office three months and I have read about nine of these this quarter and that is not a coincidence, Rusk, that is a *form*. The thing that answered your comparison is a form with your sheet's date typed into the top of it. It was written before the district office had even asked the ward for its schedule, and it went out on the Wednesday, which means somebody upstairs knew on the Monday that you were going to go on asking, and that is the whole of what a form is."

"And the name on the schedule of duties maintains the form."

"The name on the schedule of duties maintains the form. And the form is correct. And the form is going to be the answer in every quarter of this year and probably for the rest of your career, and the reason it is going to be the answer is not that it is good, it is *sufficient*. It says five true things and a sixth courteous thing and a line at the foot, and there is not one word in it that a person can hold up, and it will therefore never be improved and never be withdrawn, and it will go in a drawer and it will be brought out in October, and the man who brings it out will not be a villain and will not have done anything wrong and will be a person doing a good job in the way he was trained."

"Second?"

"The second is the thing I have come to say and I am going to say it once and then I am going to go home." She took her hand out of her coat. "You have made yourself a standing item. That is a real thing and it is worth a very great deal more than a good answer in a corridor would have been worth, and you should be pleased, and you should also understand that you have not got it for free and that you will not be able to put it down.

"Every Saturday from now until somebody takes that table down, people will look at that door on the way in. Some of them will read the sheet. Some of them will read the answer. Some of them will look at you. And from today they will do it *expecting* something, and a room that expects something from you is a room that has stopped telling you what it thinks and started telling you what it would like, and the difference between those two things is the whole of what it means to be a public person." She looked at him steadily. "You have lost the ability to be surprised in a room, Rusk. You will walk into a hall and people will already be looking at the door and you will not find out what the room thinks until after you have spoken. That is a real loss and nobody warns you about it and I am warning you about it in a wet coat on a Saturday, and I am not going to say that I am sorry, because I told you on the sixth day of the last month what you were doing and you said the two sentences out loud and I am not going to pretend the thing you got is a thing I did not warn you about."

"Which was the other thing," said Owen Rusk.

"The other thing is much better news and I am going to be short about it." She nodded at the table. "The office posted the answer at a public table on a Saturday morning in a room with a queue in it. It did not send it to the district office and it did not send it to the docket and it did not wait for the clerk to be asked. It walked out of a door and read it out and took a question at the counter and wrote it on the back of the sheet. A week ago that office sent a paragraph to a docket. It has not been warned of anything and it has done this because the circular of the Wednesday named a review in this district, and an office that has been named in a document improves its manners, and that is not sarcasm, that is a fact about how offices work, and it is also the reason the answer to your comparison is a form instead of a man."

"Which means the form is going to be a better form."

"The form is going to be a *shorter* form," said Pell, "and shorter is not better, shorter is cheaper, and cheaper is the only thing you can get out of an office by being interesting to it."

---

He got up the hill at half past two on the Saturday afternoon and the hall was shut, because a hall is shut on a Saturday unless somebody has business, and the granary book said two weeks and the crossing was shut and the sixteen were still sixteen and the return was renewed on the Friday and would come up again on the fifty-third, and the standing item in Docketford was a sheet of paper with a heading on it and two hundred and forty people behind it.

And Owen Rusk stood in the lane above the river in the rain and worked out the only arithmetic that mattered in the whole of that morning, and it took about ninety seconds and it is four sentences long and it is the truest thing he wrote all week, and he did not write it in the register that night, because it is not a fact about a document and he has been caught before about the difference.

It is a practice and not an accident. The Office will go on refusing things in four words because the four words are on a form, and a form is cheap, and a sentence costs money and can be quoted against the person who wrote it, and the whole of this office's defence of itself is that it acts on reach and not on particulars and it will not be moved off that, and it is right to be proud of that, and it is also the reason that a woman in a court off Bell Row can go to a counter four times a year and be told *no district office competent* and never once be told what a competent office would have been.

The sheet is on the table. The answer is on the table and it is correct and it grants nothing and it will be the answer in October. Next quarter there will be a sheet again, and there will be a queue, and there will be a woman in it who has never heard of any of this and who will read a heading and go away.

The forty-one are not stopped. The forty-one are not even slowed. Forty-one refusals in this quarter was forty-one refusals and there will be some in the next one, and the practice is a practice, and a practice does not have a face, and what a practice has is a form and a place in a schedule of duties and a name in that schedule, and the name is a person who has never been anybody's enemy and is not going to be anybody's villain and is going to go on being extremely good at a job that costs this city forty-one conversations a quarter.

And the argument he had made at the side of the table at eleven o'clock on the Saturday morning — the argument about the face of a duty and the face of a refusal, the ninety words, the best four minutes of public speaking of his life — was correct, and it was entirely correct, and he stood in a lane in the rain and understood that **visibility is not remedy**, and that he had spent six weeks learning the second of those two words the hard way, and that the first one is a thing a queue in a waiting hall can do in a morning, and that the whole of the difference between the two is about four hundred pounds a year for a pump and one hundred and eighty-one people off a bank and eleven days in the fourth quarter of the Concord Year 471, and that none of those numbers is going to be moved by anything he can say at the side of a table.
