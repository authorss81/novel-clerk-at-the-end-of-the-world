# Chapter 59 — Three Holds

The application is one sheet and it took him four minutes to write at the end of the second desk on the Wednesday morning of the second day of the Concord Year 488, and it asks for six things and he can fill in five of them.

It is headed **APPLICATION TO BE EXAMINED AS A CONTINUANCE EXAMINER OF THE COUNT** and it is item eleven of a schedule in the district office's Schedule of Instruments in Force, and the sixth thing it asks for is *the settlement in which the applicant is a person*, and a settlement means a place with premises of record, and Docketford district is a district and not a settlement. He looked at that line for about two minutes and then wrote **Docketford district** on it, and underlined it, and wrote under the underline: *I am a person of this district. I am not a person of any settlement in it. I understand that this may be refused.*

It was not refused. It came back on the Wednesday afternoon with a day and an hour on it, and the day was the third and the hour was ten, and the room was the long room at the Glass Stair, which is the room the ward's petition was heard in in the last month of the last year, and which has high windows, and in which Owen Rusk had read a list of sixteen names aloud and felt nothing about fifteen of them.

---

The receipt was gone and he found that out on the Wednesday morning at about a quarter to eleven, and he found it out by accident, because his hand went to the corner of the tray where he had put it under a weight on the seventy-second day of the last month and the weight was still there and the corner was not.

He stood at the end of the second desk for about a minute and a half.

Then he went and got the register, which has a column headed *disposed* and a column headed *authority* and a column headed *initials* and two hundred and six entries in it for the last year, and the disposal of expired instruments is a Tuesday and a Friday, and on the ninety-first day of the Concord Year 487 — which was a Monday, and which he knew perfectly well had been a Monday, because he had been standing in a field at twenty past six that morning reading a sheet of paper that said *the quota for the district is eleven* — there is one entry that is not a Tuesday or a Friday, and it is this:

*487-GS-19. Papers of a petition concluded. Certification of the sixty-third expired; no instrument in the matter in force. Disposed. Item nine.*

Four columns were used and there is no reason column, because there is not one. There is a line, a day, a rule and a set of initials, and the initials were not Owen Rusk's, and the entry is completely correct, and item nine says that on the expiry of the period stated on the sheet a sheet is disposed of by the standing rule, and the standing rule does not require a person to be told.

The request for a receipt was made on the twentieth day of the month in answer to a promise, and no answer had ever been made to the request, and the period of an unanswered paper is fourteen days from the last entry, and the last entry on it was the day it was written. **So the period ran out on the thirty-fourth day of the month. It stood on that spike from the twentieth day of the month before until the seventy-second, when Owen Rusk took it off it, carried it up a flight of stairs and put it under a weight on a tray of unfiled correspondence, which is not what that tray is for; and from the seventy-second to the ninety-first it stood on that tray; and on the ninety-first it was disposed of, in a room he was not in, by a person whose initials are not his; and the standing rule disposes of the papers of a matter concluded wherever the papers are put, and the rule does not require a person to be told, and it does not require a person to have taken the sheet off a spike.**

And he had got the arithmetic wrong at the time. He had stood at that spike on the Wednesday of the seventy-second day and told himself in ninety seconds that the thing was in its fourteenth day, and it was in its fifty-second, because he had counted the fourteen from the day the retention notice went up instead of from the last entry on the sheet. A notice does not start a clock. A notice reports one. There is no form in this building on which a man may record having been wrong about that, and there is a man in this building who was wrong about it, and he is the officer of the record of the district returns.

The one line he wrote in his own register about the act is the whole of the record of it, and it is in a book that leaves the building and is kept by a man who writes better forms than he does, and nothing came of it.

He did not tell anybody for six days, and the six days were not a decision; they were the fact that there was nobody to tell until the ward hall opened on the Sunday, and by then he had a qualification and three named claimants and a correction to read out, and telling them would have been a thing he had done for himself. He went back to the tray at about eleven and did forty sheets and was on time at the end of the day for the second time in a week and did not notice that either.

---

The count room at the Glass Stair is on the ground floor, at the back, and it is open from the first working hour to the last, and anybody may go into it, and it is the only room in this building that has a public stand in it.

The stand is a deal table with a rail round three sides and a shelf at about the height of a man's hip, and on the shelf there is a row of books face up with their spines out and a card in front of each one giving the title, the year of the last revision, and the rule under which it may be taken off the stand and read in the room and not taken out of the room.

He had walked past that room four times a week for eight years and had not gone into it once, and on the Sunday at the slip at Nether Sill, in front of nine people, as the fourth of four answers, a woman with a slate had told him that there was a copy of a book in it that was four hundred years out of date in about nine places.

**The schedule of premises of record for this district.** Bound, about four inches, blue cloth, and the card said *last revised, the Concord Year 402*, and Owen Rusk took it off the stand at about half past twelve on the Wednesday of the second day and read it standing up in a public room with three other people reading beside him.

It is a book of four columns. Number. Place. Street or lane. Date of entry. Nothing else in it. Nine hundred and forty rows in Docketford district, and it is the only description of the buildings of this town that has ever been kept by anybody, and it is the whole of what a *premise* is.

He looked for the mill road at Nether Sill, because that is what he had come in for, and it took him four minutes, and it is on a page near the back because it was entered early, and there it is, in the ordinary hand of a clerk of the Concord Year 402, in a column headed *street or lane*, and the entry reads:

***The Mill Road, Nether Sill. Nine premises. Two dwelling-houses and a shop, a barn, and five dwelling-houses.***

Nine premises. A street of record, in a book that has stood on a public stand in this city for eighty-six years, describing two dwelling-houses and a shop, a barn, and five dwelling-houses, which is nine, and every one of them a dwelling with a door and a roof, on a road the county itself has called a street since before anybody now living was born.

He read it three times, and then sat down on the rail of the stand, which he was not supposed to do, and worked out what it meant and what it did not mean, in the order in which a man who reads forms for a living works things out.

**What it meant.** The eight households living in two rooms at the top of a working mill are not homeless and are not unrecorded and are not a mystery. Their houses are nine rows in a book in this city with a street against them. The nine houses on that road have roofs and doors and are standing, and they are full of four feet of silt, and a house full of silt is still a house, and **the mill is a solution that eight families chose because the county has spent a hundred and forty years saying that a room in a building is not a dwelling, and a room is not a dwelling but a house with a number in a book is a dwelling, and the two are the same building.**

**What it did not mean.** A row in a book on a public stand is not an act. The nine rows are in the *copy*. The book the district office keeps is a different book and has been revised four times since and Owen Rusk had never once been in the room where it is, and the copy on the stand is a copy and was out of date in about nine places when Kade named it on a Sunday at a slip, and one of the nine places was this. Somewhere between the copy and the current book, somebody had struck those nine rows, or had never carried them forward, or had carried them forward into a column and found the column wanted a *street or lane* and the street of record had a different name that year.

He wrote one line in his own register, standing up, and it is the whole of what he wrote and he has never added to it:

*The Mill Road, Nether Sill, is a street of record in the public copy with nine premises against it. The nine are not in the county's current figure for the settlement. The difference between the copy and the current book is nine premises at one bend and I do not know yet in which book the nine went. If they went in a district office, somebody in a district office did it. If they did not, nobody did, and the figure was never nine, and the counting clerk of the Concord Year 486 was writing down a column that was already out of date when he wrote* I would rather it failed on nothing.

And then he put the book back on the stand with the spine out and the card in front of it and wrote nothing further, and it was four minutes past one on the Wednesday of the second day, and the correction for Nether Sill is dated the twelfth, and a copy of a book that has not been revised since the Concord Year 402 cannot unmake a count correction made by the Provincial Counting Office and attested by a warden, and he knew that when he read the row, and he read it anyway, on the Wednesday, three days after the day he spent arguing about a form instead of opening a book, and that was the shape of the mistake and he had it whole in his hands in a public room on his lunch.

---

The examining officer was a woman of about fifty from Concord Hall with no district in the matter and a slate of her own and no coat, and she read Owen Rusk's application for about ninety seconds and then put it face down and did not appear to think about it again.

"State the settlement," she said.

"There is no settlement. The application is by a person of Docketford district and the matter to be examined is the reconciliation of the settlement of Nether Sill, held at the mill on the ninetieth day of the month before, of which the figure is four."

"The figure is four of what."

"Four premises of record produced, of nine premises of record recorded at the reconciliation of the Concord Year 486, being three of them the sluice house, the mill house and the shop, and one the barn, unoccupied since the Concord Year 480 and not struck, and the remaining five having come down or been combined."

"That is correct and you did not have to think about it, which is what I am here for." She turned the slate round. "Now the standing question, and I am going to ask it the way I ask it, and I do not vary it, and I have asked it four hundred times."

"**State the figure, state what the figure is a figure of, and state who put it there.**"

"And the third part?"

"And the third part is not in the standing question," said the examining officer. "The third part is yours, and you put it in before I asked you, and I am going to notice that, and I am going to make you say the rest of it, and then I am going to answer it, and I am telling you now that the answer is going to be a building you work in."

"**The figure is four. It is a figure of premises of record produced. It was put there by the District Counting Office of Docketford on a return of premises of record for the settlement made in the Concord Year 486, from a schedule of premises of record, which is kept in this building for the Water Office of this district, and it was carried forward into the count of the Concord Year 487, which was suspended in the month of rain and not taken, and from the suspended count into the schedule of settlements to be reconciled for the season, which was printed for notice on the twenty-eighth day of the month before last.**"

"And who filled in the return of the Concord Year 486?"

"The settlement did. Nine rows in four hands, and a margin note under them in a counting clerk's hand at a sub-table, and the office has never seen the return, because a return goes to the office that holds it and the office that holds a settlement's return is the district office, and the district office of Docketford is on the second floor of the building I work in, and the counting clerk is at the second counter of the ground floor of the building I work in."

The examining officer wrote for the better part of a minute.

"That is a complete and correct answer and I am going to write it in your words," she said, "and I am going to write one sentence under it in mine, and the sentence is that you are the officer of the record of the district returns of the district that holds the return, and that you are therefore the answer to your own third question, and that I am obliged to record that, and that I would rather record it on Wednesday than in a room in the eighth month from a person who has worked it out at about eleven o'clock at night in a tent."

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"Now the limits, and I am going to give them to you in full, and I am going to give them to you on the first morning because a man who finds them out in the ninth week thinks somebody has cheated him."

She read them off the slate.

"A Continuance Examiner may examine a reconciliation. An examiner may challenge the arithmetic, the procedure, and the currency of any paper the reconciliation rests upon. An examiner may enter a challenge and be heard, and a challenge that is good is sustained. An examiner holds up to three active holds, each of which is a real responsibility toward a named claimant, and a hold is an answerability and not a status: it keeps one matter open and answerable and gives one person a name to go to, and it preserves no water and no figure and no suspension and nothing at all except the fact that a person in this district has been asked a question and has not been answered. **An examiner may not examine the schedule.** An examiner may not examine a choice of places. The reason column is a figure and an examiner may not put a word in it.

"And I am going to say the last thing in my own words, and I would like you to write it down, and then you will not have to be told it again. **You will spend your whole term finding errors in arithmetic, and every one of them will be about a figure, and not one of them will be about a person's water.** That is not a criticism of you. It is the shape of the work, and I have been in it eleven years, and the men who last did it are the only ones in this building who have ever been right about anything and are dead."

---

The supervised case was **Gallants Reach** and the error was the one he had found on the board on the Monday night, and he had found it by accident, because he reads columns and there are eleven rows of them on a board behind the tanneries.

The count sheet for Gallants Reach is the reconciliation of the Concord Year 479, and on Monday night he had read 479 in the second column of the published schedule and gone on to the next row, which is what a man who reads columns does.

The sheet itself is not a closed sheet. It was made out in the Concord Year 479, used for the Count of 479, and never closed, because the Count of 480 was suspended and not taken and the sheet was carried into the book for 481 with the year column still saying 479; the count of 481 was taken off the carried sheet, and every season since has been taken off the carried sheet, and the figure in the published schedule is a figure about the doors of a settlement in the Concord Year 479.

He did not write down what he felt about it, which was that he had found the mistake that puts a hamlet in front of a form while looking for the mistake that would have let a woman out of one. What he wrote down, in the challenge, in the proper form, on the Thursday of the third day, was this: *That the count sheet of Gallants Reach be a sheet of the Concord Year 479 carried forward into the Count of 481 without reconciliation and carried forward in every season since, and that the figure of premises of record given against the settlement in the schedule of settlements to be reconciled is a figure of nine years standing, and that the settlement be returned to the schedule for reconciliation in this season.*

It is a real error and it is a documented one and it is the kind of thing this office exists to find, and the counting clerk at the second counter stood over it for about ten minutes and then said, "Yes," and did it, and it was heard the same morning by the examining officer, who had examined the applicant three hours earlier and has no district in the matter, and who sustained it.

And the consequence was that **Gallants Reach came off the printed schedule and back onto it**, and the printed schedule is the schedule of settlements to be reconciled, and being on it means a visit, a stone, a room, fourteen days and a reason in a box, and Gallants Reach has six premises of record and eleven households, and the woman who told Owen Rusk to go and look at a book on a public stand was standing in front of him at the time.

He sat on the rail with the challenge in his hand and worked out the whole of it in about ninety seconds, on the Thursday afternoon of the third day of the Concord Year 488: it was a good piece of work, it was his first, and the result of it was that a hamlet of eleven households was going to be put in front of a form in the second week of the season, and the good work had been found by a man who reads columns, and **no person's water had changed by a single gallon anywhere in Docketford district.**

He wrote that in the review record himself, in his own hand, in the box the form gives you for what the challenge did: *No change in any draw, in any fund, or in the standing of any household. The settlement is returned to the schedule for reconciliation. That is the whole of it.*

And then, in the same hand, in the box below it which asks whether the applicant has any interest in the matter: *Yes.*

The examining officer read both lines and looked at him over the slate for a while, and then said:

"I am going to tell you what the office will say about that record so that you have heard it from the person who writes it. The office will say the record is thin. The office will say that a challenge is one challenge and that a single carried sheet in one district is not a course. The office will say, correctly, that you have discharged a supervised case and that a discharged case is not a qualification of standing. The office will be right about all three and I am going to write the word *thin* into the third box myself, because I would rather this building heard it from the file than from you."

"That box is not a question about the schedule," she said. "It is a question about the applicant, and nobody fills it in, and nobody has ever filled it in, and I have read four hundred of them and I have seen it filled in twice. Fill it in."

"It puts me on a file about myself for the term."

"Yes," said the examining officer. "And it is true, and the term is eleven years, and in the eleventh year of an error case you will want somebody to be able to read that you said so on the first morning. I am not asking you because it helps me. I am asking you because the alternative is that the only honest statement in the file is the one you keep in a book that leaves the building."

---

**He was examined on the third day and qualified on the fourth, and the qualification is on a card, and the card is not the card he had for eight weeks.**

The old card was a posting. It named a tray and a case, it did not travel with him off the tray, and it had four lines on it that he was forbidden to sign, and the four words were *a continuation, a certification, a suspension, a hold*, and Owen Rusk had carried that card in a coat pocket for eight weeks and had been proud of it in a way he had not examined.

The new card is cream and it is a different size and it is made out in a hand that is not the counting clerk's and not Kade's and not the examining officer's, and it is signed on the seal stair at about eleven o'clock on the fourth day of the month in front of a room of nine people, and it says:

**OWEN RUSK. CONTINUANCE EXAMINER OF THE COUNT, DOCKETFORD DISTRICT. Entered the fourth day of the Concord Year 488. May examine a reconciliation. May enter a challenge and be heard. Holds up to three, each a real responsibility toward a named claimant. A hold is an answerability and preserves nothing. A hold may be given up by the holder at any time and may be withdrawn by the office at any time. A hold may not be transferred to any other officer without the written agreement of the claimant.**

The last line is the only one in the whole of the Registry's instruments that Owen Rusk has ever read which says *the written agreement of the claimant*, and he stood at the bottom of the seal stair and read it four times, and it is the fifth part of a requirements sheet that a ward hall in Fetter Lane has been filling in by hand since the third week of the month of rain, and it is not the same thing and it is not a cure, and it took eleven weeks and a flood to get a card in this city with a sentence like that on it, and the sentence is about a person who can hold a document, and not about a girl of twelve who cannot be asked.

The first of the three holds was filled in about a minute later, on the same form, in the same hand, and nobody chose it and nobody planned it and it had been true since the eighth week of the month of rain.

**Claimant one. Ivy Sarr, of two rooms in Fetter Lane, of this district, a person who cannot be asked. Matter: the care of the claimant, and the standing of that care in the Count of the Concord Year 488.**

The instrument machinery would not let it be otherwise. A named duty and a hold are the same shape and the register will not carry a person twice under two heads, and the count's return of premises for the district has her name in the fourth column entered as **none** in capitals, in a ward's hand, in a bag from a hall door, and the office received it, and the office returns nothing, and the office is not required to return anything, and the ward has been carrying six of them since the third week of the last rain season at four in the morning and is going to carry them at four in the morning through this season and the next.

**It is not a cure.** He has written that on the form in his own hand in the box for the claimant's own words, and what he wrote is *She is not known to this office. No person in her household is to be asked to be named.* And Wenna Sarr read the four lines of the reply he sent up the hill on the Saturday morning, put them down on the table in front of him, and said, "You have made my aunt a line in a form." 

"I have made your aunt a line in a form."

"Say the rest of it."

"I do not know how to make her anything else and I have not pretended that I can. The line is a line and not a door. I have got three of them, and two of the other two are not in this ward, and I am going to be away on one of the days this week, and the ward is going to be doing the pump at four in the morning without me in it. That is what I have."

**Claimant two. Mavis Rudge, of Witherow, a person of this district who can be asked. Matter: a request by the claimant for a review of the reconciliation of Witherow.**

She came into the building on the Friday of the fourth day, on her own feet, up the hill from the flat above the weirs, and she was about fifty-four and she had a return of premises of her own in a bag, made out in three hands, and she wanted Owen Rusk to examine it and challenge the reconciliation and get the settlement put back on the schedule and get the figure of three struck out of the published column.

He read her return for about ten minutes and then told her the truth, which took about ninety seconds, and the truth was this.

"I can enter your request. I cannot get you a review that succeeds. A review has to show a current local act performed by the settlement, and your settlement's last reconciliation is nine years old and it shows three premises and eleven households, and there is no act in any column at Witherow that the settlement itself performs and records, and if I enter the request it will be heard by a woman from Concord Hall in about six days and she will find that Witherow cannot produce the thing the clause wants, and the file will say that the reconciliation is sustained, and the figure of three will stand."

"Then I do not want it," said Mavis Rudge.

"You said you wanted a review."

"I wanted a review of a count. I did not say I wanted to win it." She put her hand flat on the return, which is a thing people do and which has never once meant comfort. "Nobody in this county has ever written down that Witherow was put on a list of eleven without anybody coming. That is the thing I am asking for. I have lived here thirty-one years and my house is one of three doors that this county thinks exist, and there are eleven of us on the mud flats and five of those eleven are not on anything at all, and one of the five is my sister, and **she is not on this list and she is not on any list, and that is the reason I have come to you and not the reason you would think.** I want a line in a file with my name at the top of it that says I asked and I was answered and the answer was no. That is all. I have thirty-one years of nobody asking me anything and I would like one document in which somebody asked me and the answer was no."

Owen Rusk entered the request at about two o'clock on the Friday of the fourth day, in the proper form, in his own name and hers, both names on it, and the reading clerk read the ground of it back to both of them, and read the second name as well, which is the whole of what a name on a page is to a person who has not had one before.

It will be heard before an officer of the count having no district in the matter in the second week of the season, and the file will say *review requested, heard, reconciliation sustained, no current local act shown*, and the request and the ground of it and both names will be on the published sheet, and it will be the first document in the history of Witherow in which a person of Witherow is named as having asked this county for anything.

She thanked him at the door of the count room on the ground floor, in about nine words, and she meant every one of them, and they were for the entering and not for the finding, and she went down the hill with the bag. Owen Rusk stood at a counter and worked out that he did not know whether a woman from Concord Hall would find an act at Witherow or would not, and that Mavis Rudge did know, and that she had known it before she came up the hill, and that it was the worst thing that had happened to him since the month of rain.

**Claimant three. Ned Callow, of Quill Bank, a person of this district who can be asked. Matter: a request by the claimant that an officer of the Registry be present at the reconciliation of Quill Bank.**

Nine premises of record. Eleven households. A drainage district on the flat above the weirs with a good return and an inconvenient draw, and the draw is the reason the settlement is on the list, and the man has asked for nothing in thirty years and asked for this in the first week of the counting season, and what he asked for is a person in the room.

"You cannot," said Owen Rusk.

"No," said Ned Callow. "I did think about it."

"I know exactly why you asked, because I was asked to do it myself on Friday night by the warden, and I gave her the reason, and it is a good one, and I have given it to her in about four sentences."

"Then give it to me."

"**Because I am the officer of the record of the district returns and if I stand in a room at Quill Bank then whatever that room says about itself becomes a document of this office, and Quill Bank will not be able to say what it thinks for nine years without a man from Docketford in it who can be cross-examined about it afterwards.**"

Ned Callow took that in about as slowly as Owen Rusk had taken Kade's version of the same thing, and then he said:

"You have been in this city thirty-two years. And the first thing anybody from the Registry has ever offered me is a reason why they cannot come."

"Yes," said Owen Rusk.

"Right," said Ned Callow, and went out, and was not angry.

Owen Rusk stood at a counter on the ground floor of the River Desk and watched a man be pleased about a sentence that was, on Friday night, the best answer anybody in the Registry had given anybody, and understood at about the fourth minute that **he had spent a week being proud of a principle and had not once asked whether it was of any use to anybody, and that a principle which is only ever a reason somebody is not in a room is not a principle, it is a form, and he knows what a form is, and he has been on the wrong side of one for eleven weeks.**

He went up the hill that night and told Ada Rusk the whole of it in the shed in about four minutes, including the part about the fourth minute, and Ada Rusk listened to all of it and then said:

"Then the next time somebody in this building asks you a question, you are going to have to decide first which kind of question it is, and you have never once done that in your life. You are a man who decides what kind of question it is afterwards, and every person who has ever been let down by this office has been let down by that, and you have been one of them since the second week of the month of rain and you have been extremely good at it."

---

Junia Pell was offered the posting on the count's district correspondence on the morning of the fifth day, which is a Saturday, and it is a step up, and it is the fourth name on a roll of nineteen that has had no vacancy in eleven weeks, and it came from the same countersignature desk that has been asking her for a separation of interest in writing for eleven weeks.

It is the district's face at the count, and it would have made her a woman who could answer a form about a settlement instead of a woman who has to ask somebody else whether a form is right.

"No," said Junia Pell, in the corridor, in one sentence, without stopping, and the sentence was: *I would be the district's face for a count I have not looked at*, and she went on down the corridor, and she did not explain it, and she was not asked.

She said one other thing, at the top of the stair, on her way out, and she said it to Owen Rusk and not to anybody else and she said it in the voice she uses for a form:

"You have written *yes* in a box about yourself. I have known four hundred applicants and I have seen that box filled in twice, and I took the second of them, and he is on the second floor and he is dead. **Do not tell anybody in the ward about it.** Not because it is against them. Because a person who is on a file about himself in Docketford is a person who is expensive to befriend, and that ward has four people in it who are already on files about themselves, and one of them is a woman of twenty-four who filed an objection in her own name and made herself unwithdrawable on purpose, and she is the last person in that district who needs any of them to cost her anything more."

"I was not going to tell them."

"You were going to tell Ada Rusk on Friday night, in a shed, in four minutes, and you have just told me you did, and I am going to pretend for about four days that I do not know it," said Junia Pell, "and then I am going to want to know whether you told her, and you are going to have to decide now what you are going to say, and I am telling you this on a Saturday so that you have got the weekend to decide it in."

---

The correction came up on the Sunday afternoon of the sixth day, on the tray, in a bundle with two other things, from the counting clerk at the second counter, and it is prepared and dated and not yet attested, and the date on it is **the twelfth**, which is a Saturday, which is six days away.

It has a schedule of premises attached to it, and the schedule is one page, and it is the four premises of Nether Sill with the door numbers and the water instrument at each and the date of the last reconciliation against each, and the schedule is correct, and the schedule is the size of a tablecloth.

And at the foot of the first page there is a box the size of a hand with a line above it in the printing shop's hand that says **REASON (a figure)**, and there is a figure in it, and the figure is **four**.

There is a bundle-slip clipped to the corner of the correction and it is in the counting clerk's hand and it says, in full:

*To the Continuance Examiner for the district. This correction is examined as to arithmetic and procedure and may be read out. The correction is correct. The appeal lies to the Glass Stair and will take a season. It will be attested on the twelfth. — Counting clerk, Docketford*

Owen Rusk stood at the end of the second desk on the Sunday afternoon of the sixth day of the Concord Year 488, with a card in his pocket that he had been given on the Friday and a correction on the tray that he had read four times, and worked out the whole of the season in about a minute and a half, and it came out as follows.

He could not stop it. The appeal lay to the Glass Stair and would take a season and a season is the thing that is in front of him.

He could not restore it. The eleven households kept the cut and the mill and the bend, and none of those is in a column, and a column is what the water comes out of.

He could not even be angry about it properly, because the reason in the box was a figure and the figure was correct, and the six weeks he had given this building had gone on arguing about a form when the thing that would have helped was a book on a public stand four minutes away, which he opened on the Wednesday of the second day, three days after the stone went down, and which could not have stopped it either, because it is a copy. Both of those things were true at the same time and he did not find a way to hold them in his head at the same time until about the Saturday night.

What he could do was read it out.

And a woman with a slate had said to him on the Saturday of the eighty-ninth day, at a slip, in the rain, to a man who had not asked her anything: *It is never the argument. It is always the thing you did not do on the Saturday.*

So: a reading, in public, at the board, on the day it is attested, by the only person in this city who can do it, who has been in this city his whole life.
